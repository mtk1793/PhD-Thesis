# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background on Power System Challenges in the Renewable Energy Era

The global electricity sector is undergoing a structural transition whose pace has few precedents in the history of power engineering. According to the World Energy Outlook 2023, almost 4800 GW of renewable capacity is expected to be added worldwide between 2023 and 2028, and installed renewable capacity in 2026 is projected to be roughly 60% higher than in 2020 [1]. This growth is driven by climate policy, by the falling cost of photovoltaic (PV) modules and wind turbines, and by public support for decarbonisation. The consequence for system operators is that the resource mix they must balance changes fundamentally within a single planning decade.

The scale of the shift can be quantified from the capacity statistics published by the International Renewable Energy Agency, as shown in Figure 1.1 [2]. Global installed solar capacity grew from approximately 40 GW in 2010 to 1412 GW in 2023, wind capacity grew from 178 GW to 743 GW over the same period, and hydropower expanded more modestly from 949 GW to 1303 GW. Solar and wind, the two most variable technologies, therefore account for the overwhelming majority of the capacity added since 2010. The figure makes clear that the growth is not incremental; it is exponential in character for solar and sustained and steep for wind.

![Figure 1.1: Global installed renewable energy capacity growth by technology, 2010–2023.](figures/fig_renewable_growth.png)

This changing resource mix alters the physics of the grid as well as its statistics. Wind and solar output is weather-driven and varies on timescales from seconds to seasons; the German load and generation record used throughout this thesis, drawn from the Open Power System Data (OPSD) platform, contains three-hour national wind and solar ramps exceeding 28 GW in magnitude [42]. At the same time, inverter-coupled generation displaces synchronous machines and thereby reduces the rotating inertia available to arrest frequency excursions, a challenge examined in detail in the low-inertia systems literature [3]. Distribution networks that were designed for unidirectional flows from transmission to consumption now host rooftop PV, storage, and electric vehicle (EV) charging that can reverse flow directions within minutes. Each of these effects would be manageable alone; their coincidence is what strains conventional operating practice.

The operating environment is also more complex institutionally and electronically. Millions of small devices with mixed ownership participate in the same physical system as a few hundred large generators, so coordination problems grow combinatorially. The communication links, intelligent electronic devices, and remote access paths that enable this coordination enlarge the attack surface of the grid, and cyber-physical security has consequently become a first-order design consideration rather than an afterthought [75]. Finally, market rules and regulatory frameworks frequently lag behind the technology, leaving tariffs, interconnection standards, and ancillary-service definitions poorly matched to the resources actually being connected. Figure 1.2 contrasts the traditional, centralised and unidirectional system with the modern, distributed and bidirectional one, and Table 1.1 summarises the six challenges that follow from this contrast.

![Figure 1.2: Traditional versus modern power system control challenges.](figures/fig_trad_vs_modern.png)

**Table 1.1: Key challenges in modern power systems with high DER penetration.**

| Challenge | Description | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Intermittency and variability | Weather-driven output of wind and solar plants fluctuates on timescales from seconds to seasons | Larger reserves, curtailment risk, heavier dependence on forecasting |
| Reduced system inertia | Inverter-coupled generation displaces synchronous machines and their kinetic energy | Faster frequency excursions and narrower stability margins [3] |
| Bidirectional power flows | Distributed generation, storage, and EV charging reverse historical flow directions | Voltage rise, protection miscoordination, and congestion at weak nodes |
| Growing complexity | Many heterogeneous devices with mixed ownership act within one physical system | Combinatorial coordination burden and harder state estimation |
| Cybersecurity exposure | Wider attack surface created by communication links and smart devices | False data injection and other attacks with physical consequences [75] |
| Market and regulatory friction | Tariffs, standards, and service definitions lag behind deployed technology | Investment uncertainty and delayed adoption of flexibility |

The six challenges in Table 1.1 are coupled, which is precisely what makes them difficult. A curtailment decision interacts with inertia, with locational congestion, and with the economic viability of the plants involved, so no single-device or single-layer remedy suffices. The control question raised by this coupling, and the one this thesis pursues, is how heterogeneous fast and slow resources can be coordinated coherently in real time.

## 1.2 Evolution of FACTS Devices

Flexible Alternating Current Transmission System (FACTS) devices are the classical answer of power electronics to the problem of controlling an alternating current network whose flows are determined by impedances rather than by dispatch intent. Research programmes in the 1980s, notably at the Electric Power Research Institute, demonstrated that thyristor-based controllers could modulate reactive power and line reactance far faster than mechanically switched equipment. Hingorani and Gyugyi consolidated these ideas into the FACTS concept and its canonical device families, giving the field both its vocabulary and its theoretical foundation [4]. Their central insight was that controllability, not merely capacity, is the scarce resource in an alternating current network.

Subsequent monographs turned the concept into practical modelling and control methodology. Acha et al. developed steady-state and dynamic models of the main FACTS devices suitable for incorporation into power flow and stability programs [5], while Zhang, Rehtanz, and Pal treated FACTS modelling and control in an integrated framework that anticipates today's grid-code requirements [6]. The thyristor-controlled series capacitor (TCSC) received particular attention because series compensation interacts directly with electromechanical oscillations; Del Rosso, Cañizares, and Doña showed how TCSC controller design must trade damping improvement against stability limits of the compensation loop [7]. In its simplest steady-state representation, a TCSC changes the effective reactance of a line according to Equation (1.1):

```eq
%%1.1
X_{eff} = (1 - k)\, X_{line}, \quad 0 \leq k \leq 0.7
```

where $X_{line}$ is the uncompensated line reactance and k is the compensation degree; in the benchmark used in this thesis the operational setpoint range is restricted to k ∈ [0.3, 0.7]. Devices of this kind fall into three families, namely shunt compensators such as the static var compensator (SVC) and the static synchronous compensator (STATCOM), series devices such as the TCSC and the static synchronous series compensator, and combined devices such as the unified power flow controller (UPFC), which couples a shunt and a series element through a common direct current link. The benchmark network studied here employs one device of each class, namely an SVC at bus 14, a STATCOM at bus 39, a TCSC on the line between buses 16 and 17, and a UPFC at bus 26, so that the coordinated control question is posed across all three families.

FACTS devices retain their relevance precisely because the renewable transition increases the need for controllability [50]. Power-electronic interfaces dominate the new resource mix, and the same technology base that connects wind turbines and solar farms can steer flows and support voltage where the network was never designed to carry them [50]. It appears that the marginal value of a FACTS device rises with renewable penetration, because the devices supply the fast, dispatchable reactive and flow control that variable resources do not provide themselves. Yet in most deployed systems FACTS controllers still act on local measurements with local objectives, which motivates the coordination questions taken up in Section 1.3 and, more broadly, in the problem statement of Section 1.5.

## 1.3 Integration of EVs into Smart Grids (V2G/G2V)

The electrification of transport adds a second class of controllable, power-electronic assets to the grid. In its default grid-to-vehicle (G2V) mode, EV charging is a flexible but sizeable load, and uncontrolled evening charging tends to coincide with the residential peak; studies of residential distribution networks show that naive charging can push feeder voltages and transformer loadings beyond their limits [14, 15]. Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) operation reverses this relationship by allowing the fleet to discharge when the system needs energy or reactive support. Kempton and Tomić calculated the aggregate power and revenue potential of V2G fleets and identified frequency regulation and spinning reserve as the services for which the concept is most naturally suited [8], with a companion paper addressing the implementation path from stabilising the grid to supporting large-scale renewable energy [9].

A substantial literature has since refined how fleets should be dispatched. Sortomme and El-Sharkawi formulated optimal charging strategies for unidirectional V2G and showed that scheduling with energy-price and grid objectives can reduce cost and losses simultaneously [10]. Hu, Morais, Sousa, and Lind reviewed fleet management across services, optimisation, and control, cataloguing the auction-, linear-, and dynamic-programming approaches then available [11]. On the hardware side, Yilmaz and Krein reviewed charger topologies, charging power levels, and infrastructure requirements, distinguishing single-stage and two-stage converters and the conditions under which bidirectional power flow is economic [12]. The concept family extends beyond the bulk grid: vehicle-to-home (V2H) and vehicle-to-everything (V2X) arrangements let the battery serve the household or the local community, with the opportunities and challenges of each mode examined by Liu, Chau, Wu, and Gao [13].

This thesis models EV participation at the level of aggregated fleet stations, each behaving as a single controllable storage unit with power and energy limits. Three such stations are attached to the IEEE 39-bus benchmark at buses 3, 8, and 15, each rated at 50 MW with a 100 MWh energy reservoir, a one-way efficiency of η = 0.92, and a state-of-charge (SoC) window constrained to [0.2, 0.9]. The station abstraction corresponds to an aggregated fleet rather than to individual vehicles, which is the level at which an aggregator or distribution system operator would contract. Charging and discharging update the stored energy according to Equation (1.2):

```eq
%%1.2
\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SoC}_t + \frac{\eta P_t \Delta t}{E_{cap}} \;\; (P_t \geq 0), \quad \mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SoC}_t + \frac{P_t \Delta t}{\eta E_{cap}} \;\; (P_t < 0)
```

where $P_t$ is the dispatched station power, positive for charging, $\Delta t$ the control interval, and $E_{cap}$ the energy capacity; discharging is deliberately penalised by the efficiency factor because round-trip losses are charged to the fleet. Dispatch commands are clamped to the station power rating, and the SoC is held inside its window by forbidding charge at the upper bound and discharge at the lower bound. These constraints matter for control design because they make the action space non-trivial: a candidate dispatch that looks attractive in the power-flow sense may be infeasible for the battery, and the coordinating controller must respect such limits at every step.

## 1.4 Research Motivation

The technical case for this research rests on the observation that distributed energy resources (DERs) are growing faster than the tools available to coordinate them. Industry projections cited in the project literature suggest that DER capacity of the type considered here may grow from roughly 132.4 GW to 528.4 GW by 2026, a compound annual growth rate of about 16.7%, although such projections should be read as indications of direction rather than as forecasts. The smart grid literature has long argued that monitoring, communication, and control must advance together for this growth to be absorbed safely [48, 49]. What has changed in the last decade is that the monitoring and communication layers matured, through phasor measurement units and open datasets, while the control layer has lagged.

Three imperatives follow. The technical imperative is to keep voltage, loading, and frequency within limits as inertia falls and flows reverse, because the cost of failure is now systemic rather than local. The economic imperative is to reduce losses and curtailment and to raise the utilisation of assets that are already paid for; the January 2019 uncontrolled baseline of the benchmark used in this thesis records energy costs of 171.6 MEUR for a single month, so even fractional improvements translate into large absolute sums. The environmental imperative is that every megawatt-hour of wind or solar curtailed is replaced, in practice, by fossil generation, so control quality is coupled directly to decarbonisation outcomes. Resilience ties the three together, since a system that cannot ride through contingencies without cascading fails all three tests at once.

A further motivation is methodological honesty about data. Control studies conducted on synthetic load profiles can be tuned, however unintentionally, to the very profiles they are evaluated on. This thesis therefore builds its benchmark on the OPSD open dataset of real German load, wind, and solar generation [42], disaggregated onto the IEEE 39-bus network, so that the variability the controllers face is observed rather than invented. The dataset is large, public, and versioned, which makes the experiments repeatable by others.

Finally, the maturation of artificial intelligence (AI) makes a new class of controller plausible. Reinforcement learning provides the general grammar for sequential decision-making under uncertainty [22], deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has demonstrated human-level control in domains far more complex than a power network [23]–[26], and recent surveys catalogue a rapidly growing set of power-system applications [27, 28]. Table 1.2 summarises why AI-based control is an attractive complement to conventional schemes across six aspects.

**Table 1.2: Comparison of conventional and AI-based control approaches.**

| Aspect | Conventional control | AI-based control | Advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model dependence | Requires accurate linearised models of the network | Learns from data with physics-informed structure [31] | Tolerates model error and drift |
| Response to uncertainty | Conservative margins fixed at design time | Estimates state and risk online | Uses available headroom |
| Nonlinearity handling | Local linearisation around operating points | Nonlinear function approximation | Tracks wide operating ranges |
| Adaptation | Manual retuning after topology or load changes | Online and continual learning | Lower maintenance burden |
| Multi-timescale coordination | Separate relays, regulators, and optimisers | Layered fast and slow processes | Coherent action across timescales |
| Data usage | Sparse SCADA snapshots | Streaming PMU and smart-meter data | Exploits modern observability |

Table 1.2 should not be read as a claim that AI control is superior in every respect; conventional schemes are transparent, certified, and predictable, which matters for protection. The motivating hypothesis of this thesis is narrower, namely that a brain-inspired AI architecture can combine the speed of reflexes with the foresight of deliberation while remaining verifiable on real hardware.

## 1.5 Problem Statement

The problem addressed by this thesis is the coordinated real-time control of FACTS devices, EV fleets, and DERs in networks with high renewable penetration, under a cognitive framing borrowed from human decision-making. Kahneman's distinction between fast, automatic System 1 thinking and slow, effortful System 2 reasoning provides the organising metaphor [16], and the control literature on reinforcement learning supplies the algorithmic substrate [22]. Analysis of the current state of practice, elaborated in Chapter 2, reveals five specific gaps between what modern grids need and what existing control approaches deliver, as illustrated in Figure 1.3.

![Figure 1.3: Research gap identification framework linking the five control gaps to CAPSM responses.](figures/fig_gap_framework.png)

Gap 1 concerns control architecture for heterogeneous systems. Existing deployments coordinate voltage devices, storage, and flexible loads through separate hierarchies, each designed for one asset class and one timescale, and no principled mechanism decides which layer should act in a given situation. A control architecture for the modern grid must therefore accommodate FACTS devices that act in milliseconds, EV stations that act in minutes, and optimisers that act in hours within one coherent structure.

Gap 2 concerns real-time decision-making under uncertainty. DRL controllers are typically trained offline against a fixed environment model, and the surveys of the field emphasise sample inefficiency and constraint-violation risk as the two barriers to deployment [27, 28]. An operator cannot accept a controller that occasionally violates voltage limits while it explores, yet exploration is precisely what makes learning valuable. Methods that are safe by construction, such as constrained or Lyapunov-based reinforcement learning [29, 30], and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) that embed the power flow equations in training [31], mitigate but do not by themselves close this gap.

Gap 3 concerns coordination between FACTS devices and DERs. As noted in Section 1.2, FACTS controllers conventionally act on local objectives [4, 6], while EV aggregation is usually optimised on slower economic horizons [10, 11]. The two control problems interact through the same network voltages and flows, so independent design leaves benefit on the table and can even cause control fighting. What is missing is a formulation in which shunt, series, and combined devices and the EV stations are scheduled as one system.

Gap 4 concerns fault detection and localisation in resource-rich environments. Classical protection and state estimation were designed for a small number of measurable, slowly varying quantities [75], whereas a modern grid offers abundant PMU, SCADA, and inverter telemetry. The research question is how to fuse these heterogeneous signals so that faults, cyber-attacks, and anomalous but benign behaviour are distinguished quickly and accurately, rather than drowned in alarms.

Gap 5 concerns the divide between theory and practice, and it is the gap that conditions the credibility of all others. The overwhelming majority of AI-based power-system studies stop at offline simulation, where timing, solver behaviour, and input-output latency are idealised, and hardware validation of cognitive controllers remains rare. Controller-Hardware-in-the-Loop (CHIL) testing, in which the controller executes on real hardware while the network runs in real time, is the accepted bridge for protection and control products [51, 52]; this thesis treats such validation not as an afterthought but as a design requirement, and Figure 1.4 shows the architecture that results from taking the five gaps seriously together.

![Figure 1.4: Overview of the proposed AI-driven CAPSM framework.](figures/fig_capsm_architecture.png)

The Cognitive Adaptive Power System Management (CAPSM) framework, introduced in overview in Figure 1.4 and specified fully in Chapter 3, responds to all five gaps simultaneously: a dual-process architecture addresses Gap 1, a candidate-based quantum-inspired deliberation layer with a reflexive neural path addresses Gap 2, an explicit blended action space over FACTS and EV actuators addresses Gap 3, a multi-modal detection layer addresses Gap 4, and a validation pipeline that ends in CHIL testing addresses Gap 5.

## 1.6 Research Objectives

Five objectives translate the gaps into research goals, each carrying a measurable target. The first objective is to design a dual-process control architecture that separates reflexive from deliberative action and arbitrates between them, with the measurable goal of a System 1 reflex path that meets a sub-5 ms timing budget and a 37% reduction in response time to critical events relative to a traditional protection cycle; these figures are design targets from the framework specification. The second objective is to construct a deliberative optimiser that decides under uncertainty without violating operational constraints, with the measurable goals of a System 2 budget below 50 ms, a Metacognitive Arbiter step of 10 ms, and greater than 99.9% constraint compliance through physics-informed training. The third objective is to coordinate FACTS devices and EV fleets in one action space, with the measurable goals of a 32.7% reduction in voltage deviation and a 42% enhancement of stability metrics in extreme events, again as design targets.

The fourth objective is to detect and localise faults in resource-rich environments, with the measurable goals of 95.8% detection and localisation accuracy and sub-10 ms fault detection measured on real-time hardware. The fifth objective is to validate the whole framework on real-time hardware, with the measurable goals of meeting the per-core timing budgets of 5 ms, 50 ms, and 10 ms on a four-core OPAL-RT target and 100% power-flow convergence across the offline benchmark. Table 1.3 collects the objectives, goals, and target metrics, and distinguishes design targets from outcomes measured during the hardware campaign.

**Table 1.3: Research objectives, measurable goals, and target metrics.**

| Objective | Measurable goal | Target metric |
|---|---|---|
| O1 – Dual-process architecture | Reflexive action within protection-grade latency | 37% response-time reduction; System 1 budget below 5 ms (design targets) |
| O2 – Deliberation under uncertainty | Constrained optimisation within dispatch cycles | System 2 below 50 ms; arbiter 10 ms; over 99.9% constraint compliance (design targets) |
| O3 – Coordinated FACTS–DER control | Joint scheduling of shunt, series, combined devices, and EV stations | 32.7% voltage-deviation reduction; 42% stability enhancement (design targets) |
| O4 – Fault detection and localisation | Multi-modal detection on real telemetry | 95.8% detection accuracy (design target); sub-10 ms detection on hardware (HIL-campaign outcome) |

The distinction between design targets and measured outcomes is deliberate and should be maintained throughout the thesis. The targets in Table 1.3 originate in the framework specification and in the HIL campaign design, and they define what success means before the experiments are run. The offline Stage-1 measurements of the benchmark, including the violation counts and per-step inference times reported there, belong to the results analysis of Chapter 11 and are never substituted for the targets here. ## 1.7 Original Contributions

The thesis claims nine original contributions, which map onto the five objectives and onto the chapters that follow. They are stated here in the order in which the thesis develops them.

- *Contribution 1: CAPSM dual-process architecture.* The thesis proposes a control architecture that implements Kahneman's dual-process theory for power networks [16], pairing a reflexive System 1 with a deliberative System 2 under a Metacognitive Arbiter. The architecture assigns each process an explicit timing budget and an explicit action space, so that the two processes compose rather than compete. This responds directly to Gap 1 and is elaborated in Chapter 3.

- *Contribution 2: Quantum-inspired reinforcement learning with Born-rule selection and tunnelling.* System 2 maintains a superposition of 32 candidate actions whose amplitudes are reinforced by observed rewards and sampled through the Born rule, with a tunnelling mechanism that re-initialises a fraction of candidates each step. The mechanism balances exploitation against exploration inside the action box and is analysed formally in Chapter 4.

- *Contribution 3: Metacognitive Arbiter.* A stress-dependent sigmoid weight blends the reflexive and deliberative actions into a single command at every step, with five operating modes that include reflex, planning, override, safe, and blended states. The arbiter supplies the missing decision mechanism of Gap 1 and its properties are proved in Chapter 4.

- *Contribution 4: Coordinated FACTS–EV control.* The thesis formulates voltage and flow control over shunt, series, and combined FACTS devices and three aggregated EV stations in one 9-dimensional action space, so that coordination is structural rather than negotiated between separate controllers. This addresses Gap 3 and is developed in Chapters 3 and 8.

- *Contribution 5: Multi-modal fault detection and localisation.* A detection layer fuses PMU, SCADA, and inverter telemetry to separate faults, false data injection, and benign anomalies, addressing Gap 4. Its design and its fusion logic are presented in Chapter 7.

- *Contribution 6: AI-based optimal placement of controllers.* Placement of FACTS devices and EV stations is formulated as a hybrid optimisation combining genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimisation, and deep reinforcement learning [64, 65, 66]. The method couples placement to the control objective rather than treating it as a separate planning exercise, and appears in Chapter 5.

- *Contribution 7: Digital twin methodology.* The thesis develops a digital twin workflow in which the offline QSTS benchmark, the Simulink model, and the real-time simulator remain synchronised representations of the same network. The methodology supports the validation argument of Gap 5 and is documented in Chapter 10.

- *Contribution 8: HIL validation methodology on a 4-core OPAL-RT.* The thesis contributes a validation methodology that allocates the controller and network across four simulator cores with explicit timing budgets of 5 ms, 50 ms, 10 ms, and 1 ms, and that progresses through model-in-the-loop, software-in-the-loop, processor-in-the-loop, and Controller-HIL stages [51, 52]. The methodology and its execution are the subject of Chapters 10 and 11.

- *Contribution 9: End-to-end reproducible pipeline.* All experiments run on the versioned OPSD public dataset [42], all code is tested by an automated suite of 52 tests, and every reported figure is traceable to a script. This contribution makes the scientific claims of the thesis independently checkable.

Taken together, the nine contributions form a single argument rather than nine separate results: a cognitive architecture whose components are individually analysable, whose coordination is structural, and whose deployment claims are tested on hardware rather than asserted in simulation.

## 1.8 Thesis Organization

The remainder of the thesis is organised into twelve further chapters. Chapter 2 reviews the literature across AI in power systems, FACTS devices, decision-making methods, EV integration, brain-inspired computing, DER integration technologies, and real-time simulation, closing with the gap analysis that motivates hybrid frameworks. Chapter 3 presents the CAPSM framework, its dual-process architecture, its mathematical models, and its design philosophy; *Section 3.10* is a new HIL-ready architecture design section that specifies how every component is prepared for real-time deployment from the outset.

Chapter 4 details the advanced AI control architecture, including the CNN-LSTM reflex network, the quantum-inspired deliberation layer, and the arbiter, with formal analyses of their properties; *Section 4.7* adds the real-time execution path, including export of trained models to the ONNX interchange format [101]. Chapter 5 develops AI-based optimal placement of FACTS devices and EV stations through genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimisation, and reinforcement learning, and *Section 5.8* extends the placement study to respect the constraints of real-time deployment. Chapter 6 addresses stability enhancement using deep learning, including PV-curve and critical-clearing-time analysis, and *Section 6.6* revisits the stability claims under the timing budget measured in the real-time campaign.

Chapter 7 presents multi-modal fault detection and localisation, including state-estimation-based residual methods and deep classifiers, with *Section 7.6* porting the detection stack to the Controller-HIL environment where sub-10 ms detection is demonstrated. Chapter 8 treats EV integration and DER management in depth, including charging behaviour, degradation, and V2G scheduling, and *Section 8.7* describes the aggregation model as implemented in real time. Chapter 9 develops coordinated control using hybrid AI, including multi-agent and distributed formulations, and *Section 9.6* maps the coordination scheme onto the multi-core real-time target.

Chapter 10 is the testing and validation framework, the central methodological chapter of the thesis; it presents the digital twin methodology, the V-model traceability argument, the rate-monotonic schedulability analysis of the four-core allocation, and, in *Section 10.11*, the full Controller-HIL campaign on the OPAL-RT. Chapter 11 reports the results and performance analysis, from the offline January 2019 benchmark through the HIL timing measurements, including the violation reductions, convergence, and inference-time figures that anchor the quantitative claims of the thesis. Chapter 12 discusses the implications of the results for operators, regulators, and researchers, including economic and policy considerations, and states the limitations of the study candidly. Chapter 13 concludes, summarises the contributions against the objectives of Section 1.6, and outlines future work in quantum hardware, market integration, and multi-agent extensions.
