# Chapter 10: Testing and Validation Framework

## 10.1 Three-Stage Validation Methodology

Claims about a controller that acts on a power network in real time are only as strong as the evidence chain behind them, and this chapter makes that chain explicit. The validation methodology proceeds through three stages of increasing fidelity and increasing cost: offline quasi-static simulation against real data, real-time-capable model preparation in Simulink, and Controller-Hardware-in-the-Loop (CHIL) execution on a 4-core OPAL-RT simulator. Each stage answers a distinct question. Stage 1 asks whether the algorithms are correct and beneficial on a year of real operating data; Stage 2 asks whether the models execute in real time and partition across processors; Stage 3 asks whether the controller meets its timing budgets on hardware and whether the hardware behaviour remains consistent with the offline results. Figure 10.1 shows the pipeline and the exit criteria that gate promotion between stages.

![Figure 10.1: Three-stage validation pipeline from offline QSTS to Controller-HIL.](figures/fig_validation_pipeline.png)

Stage 1 is the offline benchmark built on PYPOWER quasi-static time-series simulation with the OPSD data foundation. The environment replays real German load, wind, solar, and day-ahead price profiles at native case magnitudes on the IEEE 9, 14, 39, 118, and 300-bus test systems, applies controller actions, and solves an AC power flow at every timestamp [42]. The exit criteria are quantitative and complete: the 52-test pytest suite passes (52/52), power-flow convergence is 100% on every run, and data provenance is verified by cryptographic hashes of the input files and recorded random seeds. Only a build that meets all three criteria may advance, because every later stage inherits its reference numbers from this one.

Stage 2 prepares the real-time models. The IEEE 39-bus plant is taken from the DESL-EPFL repository implementation that is native to the OPAL-RT eMegaSim environment [57], extended with FACTS device subsystems built from Simscape Electrical component models [102] and with the three bidirectional EV V2G charger models of Chapter 8. The RT-LAB toolchain and the ARTEMIS solver suite prepare the model for real-time execution, splitting the network into subsystems with stable interfaces [56]. The exit criteria are that the complete model builds without error, partitions cleanly into the four-target allocation with no unresolved interface signals, and produces a CPU-load estimate within budget for every core.

Stage 3 is the Controller-HIL campaign. The plant executes on the OPAL-RT at a 50 µs electromagnetic-transient (EMT) step, while the CAPSM tasks (System 1, System 2, the Arbiter, and I/O) run as real-time tasks on the simulator's four cores under the timing budgets of Section 10.5. The exit criteria are that every core meets its deadline across the full scenario suite, and that the offline-to-HIL consistency checks show the hardware campaign reproducing the Stage-1 reference behaviour within the agreed tolerances. Real-time simulation technologies have a long validation pedigree, from early relay-testing simulators through modern multicore platforms [52], [53], [54], and the three-stage structure adopted here follows the accepted progression from numerical validation to hardware evidence [55].

## 10.2 Simulation Platform Development

The Stage-1 platform is a Python environment whose central object is a gym-like QSTS engine. At every step the engine takes one timestamp of the OPSD profiles, applies the controller action vector, and solves an AC power flow with the PYPOWER Newton solver; the observation returned to the controller comprises the 160-feature state defined in Chapter 4 (bus voltage magnitudes and angles, branch flows, generator injections, FACTS states, EV station states, and scalars), and the metrics frame records losses, voltage deviation, violations, and loading. The interface follows the reset/step/close convention so that controllers, baselines, and test drivers interact with the plant identically, which is what makes the controller comparisons of Chapter 11 statistically clean.

Two documented modelling rules keep the QSTS engine convergent on real data, and both are part of the platform specification rather than hidden heuristics. The renewable curtailment rule scales wind and solar injections down whenever their combined injection would exceed 90% of the current load; this rule activates in 15 of the 721 January 2019 hours (2.1%), and those hours are real extreme events in which national renewables approached the full load. The proportional redispatch rule scales the non-slack generator setpoints with net load, with the factor clipped to [0.2, 1.5] and capped at each unit's Pmax while the slack bus balances the residual; without it, the slack must absorb multi-gigawatt imbalances on low-load, high-wind nights and the Newton iteration diverges. Generator voltage setpoints are additionally capped at 1.04 p.u. in the environment constructor, which removed roughly 2,386 false-positive violation bus-hours inherent to the shipped case data and left the 2,847 genuine operational violations that the controllers address. Reactive load scales with each bus's constant power factor.

The platform carries the five test systems of Table 3.13's specification: IEEE 9 (native base load 315 MW, used for debug and HIL bring-up), IEEE 14 (259 MW, voltage-stability studies), IEEE 39 (6,254 MW, the primary benchmark), IEEE 118 (4,242 MW, scalability), and IEEE 300 (23,526 MW, offline phasor studies only). Measured QSTS execution costs on the uncontrolled benchmark are approximately 2, 3, 7.8, and 15 ms per step for the 9, 14, 39, and 118-bus systems respectively, and every run converges on every timestamp. These costs matter for the later stages because they bound how much of each controller period the plant-side computation can consume in software-in-the-loop configurations.

The Stage-2 toolchain converts the validated algorithms and plant models into real-time artefacts. The FACTS devices are rebuilt as EMT subsystems from Simscape Electrical components, with the steady-state implementations of Chapter 3 (controllable shunt susceptance for SVC and STATCOM, series reactance modification for TCSC, combined shunt and series for UPFC) mirrored at circuit level [102]. The trained controller models are exported through a portable runtime representation (ONNX [101]) so that the inference path executed on the simulator cores is the deployed artefact rather than a training framework. Integration middleware ties the layers together with four services: time synchronisation across the data foundation and the real-time target, event recording with per-step metric frames, fault and cyber-event injection hooks, and scenario management that reproduces the exact conditions of Table 10.5 across stages. This middleware is what allows the same January 2019 window to be replayed in Python, in host Simulink, and on the OPAL-RT with identical event timing.

## 10.3 Digital Twin Creation Methodology

The digital twin is the continuously reconciled digital counterpart of the physical system under study, and the twin concept originates in engineering asset management before its adoption by power systems [77], [78]. For the CAPSM test bed, the twin is not a single model but a stack of coordinated models with declared fidelity, data links, and calibration state, so that any experiment can state exactly which layer of the twin produced its numbers. Figure 10.2 shows the layered architecture: a physical layer holding the network and device models, a measurement and acquisition layer, a communication layer, a control layer hosting the CAPSM stack, and an analytics and visualisation layer that consumes the recorded frames. Each layer exposes a contract (variables, timing, and fidelity level) to the layers above it.

![Figure 10.2: Digital twin layered architecture for the CAPSM test bed.](figures/fig_digital_twin_layers.png)

Fidelity is declared per layer and per experiment, because a twin that claims a single fidelity is either wasteful or misleading. Table 10.2 defines the four levels used throughout the thesis. Functional models support algorithm development at negligible cost; behavioural models add converter-average dynamics for controller tuning; detailed EMT models resolve switching and protection for HIL testing; and high-precision models carry vendor-calibrated parameters and measured channel offsets for certification-grade runs. The January 2019 benchmark, for example, is a Stage-1 behavioural-level result, while the fault scenarios of Section 10.6 are detailed-level by construction.

**Table 10.2: Digital twin fidelity levels.**

| Level | Description | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | Steady-state phasor models with static device limits; no dynamics | Algorithm development, screening studies |
| Behavioural | Average-value converter and control dynamics; simplified excitation | Controller tuning, scenario sweeps, PIL dry runs |
| Detailed | EMT converter switching models, protection, magnetic saturation | HIL testing, fault and unbalanced studies |
| High-precision | Vendor-calibrated device parameters, measured channel offsets | Certification-grade validation, deployment rehearsal |

Data synchronisation is the mechanism that keeps the twin honest against the physical world, and in this thesis the reconciliation engine is the weighted-least-squares (WLS) state estimator of Chapter 7. Equation (10.1) restates the estimator: given measurements z with weights w and measurement functions h, the estimate x_hat minimises the weighted residual squared norm [74]. The twin consumes the estimator output in two directions: measurements correct the model state, and the residual statistics flag measurements or model segments whose agreement has degraded, which triggers recalibration. The chi-squared detection machinery of Chapter 7 runs on the same residuals, so bad-data rejection and cyber-attack detection share one statistical foundation with the twin's synchronisation loop.

```eq
%%10.1
\hat{x} = \arg\min_{x} \sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i \left( z_i - h_i(x) \right)^2
```

Calibration is a scheduled, evidence-driven activity rather than a one-off identification. Each calibration cycle compares twin predictions against recorded measurements over a defined window, estimates parameter corrections for the drifting elements (load compositions, converter losses, channel offsets), applies them under version control, and records the before-and-after metric deltas in the results database. The applications of the twin follow directly from this discipline: controller tuning against the current plant, what-if analysis for planned topology and demand changes, failure-mode exploration that would be unsafe on hardware, operator training on the behavioural and detailed levels, and progressive deployment in which new controller builds are shadow-tested against the twin before entering the HIL suite [77], [78]. In each application the declared fidelity level states how far the conclusion may be pushed, which is the twin's principal contribution to validation integrity.

## 10.4 Hardware-in-the-Loop Testing Architecture

The HIL architecture places a real-time simulation of the plant between the controller under test and the controlled process, so that the controller experiences plant dynamics it did not help compute. Figure 10.3 shows the CAPSM configuration: the OPAL-RT OP5700 target executes the IEEE 39-bus network with the ARTEMIS solver at a 50 µs EMT step and the ePHASORSIM phasor solver at a 1 ms step for slower monitoring loops [56]; the four CPU cores carry the allocation of Table 10.3; and the I/O subsystem connects the simulated grid to external instrumentation and to the controller tasks. The general characteristics and design rules of power-HIL interfaces follow the established literature on PHIL design and real-time simulation practice [51], [55].

![Figure 10.3: Controller-HIL testing architecture on the 4-core OPAL-RT target.](figures/fig_hil_architecture.png)

The four-core allocation is the organising contract of the whole campaign and is reproduced in Table 10.3 exactly as configured in the RT-LAB project. Core 1 owns the north region of the network (buses 1 to 19) and the System 1 reflex task on a 5 ms period with the highest priority; Core 2 owns the south region (buses 20 to 39) and the System 2 deliberative task on a 50 ms period; Core 3 owns the Metacognitive Arbiter and inter-subsystem communications on a 10 ms period; and Core 4 owns the I/O, logging, and HIL interface on a 1 ms period. The network split places the FACTS devices and EV stations of the benchmark close to the cores that control them, which keeps inter-core signal exchange on the slower coordination paths rather than in the control loops.

**Table 10.3: Four-core OPAL-RT allocation for CAPSM real-time execution.**

| Core | Subsystem name | Region | Component | Period | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | sm_master | North (buses 1 to 19) | Grid + System 1 CNN-LSTM | 5 ms | 1 |
| 2 | sm_slave1 | South (buses 20 to 39) | Grid + System 2 QIRL | 50 ms | 2 |
| 3 | sm_slave2 | Global | Arbiter + communications | 10 ms | 3 |
| 4 | sm_slave3 | I/O | I/O + logging + HIL interface | 1 ms | 4 |

Communication follows the substation and synchrophasor standards. Process-level signalling uses IEC 61850, whose GOOSE and Sampled Value services carry trip signals and measurement streams [91]; wide-area monitoring data uses the IEEE C37.118 synchrophasor format [89]; and time synchronisation across cores, I/O, and instrumentation uses the Precision Time Protocol (PTP, IEEE 1588) [92]. PTP offset estimation from the four timestamps of a delay-request exchange follows Equation (10.2), where t1 and t4 are the master-side transmit and receive times and t2 and t3 the slave-side receive and transmit times.

```eq
%%10.2
\hat{\theta} = \frac{(t_2 - t_1) - (t_4 - t_3)}{2}
```

**Proposition 10.1 (PTP synchronisation error bound).** The offset estimate of Equation (10.2) is exact when the forward and backward propagation delays are equal, and its error is bounded by half the delay asymmetry plus timestamping error: |epsilon_sync| ≤ |d_fwd − d_bwd| / 2 + epsilon_ts, as stated in Equation (10.3). Timestamping quantisation contributes epsilon_ts, and hardware timestamping holds this term near the sub-microsecond class, which is the accuracy class required by the measurement streams of Table 10.4 [92].

```eq
%%10.3
|\epsilon_{sync}| \le \frac{|d_{fwd} - d_{bwd}|}{2} + \epsilon_{ts}
```

The I/O interface translates between the simulated network and the physical world. Analogue output channels carry scaled voltage and current waveforms to amplification and measurement equipment; analogue inputs return physical sensor readings into the model; and digital channels carry breaker states and trip signals with deterministic latency. Channel scaling is calibrated so that a per-unit quantity in the model corresponds to the agreed physical range at the connector, and the calibration constants are stored in the project configuration under version control, so that any recorded hardware trace can be mapped back to per-unit quantities exactly.

Where power is amplified into physical devices, the interface becomes a power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) interface, and its compensation requires an explicit stability argument. The amplifier receives the compensated command of Equation (10.4): the simulated voltage V_sim is passed through the compensation transfer function G_comp(s) before amplification, and G_comp is designed to cancel the amplifier's own transfer distortion and to present the simulated network with the correct equivalent impedance [51].

```eq
%%10.4
V_{amp}(s) = G_{comp}(s)\, V_{sim}(s)
```

**Theorem 10.1 (Small-gain stability of the PHIL interface).** Consider the PHIL feedback interconnection of the simulated network and the amplified physical device, closed through the compensated interface V_amp = G_comp(s) V_sim. If the simulated subsystem is stable with induced gain g_sim and the compensation path has induced gain g_comp = sup_omega |G_comp(i omega)|, then the interconnection is input-output stable whenever g_sim times g_comp < 1. With the simulated subsystem passive and normalised so that g_sim ≤ 1, the sufficient design condition is

```eq
%%10.5
|G_{comp}(i\omega)| \le 1 \quad \forall \omega
```

*Proof sketch.* The coupling between the simulator and the amplifier is a linear feedback interconnection of two stable operators: the simulated network seen from the interface port, and the amplifier-plus-compensation path. The loop gain of this interconnection is the product of the two induced gains. By the small-gain theorem, a feedback interconnection of stable operators is stable when the product of their induced gains is below unity [113]. Absorbing the simulated-side gain into the condition (g_sim ≤ 1 by passivity and normalisation) yields Equation (10.5), with strict inequality preferred in practice so that the margin covers model error near resonances; practical implementations enforce the margin by adding a damped series impedance or a gentle low-pass roll-off to G_comp. This completes the sketch.

The communication interfaces are specified per service with bandwidth, latency, precision, and protocol, and Table 10.4 collects the design values used in the campaign. The values are interface specification targets for the test bed; they are verified during calibration (Section 10.10) by round-trip measurements on the deployed channels, and any deviation beyond tolerance blocks promotion to the next validation stage.

**Table 10.4: Communication interface specifications (design values for the test bed).**

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 10.5 Real-Time Task Scheduling

Real-time correctness is a scheduling property before it is a numerical one: a perfectly computed control action that misses its deadline is a wrong action. Each OPAL-RT core runs a fixed-priority scheduler over periodic tasks, and the analysis language for such systems is rate-monotonic (RM) scheduling, in which priorities are assigned by period, the shortest period receiving the highest priority [45]. For task i with worst-case execution time (WCET) C_i and period T_i, the individual utilisation is defined by Equation (10.6), and the total utilisation of a core with n tasks is given by Equation (10.7).

```eq
%%10.6
U_i = C_i / T_i
```

```eq
%%10.7
U_{\Sigma} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i / T_i
```

**Theorem 10.2 (Liu-Layland sufficient bound).** A set of n independent, preemptible, periodic tasks with relative deadlines equal to their periods is schedulable under rate-monotonic scheduling on a single processor if the total utilisation satisfies

```eq
%%10.8
U_{\Sigma} \le n \left( 2^{1/n} - 1 \right)
```

*Proof sketch.* The classical argument of Liu and Layland proceeds from the critical instant, the simultaneous release of all tasks, at which fixed-priority response times are maximised [45]. If any task is unschedulable, a deadline miss occurs at a synchronous release; accumulating the execution demand of the interfering higher-priority tasks over the response interval and iterating the demand recursion shows that the worst case for the schedulability condition is reached when each task's period equals the completion time of the task below it. Solving the resulting recursion yields the utilisation sequence whose limit is n(2^(1/n) − 1). Since any task set whose utilisation does not exceed this bound completes every job by its deadline regardless of phasing, the bound is sufficient. The bound is not necessary: task sets above it may still be schedulable for favourable phasing, which is why the exact response-time test below is also applied. This completes the sketch.

The exact schedulability check for a specific task set is the response-time recurrence of Equation (10.9), in which R_i is the worst-case response of task i and hp(i) denotes the tasks of higher priority on the same core; the task is schedulable when R_i ≤ D_i for every i, solved by iteration from R_i = C_i [45].

```eq
%%10.9
R_i = C_i + \sum_{j \in hp(i)} \left\lceil \frac{R_i}{T_j} \right\rceil C_j
```

The utilisation audit of the four-core allocation uses representative WCET estimates adopted for the timing verification analysis; each estimate is checked against measured execution times during deployment (Section 10.10), and the audit is repeated whenever the build changes. Core 1 hosts the north-region grid update (C = 3.4 ms) and the System 1 reflex task (C = 0.7 ms), both at T = 5 ms: Equation (10.7) gives U_Sigma = 0.68 + 0.14 = 0.82 against the n = 2 bound of 2(2^(1/2) − 1) = 0.828, so the core passes the sufficient test, with a thin margin that is exactly why the exact test of Equation (10.9) is also run at deployment. Core 2 hosts the south-region update (3.2 ms) and the System 2 task (1.1 ms) at T = 50 ms: U_Sigma = 0.086, comfortably inside the bound. Core 3 hosts the arbiter task (1.2 ms) and the communications task (1.0 ms) at T = 10 ms: U_Sigma = 0.22 against the n = 2 bound of 0.828, which passes with a wide margin. Core 4 differs in kind: it executes a single non-preemptible I/O and logging activity with C = 0.9 ms at T = 1 ms, so U = 0.9; with n = 1 the bound of Theorem 10.2 evaluates to 1.0, and a single non-preemptible periodic task is feasible exactly when C ≤ D = T, which holds here (0.9 ms ≤ 1 ms) and is verified every frame by the deadline monitor. The design rule adopted across the platform is that every preemptive core stays inside the sufficient bound, so that schedulability never depends on task phasing.

Figure 10.5 shows the resulting frame schedule across the four cores as a Gantt chart, with task periods, executions, and the arbitration message exchanges between cores. The visual makes the two-speed structure of the controller evident: Core 1's 5 ms reflex loop and Core 3's 10 ms arbitration loop interleave many times within one 50 ms deliberation cycle of Core 2, and Core 4's 1 ms I/O frames underpin them all. Algorithm 10.1 specifies the scheduling and deadline-monitoring procedure that runs on every core; its deadline-miss log is the primary timing artefact of the campaign and feeds the regression gates of Section 10.8.

![Figure 10.5: Real-time task scheduling across the four OPAL-RT cores.](figures/fig_core_gantt.png)

**Algorithm 10.1: Rate-monotonic scheduling and deadline monitoring.**

```text
Require: per-core task sets T_k = {(C_i, T_i, D_i, prio_i)}, frame period T_frame
Ensure:  dispatch schedule and deadline-miss log
# offline phase, executed per build
1: for each core k do
2:     compute U_Sigma(k) = sum_i C_i / T_i
3:     if U_Sigma(k) > n_k * (2^(1/n_k) - 1) then
4:         flag schedulability risk (preemptive RM sufficient bound violated)
5:     assign fixed priorities in rate-monotonic order (shortest T_i highest)
6:     solve response-time recurrences R_i; require R_i <= D_i for all i
# online phase, executed every frame
7: on frame boundary do
8:     release every task i whose period boundary has expired
9:     while ready set is not empty do
10:        dispatch the highest-priority ready task and execute
11:    for each completed task i do
12:        record response R_i = finish_time - release_time
13:        if R_i > D_i then
14:            log deadline miss (core, task, R_i) to the results database
15:            raise degraded-mode request (hold last setpoints, drop System 2 proposals)
16: return schedule, deadline-miss log
```

The end-to-end latency budget closes the scheduling picture. The RT-LAB configuration allocates a 20 ms maximum latency to the PMU measurement channel, and the tier-specific link budgets are 5 ms for the System 1 reflex path, 50 ms for the System 2 deliberative path, and 10 ms for the command path to the I/O core; these are per-link budgets on distinct paths rather than additive stages of one chain. The budget decomposition is checked cumulatively at deployment by timestamping messages at both ends of each link, and the measured per-link latencies appear in the timing report of Chapter 11 alongside the deadline statistics of Algorithm 10.1.

## 10.6 Scenario Design for HIL Testing

Scenario design determines what the HIL campaign can claim, because a controller validated only on benign conditions has been validated for nothing in particular. The scenario set follows three principles: normal operation must dominate the suite, because the controller's duty cycle is overwhelmingly normal; rare extremes from the real data must appear, because they are where the controller earns its keep; and adversarial injections must include faults, renewable extremes, and cyber-attacks, because the controller must degrade gracefully rather than catastrophically. Every scenario is anchored in the OPSD data foundation so that the same conditions can be replayed in Stages 1 and 2, and Table 10.5 summarises the five scenarios with their durations, injection parameters, and metrics.

**Table 10.5: HIL test scenarios.**

| Scenario | Duration | Injection parameters | Metrics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal operation | 721 h profile offline; 3,600 s accelerated window per HIL run | OPSD January 2019 load and renewable profiles (20% wind, 10% solar), no injected events | Violation bus-hours, mean and max losses, min and max Vm, convergence rate, per-step latency |
| Symmetrical fault | 60 s run | Three-phase fault at bus 29 cleared after 100 ms; companion N-1 trip of line 16-17 | Detection latency, clearing and recovery, post-event losses, controller budget compliance |
| Renewable variability | 120 s run | 60% generation drop at bus 33 at t = 10 s; profile anchors from mined 3-h national ramps up to +28,390 MW | Frequency nadir and recovery, FACTS response, EV ramp tracking, violation counts |
| Cyber FDI | 60 s run | Voltage-magnitude bias of +0.02 to +0.06 p.u. (nominal 0.05) on selected buses (e.g., 5 and 10) from t = 20 s | Detection rate, false positives, time to detection, Safe Mode activation and recovery |
| EV V2G coordination | 300 s run | Coordinated V2G/G2V engagement at stations 3, 8, 15 from t = 60 s; frequency event injection | Fleet response time, power tracking error, SoC feasibility, secondary restoration time |

The normal-operation scenario replays the full January 2019 benchmark: 721 hours of real load, wind, solar, and price shapes on the IEEE 39-bus system, compressed into accelerated real-time windows of 3,600 s per run for the campaign. Its purpose is baseline establishment, and its metrics are exactly the Stage-1 reference quantities (2,847 violation bus-hours uncontrolled, mean losses 46.11 MW, minimum voltage 0.877 p.u., 721/721 convergence) against which the hardware campaign's consistency is judged. The accelerated replay preserves the profile shape and event timing while keeping each hardware run within a practical wall-clock envelope; the mapping is recorded in the run manifest so that any timestamp in a hardware log can be traced to its source hour in the data foundation.

The symmetrical-fault scenario injects a three-phase fault at bus 29, cleared after 100 ms, together with the companion N-1 trip of line 16-17 whose offline behaviour is already characterised: the Phase-2 measurement of that trip at real operating conditions showed losses rising from 51.1 to 58.7 MW (+14.9%), a minimum system voltage of 0.9505 p.u. during the contingency window, convergence in all 13 test repetitions, and bit-for-bit restoration of the pre-event state (Stage-1 measured). The HIL question is whether the controller detects and responds within budget on hardware; the HIL-campaign outcome for fault detection on the platform is in the sub-10 ms class, and the scenario records detection latency, clearing, and the recovery trajectory as its primary metrics. The faulted bus and the fault impedance are configuration parameters, and the development configuration also supports alternative buses (bus 14 in the project defaults), so the scenario family rather than a single point fault is what the campaign validates.

The renewable-variability scenario exercises the ramp problem at hardware timescales. The baseline injection is a 60% generation drop at bus 33 applied at t = 10 s of the 120 s run, and the profile anchors are the largest mined three-hour national ramps from the OPSD data, +28,390 MW upward and −23,588 MW downward, which bound the variability the fleet-scale system has exhibited in the data foundation. The scenario measures how the FACTS devices absorb the fast edge while the EV stations ramp behind them, and its metrics (frequency nadir, recovery time, ramp tracking error, violations) quantify the anticipation behaviour of Chapter 9 in hardware terms.

The cyber scenario injects false data into the observation layer: a voltage-magnitude bias of +0.02 to +0.06 p.u. (nominal 0.05) on selected buses, with buses 5 and 10 in the development configuration, beginning at t = 20 s of the 60 s run. The attack is a stealthy bias rather than an outage, so its detection is statistical, and the detection machinery of Chapter 7 (residual testing with chi-squared thresholds) is the responder. The scenario records the detection rate across repeated runs, the false-positive rate on un-attacked windows, the time from injection to alarm, and the Safe Mode activation and recovery path; these are exactly the functional metrics of Section 10.9 in their adversarial form.

The EV V2G scenario runs the coordinated charging and discharging of the three stations from t = 60 s of the 300 s run, with a frequency event injected to force V2G/G2V transitions within the secondary-control timescale. The scenario verifies the properties proven in Chapter 8 under real-time execution: the SoC window [0.2, 0.9] is respected by the mode-transition logic at every step, station powers track their setpoints through the transition, and the fleet response arrives within the secondary-control band. Its metrics (response time, tracking error, SoC feasibility, restoration time) are the hardware counterparts of the offline dispatch tests.

## 10.7 Progressive Validation from MIL to CHIL

Progressive validation promotes a build through four test levels, each with a defined scope and exit criteria, so that defects are caught at the cheapest level that can expose them. The levels are model-in-the-loop (MIL), where the controller runs as software against the Python plant; software-in-the-loop (SIL), where the real-time models execute in host simulation; processor-in-the-loop (PIL), where the controller code runs on target-class processors against simulated plant; and controller-hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL), where the full real-time target closes the loop. The V-model of Figure 10.4 organises the progression: each development artefact on the left leg (requirements, algorithms, models, deployment code) is paired with a validation activity on the right leg (unit tests, benchmark validation, PIL timing, CHIL scenarios), and traceability means every right-leg activity cites the artefact it verifies.

![Figure 10.4: V-model of progressive validation from MIL to CHIL.](figures/fig_vmodel.png)

The four levels answer four different questions. MIL asks whether the algorithms are correct on real data, and its evidence is the Stage-1 suite (52/52 tests, 100% convergence, provenance verified). SIL asks whether the real-time models are numerically faithful and partitionable, and its evidence is clean builds, stable interfaces, and host-side timing estimates. PIL asks whether the controller code executes within budget on target-class hardware, and its evidence is measured WCET against the per-task budgets. CHIL asks whether the integrated system behaves under real-time constraints with real I/O, and its evidence is the scenario suite of Table 10.5 executed with deadline monitoring. This staged structure is the standard discipline for HIL programmes [55], and Table 10.6 states the exit criteria that gate each promotion.

**Table 10.6: Progressive validation stages and exit criteria.**

| Stage | Platform | Validation scope | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| MIL | Python, PYPOWER QSTS | Algorithm-level correctness on real OPSD data | 52/52 tests pass; 100% power-flow convergence; data provenance verified |
| SIL | Simulink / RT-LAB host simulation | Numerical fidelity of EMT and phasor models | Model builds without error; clean four-way partition; CPU-load estimate within budget |
| PIL | Controller code on target-class processor | Embedded timing of CAPSM tasks | Measured WCET within per-task budgets; communication round trip within tier latency |
| CHIL | 4-core OPAL-RT with I/O | Integrated real-time behaviour | Timing budgets met on all cores; scenario suite passes; offline-to-HIL consistency within tolerance |

Algorithm 10.2 specifies the promotion procedure. Its two design choices matter for audit integrity. First, promotion is blocked by any failing criterion, and the failing evidence is filed as a defect with the build identifier, so the history of a build can be reconstructed from the results database alone. Second, every promotion writes a manifest (data hashes, seeds, configuration snapshot, model version) into the database, which is what makes a later analysis able to reproduce the exact conditions of any recorded run.

**Algorithm 10.2: Progressive validation with promotion criteria.**

```text
Require: candidate build B, validation stage s in {MIL, SIL, PIL, CHIL}
         exit-criteria set E(s) per Table 10.6, Stage-1 reference metrics
Ensure:  promotion decision and validation record
1: run the exit-criteria test suite for stage s on build B
2: for each criterion e in E(s) do
3:     if e fails then
4:         block promotion; file defect with failing evidence and build id
5: compare key metrics (violations, losses, min Vm) for B against the
   Stage-1 reference for the same scenario window
6: if the relative gap exceeds the stage tolerance then block promotion
7: if s == CHIL then additionally require:
8:     timing budgets met on all four cores (Algorithm 10.1 logs)
9:     offline-to-HIL consistency within the reporting tolerance
10: if all criteria pass then
11:    promote B to the next stage
12:    write manifest (data hashes, seeds, config snapshot, model version)
13: return decision, validation record
```

The V-model's traceability argument deserves one explicit sentence, because it is the defence against silent scope creep. A validation activity is admissible in the campaign only if it names the artefact it verifies and the criterion it applies; conversely, every criterion in Table 10.6 names the artefacts it covers. Under this discipline, a build that reaches CHIL has, by construction, passed every lower-level criterion relevant to it, and any gap in the chain is visible in the database rather than discoverable only after a hardware failure.

## 10.8 Automated Regression Testing

Regression protection on this platform is automated and quantitative. The pytest suite stands at 52 tests, accumulated by phase: 8 data-layer tests after Phase 1, 16 after Phase 2 (8 data plus 8 grid), 22 cumulative after Phase 3 (adding grid and controller tests), 35 after Phase 4, 44 after Phase 5, and 52 after Phase 6. The suite covers the data foundation (gap policy, interpolation masks, split integrity), the grid environment (power-flow equivalence with a direct PYPOWER run, FACTS device effects, EV dispatch bounds over consecutive steps, line trip and restoration bit-for-bit, FDI corruption of targeted measurements), the controllers (behavioural cloning training, QIRL candidate evaluation and amplitude update, arbiter modes), and the metrics frame. A build that fails any test cannot be promoted, and the suite runs on every proposed change.

Continuous integration executes the suite and the benchmark smoke runs on every commit, and the regression gates are explicit thresholds rather than human judgement: power-flow convergence must remain 100% on the smoke windows; violation counts and losses for each controller must not regress beyond a small tolerance against the recorded reference; and the timing margins of Algorithm 10.1 must stay positive on all four cores for the timing-relevant builds. A gate failure blocks the merge, and the results database records the blocked build with its failing metrics, so regressions are visible as a time series rather than as surprises.

The results database is the campaign's memory. Every run (test, benchmark window, or HIL scenario) writes a frame with its configuration hash, data hashes, seeds, code version, per-step metrics, and artefact links; queries over this database produce the comparison tables of Chapter 11 and the trend charts used in development. Because the database is keyed by content hashes rather than by trust, any two runs can be compared on equal footing, and any recorded number can be traced to the exact code and data that produced it.

Reproducibility is anchored cryptographically. The OPSD hourly file (130,339,665 bytes, first-16 SHA-256 6a7f2bc571314cbf) and the 15-minute file (112,072,477 bytes, first-16 SHA-256 194f3ee7d110d043) are fingerprinted at ingest and re-verified at every run start [42]; random seeds for the QIRL sampling, scenario generation, and any stochastic test are recorded in the manifest; and the promotion manifest of Algorithm 10.2 binds a build to a data version, a seed set, and a configuration snapshot. A third party holding the repository, the data files, and the manifest can therefore reproduce any published number to numerical identity, which is the standard this thesis adopts for its own results.

## 10.9 Validation Metrics and Reporting

The metric hierarchy is four-level, and each level answers for a different audience. Technical metrics quantify the controller against the network: violation bus-hours, mean and maximum losses, minimum and maximum voltage magnitude, convergence rate, and per-step computation time. Functional metrics quantify the protective functions: detection rate, false-positive rate, and detection latency. Operational metrics quantify service behaviour: availability of the control function, curtailment energy, and operating cost. Business metrics quantify the investment case: return on investment, payback period, net present value, and internal rate of return. The levels are nested: a business claim is admissible only if the operational numbers beneath it are recorded, and those only if the technical frames exist.

The technical level is anchored by the Stage-1 reference frames. For the January 2019 IEEE 39-bus benchmark these are the NoControl frame (2,847 violations, 46.11 MW mean losses, 0.877 p.u. minimum voltage, 721/721 convergence, 23.5 ms per step) and the controller frames that improve on it (System 1: 2,822 violations at 46.15 MW; System 2: 2,810 at 46.19 MW; CAPSM: 2,813 at 46.20 MW; all Stage-1 measured). Cross-platform comparison extends the same frames across the validation stages: the same window is replayed in Python, in host simulation, and on hardware, and the metrics are compared platform by platform, which is how the offline-to-HIL consistency of Chapter 11 is computed.

The functional and operational levels add the protective and service dimensions. The functional headline of the HIL campaign is fault detection within the sub-10 ms class on hardware (HIL-campaign result), with detection rate and false-positive behaviour recorded across the cyber scenario repeats; the associated framework design targets (95.8% fault detection and localisation accuracy, 74% false-positive reduction relative to conventional schemes) are labelled as design targets and are assessed in Chapter 11 against the measured distributions. The operational level records energy cost and curtailment; for the uncontrolled January 2019 window the energy cost is 171.625 MEUR (Stage-1 measured over the replayed prices), and the framework's curtailment-reduction design target is 42.5%, again labelled as a target rather than a measured Stage-1 outcome.

The business level is reported with the same labelling discipline. The IEEE 118-bus ten-year economic study of the framework specification reports 12.4% annual cost savings (51.5M dollars per year), a payback of 1.7 years, an internal rate of return of 83.7%, and a net present value of 378.6M dollars; these are design targets from the framework specification and are presented as such, with the formal NPV, IRR, and ROI definitions and their computation deferred to Chapter 11. The labelling matters because a thesis that mixes design targets into measured tables loses the reader's trust precisely where it matters most.

Uncertainty quantification closes the reporting loop. Point metrics are reported with their variation: paired differences between controllers over the 721 hourly samples support the statistical tests of Chapter 11; the quantile forecasts of Chapter 8 supply the input-side uncertainty bands; and scenario repeats under different seeds characterise the stochastic elements of the controller itself. Reporting conventions follow one rule throughout: every number in this thesis carries its provenance label (design target, Stage-1 measured, or HIL-campaign result), and tables that mix levels separate the labels explicitly.

## 10.10 OPAL-RT Setup and Configuration

The practical deployment begins on the host workstation, where the RT-LAB project defines the model, the subsystem split, the communication channels, and the target mapping before anything is compiled [56]. Figure 10.6 shows the deployment topology: the host PC runs the RT-LAB project and the operator interface; the OP5700 target executes the four subsystems of Table 10.3 on four cores; the I/O conditioning connects the target to amplification, measurement, and the physical device bay where CHIL interfaces are made; and the PTP grandmaster distributes time to the target and the instrumentation. The plant model originates from the DESL-EPFL eMegaSim implementation of the IEEE 39-bus system [57], which was built for the OPAL-RT environment and adapted here to the OP5700 target and to the CAPSM subsystem split.

![Figure 10.6: OPAL-RT deployment topology for the CAPSM real-time campaign.](figures/fig_opal_topology.png)

Model splitting follows the allocation contract exactly. The network is cut into the north region (buses 1 to 19, assigned to Core 1 with System 1) and the south region (buses 20 to 39, assigned to Core 2 with System 2); the cut points are chosen on branches with well-conditioned interface signals, and the interface variables are resolved by the RT-LAB communication infrastructure at each step. The Arbiter and communications subsystem on Core 3 subscribes to both regions' states and publishes the blended commands, and the I/O subsystem on Core 4 subscribes to the signals required by the physical channels. Splitting rules are enforced in the project: no algebraic loops across partitions, every inter-subsystem signal mapped and versioned, and no control loop closed through a slower core than its period requires.

Table 10.7 states the six-step build and deployment procedure with its tools, outputs, and verification actions. The procedure is deliberately boring: each step produces a named artefact and a named verification, and a step that fails verification stops the deployment rather than being patched informally.

Calibration and timing verification complete the deployment. Calibration verifies the channel scalings against reference instruments, sets the amplifier gain and the interface compensation of Theorem 10.1, and confirms the communication round trips of Table 10.4; its outputs are the calibration constants stored under version control. Timing verification then executes the scenario suite of Table 10.5 with the deadline monitor active, producing the timing report whose structure is Component, Target Time, Measured Time, Margin, and Status; Figure 10.7 shows the timing budget across the control layers, and the measured report for the campaign is tabulated in Chapter 11. A deployment is accepted only when every core's deadline statistics are clean across the suite, because a controller that meets its budget only on average has not met it at all in a real-time system.

## 10.11 Chapter Summary

This chapter specified the testing and validation framework that carries the thesis claims from algorithms to hardware evidence. The three-stage methodology (offline PYPOWER QSTS against the OPSD foundation, real-time model preparation in Simulink with the DESL-EPFL IEEE 39-bus plant, and CHIL on the 4-core OPAL-RT) was defined with quantitative exit criteria at every gate. The simulation platform, its two documented convergence rules (renewable curtailment above the 0.90 load fraction and proportional redispatch clipped to [0.2, 1.5]), and the 1.04 p.u. generator-voltage cap were specified as the measurable contract of Stage 1, whose January 2019 benchmark yields the reference frames (2,847 violations uncontrolled, 46.11 MW mean losses, 0.877 p.u. minimum voltage) used by every later comparison.

The HIL architecture was fixed as the four-core allocation of Table 10.3 with ARTEMIS at 50 µs and ePHASORSIM at 1 ms, standard communications (IEC 61850, IEEE C37.118, PTP with the Proposition 10.1 error bound), and a PHIL interface whose compensation obeys the small-gain condition of Theorem 10.1. Real-time correctness was analysed with rate-monotonic scheduling: Theorem 10.2 supplies the sufficient utilisation bound, the exact response-time test refines it, and the core-by-core audit (0.82 against 0.828 on Core 1, 0.086 on Core 2, 0.22 on Core 3, and a feasible single non-preemptible task at U = 0.9 on Core 4) demonstrates that the allocation meets its budgets by construction rather than by luck, with Algorithm 10.1 enforcing deadlines at run time.

The five HIL scenarios were specified with injections, durations, and metrics; the four-level progressive validation (MIL, SIL, PIL, CHIL) was given exit criteria and the promotion procedure of Algorithm 10.2; and the regression discipline (52/52 pytest gates, cryptographic data provenance with SHA-256 fingerprints, seeds, manifests, and the results database) makes every recorded number reproducible. The metrics hierarchy (technical, functional, operational, business) with its provenance labels completes the reporting contract. Chapter 11 applies this framework and reports the measured outcomes of the offline benchmark and the HIL campaign side by side.
