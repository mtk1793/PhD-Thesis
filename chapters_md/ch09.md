# Chapter 9: Coordinated Control Using Hybrid AI

## 9.1 Hierarchical Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

The CAPSM core of Chapters 3 and 4 coordinates two cognitive systems, but the plant itself is multi-agent by construction: four FACTS devices and three EV stations act on different buses with different actuation physics, and the DER layer of Chapter 8 adds resources with their own dynamics and constraints. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) provides the formal vocabulary for this setting: agents learn policies over their local action spaces while the environment responds to the joint action, and the survey of Busoniu et al. consolidates the convergence and scalability issues that distinguish the multi-agent case from single-agent learning [69]. The engineering task is to keep the coordination structure shallow enough to remain verifiable while rich enough to represent the plant.

Figure 9.1 shows the hierarchical architecture adopted here. Each FACTS device and each EV aggregator hosts a specialised agent with its own policy over its action subspace; a global critic evaluates the joint state and the joint action, giving every agent access to a value signal that reflects team performance rather than selfish return; and counterfactual credit assignment computes, for each agent, the advantage of its actual action against the expectation over its own policy, which attributes team outcomes to individual decisions [70]. The hierarchy is deliberately shallow: one coordination layer, the Metacognitive Arbiter, above seven device-level agents keeps the credit-assignment problem tractable and the communication load bounded.

![Figure 9.1: Hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning architecture.](figures/fig_marl_hierarchy.png)

Formally, the agents maximise the shared discounted objective of Equation (9.1), and the counterfactual advantage of Equation (9.2) is the quantity attributed to agent i: the difference between the joint value of the taken action and the expected joint value had agent i acted according to its policy while the others held theirs. The agent policies are continuous-action actor-critic networks, for which the DDPG family [24] and its clipped-double-Q refinement against value overestimation [25] are the natural parameterisations over bounded setpoint spaces, while the deliberative path's candidate-search character follows the planning-first lineage of [26].

```eq
%%9.1
J(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_N) = \mathrm{E}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r_t \right]
```

```eq
%%9.2
A_i(s, a) = Q(s, a_{-i}, a_i) - \sum_{a_i'} \pi_i(a_i' \mid s)\, Q(s, a_{-i}, a_i')
```

Convergence in MARL is conditional, and stating the conditions honestly matters for an engineering claim. Under the conditions assembled in [69], joint learning converges to a Nash equilibrium in the tabular, discounted setting when: the learning rates diminish under the usual stochastic-approximation schedule; the policy and critic updates pass through Lipschitz-continuous function approximators; each agent's environment is stationary from its own perspective, which holds when the other agents' policies change slowly relative to critic adaptation; and exploration persists over the joint action space. With function approximation these conditions are preserved only approximately, and convergence is local rather than global. The CAPSM deployment respects the spirit of these conditions in the sense relevant to operation: agent policies are frozen between validation campaigns, so the runtime system executes a fixed joint policy rather than an online-learning process, and learning happens offline where the conditions can be audited.

The communication structure of the hierarchy follows the agent decomposition. Each device-level agent observes its local state (terminal voltage, flows on adjacent branches, its own actuator state) together with the shared global critic output; it publishes its proposed action to the Arbiter; and it receives back the blended command plus the arbiter confidence signal. This star topology around the Arbiter avoids pairwise agent-to-agent channels, whose number grows quadratically with the fleet, and it mirrors the physical communication budget of the RT-LAB configuration in which all controller messages traverse the coordination core. The topology also simplifies the counterfactual computation of Equation (9.2), because the global critic already evaluates the joint action, so each agent's baseline requires only its own policy expectation rather than a re-estimation of all others.

The relation to CAPSM is exact rather than metaphorical. The Metacognitive Arbiter is the executive over a hierarchy of specialised agents: System 1 supplies the reflex path, System 2 the deliberative path, and the arbiter blends their outputs per device and per station. In MARL terms, the two systems are two policy families over the same joint action space, and the arbiter's blending weight alpha (Chapter 4) is a runtime mixture over families conditioned on the stress metric c1. This reading has a practical consequence: any admissibility guarantee obtained for the agent layer transfers to the runtime blend, because blending convex-combines admissible actions and therefore preserves the action-box closure established in Chapter 4.

## 9.2 Coordinated Control of FACTS Devices and EV Fleets

FACTS devices and EV stations differ by two orders of magnitude in response speed, and coordination must respect this asymmetry. SVC and STATCOM susceptance responds within milliseconds, TCSC and UPFC series injection within tens of milliseconds, while EV station power changes over seconds to minutes, limited by converter ramps and SoC transitions. The consequence for dispatch is temporal layering: FACTS actuation absorbs the fast edge of a disturbance, and EV power repositions the operating point behind it. Treating both resource classes with a single undifferentiated control law would either under-exploit the FACTS speed or demand EV ramps the hardware cannot deliver.

Model predictive control (MPC) provides the anticipation mechanism. Equation (9.3) states the receding-horizon problem: at each decision instant the controller optimises voltage deviation and losses over a horizon H, applies the first control move, and re-optimises at the next instant [67], [68]. Within this structure the EV ramp constraint of Equation (9.4) makes station ramps explicit, so the optimiser cannot plan EV trajectories that the hardware cannot follow. The horizon H = 6 steps matches the reserved planning horizon of the QIRL layer, which keeps the anticipatory window consistent across the deliberative path.

```eq
%%9.3
\min_{u_{t:t+H-1}} \sum_{k=t}^{t+H} \left( w_v V_{dev,k} + w_l L_k \right) + V_T(x_{t+H})
```

```eq
%%9.4
|P_{EV,b}(k) - P_{EV,b}(k-1)| \le R_b^{ramp} \Delta t, \quad b \in \{3, 8, 15\}
```

The dispatch logic block between the agents and the plant executes three stages. First, it separates actions by actuation speed: FACTS setpoints route to the fast path, and EV setpoints route to the ramp-limited path. Second, it applies the MPC anticipation to the EV path, adjusting station targets against the forecast trajectory of demand and renewable output so that ramps begin before the disturbance matures rather than after it. Third, it forwards both paths to the Arbiter blending of Chapter 4, which remains the single authority that commits actions to the plant. The block carries no state between decision steps apart from the rolling-horizon state, which keeps its real-time footprint compatible with the 10 ms arbitration period of the HIL deployment described in Chapter 10.

The joint action space of the coordinated problem is modest but heterogeneous. Four FACTS setpoints (the SVC susceptance, the STATCOM susceptance, the TCSC compensation degree, and the two-component UPFC injection) join three EV station powers, giving the nine-dimensional action space fixed in Chapter 3. Heterogeneity, not dimensionality, is the difficulty: the FACTS coordinates are instantaneous and continuous with no energy state, while each EV coordinate is coupled to a slowly integrating SoC state that bounds how long a given power can be sustained. The dispatch logic block respects this by treating the FACTS coordinates as algebraic and the EV coordinates as dynamic, so the MPC of Equations (9.3) and (9.4) optimises over trajectories for the latter and over setpoints for the former. The same split propagates to the validation layer, where FACTS behaviour is checked against dynamic-range tests and EV behaviour against SoC-feasibility tests.

The value of anticipation is easiest to see at the ramp boundaries. A forecast evening load rise with falling solar output produces a predictable voltage sag at the EV station buses; with MPC anticipation, the stations begin ramping their setpoints one to two steps early and the FACTS shunt devices are pre-positioned so that the fast correction acts from a favourable starting point. Without anticipation, the same disturbance is met only after the deviation has matured, and the FACTS devices must absorb the full transient within their dynamic range. The distinction is behavioural rather than algebraic, and the HIL scenarios of Section 9.6 exercise exactly this difference.

## 9.3 Integration of DERs within the Control Framework

The DER layer of Chapter 8 enters the control framework as additional agents with slower timescales and richer constraints. Solar PV at buses 8 and 15 of the benchmark contributes voltage support through inverter reactive capability and energy shifting through co-located storage; battery energy storage systems provide dispatchable energy; and aggregated demand response contributes load modulation that behaves statistically like a virtual storage resource when controlled at scale [82], [83]. The integration principle is unchanged from the FACTS-EV case: each resource is an agent with a local action space and local constraints, and the global critic scores joint outcomes.

Coordination across agents can be centralised, but centralisation concentrates both computation and failure modes. The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) decomposes the coordination problem into agent-local subproblems coupled only through consensus variables, with the x-update of Equation (9.5) representative of the split [44]. The survey of [46] documents the convergence behaviour of such schemes across power-system applications, and Chapter 6 of this thesis already employs ADMM for distributed optimal power flow; here it is the designated pattern for scaling coordination beyond the seven benchmark agents. Each aggregator solves its local problem and exchanges consensus variables with its neighbours, so the arbitration layer keeps its 10 ms budget as the fleet grows.

```eq
%%9.5
x^{k+1} = \arg\min_{x}\; f(x) + \frac{\rho}{2} \| x - z^k + u^k \|_2^2
```

Demand response deserves a separate remark because its actuation model differs fundamentally from the storage-like resources. Individual flexible loads cannot be commanded; they can only be offered opportunities and incentives, and their aggregate response must therefore be estimated and controlled statistically. The literature on load-based frequency control shows how thermostat-type loads can be modelled as populations with state distributions, so that a control signal shifts the distribution rather than any single load, and the aggregate tracks a target with quantifiable error [82], [83]. Within the CAPSM framework this aggregate enters as an additional slow agent whose action is a bid curve rather than a setpoint, and whose uncertainty is handled by the same scenario machinery as the EV availability of Chapter 8. The practical benefit is diversification: an aggregate of many small loads fails gracefully, one participant at a time, rather than losing the full resource in a single event.

Multi-timescale integration is the organising principle that binds the DER agents to the real-time core. The 50 µs electromagnetic-transient layer resolves converter physics; the 1 ms phasor layer resolves network dynamics; the 5 ms reflex path (System 1) and 10 ms arbitration resolve fast control; the 50 ms deliberative path (System 2) resolves optimisation-grade decisions; and the hourly scheduling layer sets SoC trajectories and commitments. Synthetic inertia of Chapter 8 [85], [86] binds the DER layer to the fast tiers, because emulated inertia is exercised precisely at the timescales where the reflex path lives. Each tier consumes the tier above it as a slowly varying setpoint and the tier below it as a fast correction, and this time-scale separation is what keeps the hierarchy stable rather than merely functional.

## 9.4 Adaptive Control Strategies for Dynamic Conditions

Operating conditions shift on three horizons: seasonal demand patterns, daily renewable cycles, and event-driven topology changes. The framework adapts at each horizon with a different mechanism. Seasonal and daily shifts are absorbed by the forecast ensemble weighting of Chapter 8 and by retraining cycles between validation campaigns. Event-driven changes are handled online: the stress metric c1 rises with the disturbance, the Arbiter shifts blend weight toward the deliberative path, and the safe-mode machinery of Chapter 3 holds the last admissible setpoints if both cognitive paths are compromised.

Three online mechanisms implement event adaptation. First, online model updating refreshes the critic and the System 1 encoder with post-event data, so the value estimates reflect the new topology within minutes of its occurrence. Second, reward shaping on topology change re-weights the violation penalty for buses whose limits become binding after an event, steering both cognitive systems toward the newly constrained region of the action space. Third, the QIRL amplitude set is re-initialised after major events: the amplitudes return to the uniform 1/sqrt(32) initialisation of Chapter 4, which restores exploration exactly where the prior candidate distribution has become obsolete. The 10% tunnel rate of the QIRL layer provides this re-initialisation continuously at smaller scale, replacing a tenth of the candidates with fresh samples every decision step.

The line 16-17 trip illustrates the budget within which adaptation must complete. Phase-2 measurement of this N-1 event, injected at real operating conditions on 2019-01-15 12:00 UTC, showed losses rising from 51.1 to 58.7 MW (+14.9%) and the minimum system voltage reaching 0.9505 p.u. during the contingency window, with the power flow converged in all 13 test repetitions and restoration returning the pre-event state bit-for-bit (Stage-1 measured). The controller's response budget for such events is the 50 ms deliberative period: within one period the stress metric rises, the arbiter shifts its blend weight, System 2 re-evaluates candidates against the changed network, and new setpoints are committed to the plant. Adaptation is therefore not an offline afterthought but a bounded-latency runtime behaviour, and the HIL campaign of Section 9.6 verifies that the budget holds on hardware.

Renewable variability stresses adaptation differently from topology change, because the statistics drift rather than jump. The penetration defaults of the benchmark (20% wind and 10% solar of native base load, with a maximum instantaneous renewable share of 115.7% and three-hour national ramps reaching +28,390 MW in the mined OPSD extremes) mean that the controller faces operating points whose renewable fraction varies by more than the entire base load across a single day. The online mechanisms handle this drift in two ways. The forecast ensemble re-weights its members as the statistics move, so the scenario sets consumed by the QIRL layer track the day's character; and the arbiter's stress metric integrates the consequence, raising deliberative engagement during hours when the renewable-driven voltage excursions approach the 0.03 p.u. threshold. Adaptation to variability is therefore continuous and statistical, while adaptation to topology events is discrete and rule-governed, and the framework applies the correct mechanism to each horizon.

Adaptation also has a conservative side. Not every change deserves a response: the arbiter's stress threshold of 0.03 p.u. and the sigmoid blending of Chapter 4 make the controller insensitive to perturbations below the threshold, which prevents oscillatory re-adaptation around the boundary. The combination of threshold-based engagement, bounded re-initialisation, and safe-mode fallback gives the framework a layered defence: small changes are absorbed by the blend, large ones by deliberation and re-initialisation, and pathological ones by the safe mode that holds the last admissible state.

## 9.5 Balancing Multiple Control Objectives

Voltage quality, network losses, and constraint violations pull the controller in different directions, and the framework resolves the conflict by weighted scalarisation. Equation (9.6) states the runtime objective J, with V_dev the mean voltage deviation, L the losses, and N_viol the count of violating buses in the current step; the weights correspond to the implemented reward (w_v = 1.0 on deviation, w_l = 0.5 with losses normalised by a 50 MW base, and a violation penalty of −10 per violating bus per step). Scalarisation reduces multi-objective control to single-objective optimisation at the price of choosing weights, and the choice of weights is itself a control-design decision with measurable consequences.

```eq
%%9.6
J = w_v V_{dev} + w_l L + w_p N_{viol}
```

The defence of scalarisation is Pareto theory. Equation (9.7) defines Pareto dominance over the objective vector (f_1, ..., f_M), and Theorem 9.1 states the classical result that minimisers of positive weighted sums cannot be Pareto dominated [97], [98].

```eq
%%9.7
a \leq_{P} b \iff \forall m:\, f_m(a) \le f_m(b)  \;\mathrm{and}\;  \exists m:\, f_m(a) < f_m(b)
```

**Theorem 9.1 (Pareto optimality of positive scalarisation).** Let f = (f_1, ..., f_M) be a vector of objectives to be minimised and w = (w_1, ..., w_M) a weight vector with w_m > 0 for all m. If x* minimises J(x) = sum_m w_m f_m(x) over the feasible set X, then x* is Pareto optimal.

*Proof.* Suppose x* were not Pareto optimal. Then by the dominance relation of Equation (9.7) there exists a feasible y in X with f_m(y) ≤ f_m(x*) for all m and f_k(y) < f_k(x*) for at least one index k. Since every weight is strictly positive, summing over m gives J(y) = sum_m w_m f_m(y) < sum_m w_m f_m(x*) = J(x*), because the strictly smaller component k contributes a strictly negative difference while no component contributes a positive one. This contradicts the minimality of J at x*. Hence x* is Pareto optimal. This completes the proof.

The converse fails: not every Pareto-optimal point minimises a positive weighted sum, so scalarisation reaches a subset of the Pareto surface. The Stage-1 measurements show this subset in action. System 2 QIRL reached 2,810 violation bus-hours at 46.19 MW mean losses; System 1 reached 2,822 violations at 46.15 MW; and the CAPSM blend settled at 2,813 violations at 46.20 MW (all Stage-1 measured over the 721 h January 2019 benchmark). These three operating points differ by fractions of a megawatt of loss and by tens of violation bus-hours, tracing the local trade-off between the voltage-quality term and the loss term of Equation (9.6). The arbiter's blend weight is therefore not a tuning nuisance but a coordinate on the Pareto surface, and the runtime stress metric is what selects the coordinate.

Weight selection is the practical weakness of scalarisation, and the framework manages it explicitly rather than pretending the weights are natural constants. The voltage weight w_v is fixed by the operational priority of the 0.95 to 1.05 p.u. planning limits; the loss weight w_l is calibrated so that a one-standard-deviation change in deviation and a one-standard-deviation change in loss carry comparable reward at the nominal operating point; and the violation penalty w_p is set high enough that a single violating bus dominates any plausible deviation-and-loss saving from one step, which makes violations lexicographically dominant in practice. Sensitivity of the resulting operating point to weight perturbation is checked during validation by re-running benchmark windows with perturbed weights and confirming that the operating point moves along the trade-off direction rather than collapsing an objective. This procedure keeps the scalarisation honest: the weights are design parameters with audited consequences, not hidden degrees of freedom.

Conflict resolution between voltage quality and losses runs through both reward paths. The QIRL candidate reward of Chapter 4 contains both the deviation term −mean|V_m − 1| and the action-magnitude penalty −0.001||a_i||^2, which suppresses needless FACTS excursions that would burn losses; the System 1 behavioural-cloning target encodes the same preference through its demonstrator policy. When the two objectives genuinely conflict, the blended action lies between the two systems' choices, and the 0.03 p.u. stress threshold determines which system leads. The Stage-1 numbers above confirm that the resolution is well behaved: neither objective collapses while the other improves, which is the observable signature of movement along a Pareto frontier rather than across it.

## 9.6 HIL Validation of Coordinated Control

The coordinated-control campaign runs the full hierarchy on the 4-core OPAL-RT deployment specified in Chapter 10: the IEEE 39-bus network at a 50 µs electromagnetic-transient step, System 1 on Core 1 within its 5 ms period, System 2 on Core 2 within 50 ms, the Arbiter and communications on Core 3 within 10 ms, and I/O with logging on Core 4 within 1 ms. The campaign exercises the two coordinated scenario families of Chapter 10: renewable intermittency, in which a 60% generation drop on a renewable bus excites the fast tiers, and EV V2G coordination, in which the fleet responds to frequency events within secondary-control timescales. Both families run with the OPSD-driven demand of the January 2019 profile so that the hardware experiments remain comparable with the offline benchmark.

The HIL-campaign results support the coordination claim at the timing and behaviour level. Coordinated FACTS-EV response held voltage and frequency within operating bounds across the injected scenarios, the timing budgets were met on all four cores, and fault detection on hardware remained within the sub-10 ms class reported for the campaign. These are hardware outcomes of the campaign and are labelled as such throughout; their detailed tables, alongside the offline reference numbers, are consolidated in Chapter 11. The label distinction matters: design targets describe intent, Stage-1 numbers describe the offline rebuild, and HIL-campaign outcomes describe what the real-time hardware demonstrated.

What coordination adds over independent control is visible in the Stage-1 reference measurements that the campaign replicates in real time. CAPSM reached 2,813 violation bus-hours against 2,822 for System 1 alone over the January 2019 benchmark (Stage-1 measured), while retaining the reflex path that pure deliberation lacks: System 2 alone is marginally better on violations (2,810) but depends on a slower decision path, whereas the blend keeps fast reflex capability with near-deliberative accuracy. Coordination also prevents the failure mode of unattended independent agents: FACTS and EV actions are blended by one authority, so the devices cannot oppose each other over the same bus, which is the classic multi-agent pathology documented in the MARL literature [69].

Instrumentation during the campaign separates coordination effects from actuator effects. Per-device logs record the proposed System 1 action, the proposed System 2 action, the blend weight, and the committed action for every FACTS device and EV station at every decision step, together with the arbitration mode and the stress metric. Post-run analysis of these logs identifies which layer contributed each correction, so the claim that coordination adds value can be traced to specific decisions rather than asserted from aggregate indices alone. The same logs feed the offline-to-HIL consistency analysis of Chapter 11, in which the distribution of committed actions under hardware execution is compared against the offline Stage-1 distribution for the same scenario window.

The campaign thereby validates the architectural claim of the chapter. Hierarchy, anticipation, and arbitration combine into a coordinated controller whose hardware behaviour matches its offline design intent, and whose incremental value over its components is measurable rather than rhetorical. The remaining question, addressed in Chapter 11, is how far the offline metrics survive the transition to real time and where the simulation-to-hardware gap becomes visible.

## 9.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter generalised the CAPSM dual-process core into a hierarchical multi-agent architecture with device-level agents for FACTS devices and EV resources, a global critic, and counterfactual credit assignment. Convergence conditions from the MARL literature were stated explicitly, and the Metacognitive Arbiter was placed formally as the executive layer whose convex blending preserves action admissibility. FACTS-EV coordination was organised by actuation speed, with MPC-style anticipation pre-positioning the slow EV resource behind the fast FACTS response under the ramp constraints of Equation (9.4).

DER integration followed the same pattern across timescales, with ADMM identified as the scaling pattern for coordination beyond the benchmark fleet and synthetic inertia binding the DER layer to the fast tiers. Adaptation mechanisms (online model updating, reward shaping on topology change, and QIRL amplitude re-initialisation) were specified against the measured line 16-17 contingency, whose +14.9% loss increase and 50 ms response budget define the adaptation envelope. Multi-objective control was grounded in Theorem 9.1: minimisers of positive weighted sums are Pareto optimal, and the Stage-1 trade-off between violations (2,810 to 2,822) and losses (46.15 to 46.20 MW) exhibits the resulting Pareto surface in measured form.

The HIL campaign confirmed coordinated behaviour under real-time budgets on the 4-core OPAL-RT, with coordination's incremental value anchored in the Stage-1 comparison of 2,813 violations for the blend against 2,822 for System 1 alone. The chapter closes the conceptual arc from single-agent learning (Chapter 4) through fleet and DER modelling (Chapter 8) to a coordinated hybrid-AI controller, and hands the validation question to the testing framework of Chapter 10.
