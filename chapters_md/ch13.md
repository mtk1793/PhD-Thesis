# Chapter 13: Conclusions and Future Work

This thesis set out to close the distance between learning-based control theory and deployable power-system practice, and it did so by building a brain-inspired dual-process controller, validating it against real operational data, and then subjecting it to controller-hardware-in-the-loop testing on a four-core real-time simulator. This chapter draws the work together. Section 13.1 restates the five research gaps of Chapter 1 and summarises how each was addressed, with the key measured numbers. Section 13.2 reviews the nine original contributions against the objectives they served. Section 13.3 acknowledges the limitations honestly, Section 13.4 charts the future research directions that the limitations and the architecture jointly suggest, Section 13.5 sets out an industry application roadmap, and Section 13.6 closes.

## 13.1 Summary of Key Findings

The five gaps identified in Chapter 1 structured the research programme, and each now has a concrete answer. Against Gap 1, the absence of control architectures adequate for heterogeneous, converter-rich systems, the thesis formulated the CAPSM layered architecture, in which a reflexive path, a deliberative path, and a metacognitive arbiter share one action space spanning FACTS devices and fleet resources [16], [17]. Against Gap 2, limited real-time decision-making under uncertainty, the thesis built the two processing paths with explicitly separated timescales and demonstrated that the blended controller executes in 36.5 ms per step offline while every hardware component meets its real-time period with margin (representative HIL-campaign result). Against Gap 3, insufficient coordination between FACTS devices and distributed resources, the shared nine-dimensional action space made coordination structural rather than added on, and the benchmark showed what coordination buys: the learning-based controllers reduced violation bus-hours by an order of magnitude more than local control. Against Gap 4, insufficient fault detection in resource-rich grids, the multi-modal fusion chain reached 95.8% accuracy on hardware with sub-10 ms detection (HIL campaign). Against Gap 5, the persistent theory-to-practice divide, the thesis made hardware validation an explicit contribution, with the timing, resilience, and consistency evidence of Sections 11.5 to 11.10.

The key measured numbers carry the summary. On the January 2019 IEEE 39-bus benchmark over 721 hourly steps, the blended CAPSM controller reduced voltage violations by 1.20% (2847 to 2813 bus-hours) and System 2 alone by 1.30% (2810 bus-hours), against 0.04% for droop and 0.18% for PI control, all measured in the Stage-1 rebuild, with the paired-difference analysis indicating significance at the 1% level for the learning-based reductions. Every controller converged on every hourly power flow, 721 of 721, and the same 100% convergence held across the IEEE 9, 14, 39, and 118 systems. The full test suite passes 52 of 52 checks. On hardware, every timing budget passed with margin, sub-10 ms fault detection was achieved, the V2G fleet met all five response targets, and the four tested attack scenarios were detected at rates between 93.8% and 98.2% with recovery inside 60 ms (all HIL-campaign results). Figure 13.1 restates the architecture whose components produced these numbers, and it is worth reading once more with the results attached to each block.

![Figure 13.1: The CAPSM dual-process control architecture with the validated components marked (reused from Chapter 1).](figures/fig_capsm_architecture.png)

Two interpretive points complete the summary. First, the improvement figures are placement-constrained: the violation cluster at buses 22 to 29 lies outside the electrical reach of the thesis-specified devices, so the measured percentages quantify the controller under an unfavourable placement and understate the method's potential, a point Section 13.3 returns to. Second, the dual-process design earned its complexity: the arbiter routed most of the benchmark month to the deliberative path while preserving the reflex path for transients, CAPSM tracked System 2's performance closely, and the hardware campaign showed both paths meeting their periods simultaneously. The architecture question posed in Chapter 1, whether inspiration from dual-process cognition [18] could be turned into a verifiable control structure, therefore receives an affirmative answer with numbers attached.

And contribution C1, though the least glamorous, is the load-bearing one, because the action-box closure and the arbitration equation are what allowed every other component to be verified, timed, and blended without a single infeasible command in 721 offline steps and the entire hardware campaign.

## 13.3 Research Limitations

The limitations are acknowledged in Table 13.2 in the same disciplined form used throughout the thesis, each with its category and its mitigation path. Six limitations dominate.

**Table 13.2: Research limitations and their mitigation paths.**

| Limitation | Category | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| FACTS placement constrains the reachable improvement (buses 22 to 29) | technical | placement re-optimisation per Chapter 5; additional devices at the violation cluster |
| Quasi-static renewable and fleet models in the offline stage | modelling | EMT-level validation at 50 µs in the HIL campaign; planned full EMT offline studies |
| Single-season offline evaluation (January 2019 only) | data | extension to the full 2015 to 2020 splits across seasons and extreme events |
| No learned dynamics model; QIRL plans one step ahead | algorithmic | model-based QIRL using the reserved planning horizon of six steps |
| Single-area scope of the validated deployment | scale | multi-area coordination through distributed optimisation |
| Python runtime overhead in the offline pipeline | computational | export of the reflex network to ONNX and FPGA-class targets |

The first limitation is the one that most shapes the headline numbers, and it is worth restating why it is reported as a finding rather than hidden. The 1.20% reduction was measured under a placement whose devices cannot reach the violation cluster, so the number is a floor set by the study design, and the mitigation, re-optimising placement with the Chapter 5 machinery, is a bounded engineering task rather than an open research problem. The second and third limitations bound the external validity of the offline evidence: quasi-static models and one winter month support conclusions about energy and voltage management but not about fast dynamics or seasonal generality, and the honest scope of the thesis's claims stops at that boundary, with the HIL campaign covering the dynamics dimension at the model fidelity available.

The remaining three limitations are of a different character because their mitigations are already designed in. The reserved planning horizon of the QIRL, the distributed-optimisation extension point in the layered architecture, and the ONNX export path for the reflex network are all seams deliberately left in the design, so the limitations mark not weaknesses discovered after the fact but the frontier of a framework built to grow.

One further qualification sits outside Table 13.2 because it concerns the evidence platform rather than the framework. The campaign was conducted on one real-time platform, the OPAL-RT family [56], with the IEEE 39-bus model derived from the EPFL implementation [57], and although RT-LAB projects are portable, the measured timings, the core partition, and the quantisation characteristics are properties of that platform and model rather than of CAPSM. Reproducing the campaign on an alternative real-time target, or against a network model with different parameter provenance, would strengthen the timing and consistency findings the same way the multi-system offline study strengthened the convergence finding. The mitigation is procedural rather than scientific, since the campaign protocol, the model source, and the configuration are documented for exactly this purpose, but until a second platform is exercised the hardware conclusions carry a single-platform qualifier.

Stated plainly, the thesis validates a complete dual-process controller on one system, one season, and one hardware target, and it builds everything else, the action space, the arbitration, the twin, the tests, so that the next system, season, and target require configuration rather than reinvention.

## 13.4 Future Research Directions

The first direction is the completion of genuine multi-step planning in the deliberative layer. The QIRL currently evaluates candidates against the present power flow state, and the planning horizon of six steps is reserved but unused; attaching a learned dynamics model that rolls candidate action sequences forward would convert single-step coordination into anticipatory control, which matters most ahead of the large ramps that the data foundation shows the German system produces. A physics-informed dynamics model in the spirit of the PINN literature [31] is the natural candidate, because it would carry the power-flow structure into the learned model and keep the rollout physically consistent. The reward machinery, amplitude reinforcement, and Born-rule selection would then operate over trajectories rather than states, with the same closed-form updates and the same verifiable normalisation proved in Chapter 4.

The second direction is scale, in both the multi-area and the distributed sense. The centralised arbiter is a scalability boundary, and the alternating-direction method of multipliers offers the standard route to multi-area coordination with limited information exchange [44]; the natural design partitions the network into areas, gives each area its own dual-process controller, and coordinates through the ADMM consensus variables at the tie-lines, with the arbiter retaining its role inside each area. A distributed arbiter is the companion idea: rather than one blending authority, each area's metacognitive layer negotiates its blend with its neighbours, which would let the reflex and deliberative balance differ by region and by stress level. The multi-agent literature [69] supplies both the promise and the caution for this direction, since convergence conditions for distributed learners are exacting, and the framework's structural safety properties would need to be re-proved per area rather than assumed.

The third direction concerns the learning architectures themselves. The reflex path's attention pooling is a natural insertion point for transformer-style attention [34], whose long-range temporal context would let the reflex layer track slower pre-conditions such as sustained high renewable output; the cost is inference time, and the hardware budget of 5 ms is the constraint that any replacement must respect. Graph neural networks are the complementary structural direction, because the state encoder's 160 flat features discard the topology that a graph representation preserves, and a message-passing encoder would make the reflex path transferable across networks with different bus counts without re-encoding. Both directions should be pursued under the discipline the thesis established: any new reflex architecture must pass the same 52-test-class suite, meet the same timing budget on the same target, and be validated through the same campaign methodology before its offline gains are credited.

The fourth direction extends the validation envelope itself. The offline evaluation should cover the full 2015 to 2020 data foundation, across seasons and the largest recorded ramps, so that the controller's evaluation record matches the variability of the data it will face; the hardware campaign should grow a systematic critical-clearing-time study with varied fault locations and clearing instants, converting the design target of Section 11.7 into a measured statistic; and the cyber-resilience work should move from the four tested injection patterns toward an adaptive adversary that knows the detector's structure, since Table 11.10's scenarios were fixed rather than adversarially designed. Each of these extensions uses machinery the thesis already built, which is the practical test of whether a research programme has been constructed to continue.

## 13.5 Industry Application Roadmap

The transition from validated research to operating infrastructure proceeds through a staged roadmap, summarised in Table 13.3 and visualised in Figure 13.2. The staging follows the deployment logic argued in Section 12.3: authority is granted in steps, and each step's evidence gate is the campaign-class demonstration that the previous stage performed as designed under live conditions.

**Table 13.3: Four-phase industry application roadmap.**

| Phase | Years | Mode | Key milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | 1 to 2 | Advisory | shadow-mode operation in a control centre; operator dashboards; auditable advisory record; trust and procedure building |
| Phase 2 | 2 to 3 | Limited closed-loop | authority over one bounded subsystem with operator override; expanded HIL evidence package; first regulator engagement |
| Phase 3 | 3 to 5 | Integrated | full closed-loop FACTS and fleet coordination; certification submission built on the campaign evidence chain; seasonal evaluation completed |
| Phase 4 | 5 and beyond | Advanced | multi-area rollout with distributed arbitration; learned dynamics planning active; market-facing fleet services |

![Figure 13.2: Future research directions and the staged industry roadmap by time horizon.](figures/fig_future_roadmap.png)

The phases differ in what they prove, and the proof burden is deliberately asymmetric. Phase 1 costs the utility almost nothing in risk, since the controller holds no authority, and it buys the one asset that no offline study can produce: a live-environment record of the controller's advice against the operators' actions. Phase 2 is the decisive gate, because limited closed-loop authority is where the HIL evidence, the timing budgets, the Safe Mode behaviour, and the override protocol meet operational reality for the first time, and the scope is deliberately narrow so that any surprise is contained. Phase 3 carries the certification burden, and its milestones are evidence milestones rather than capability milestones, since the capability will already exist. Phase 4 returns to research territory, activating the multi-area and learned-dynamics directions of Section 13.4 in production, and its timeline is stated as five years and beyond precisely because it depends on the regulatory pathway matured in Phase 3.

Two supporting observations complete the roadmap. The economic design study quoted in Section 11.11, with its 12.4% annual savings, $378.6M net present value, 83.7% internal rate of return, and 1.7-year payback (design target, economic design study on the IEEE 118-bus system), makes the business case for starting Phase 1 at all, since even substantial discounts for unmeasured assumptions leave the advisory phase inexpensive relative to the indicated value. And the workforce dimension should not be understated: each phase trains the operators, protection engineers, and regulators who will own the system afterwards, and the roadmap's pace is calibrated as much to institutional learning as to technical readiness.

## 13.6 Concluding Remarks

This thesis began with an observation about the field and a hypothesis about how to answer it. The observation was that learning-based control of power systems had produced a large literature and almost no hardware evidence, so that the discipline's claims stopped where deployment questions began. The hypothesis was that a control architecture organised by dual-process cognition, with a fast learned reflex, a slower verifiable deliberation, and a metacognitive blend between them, could be specified, implemented, and validated to the standard that critical infrastructure demands. The work reported here supports the hypothesis to the extent that the evidence reaches: the architecture was built and tested end to end, the offline benchmark distinguished it from traditional control with statistical discipline, and the hardware campaign met every timing budget, detected faults in under 10 ms, regulated a fleet against frequency events, survived the tested attacks, and quantified its own distance from the offline model.

The deeper contribution, and the one most likely to outlast the specific numbers, is the validation culture in which those numbers were produced. Every quantity in this thesis carries a label stating where it came from, every result regenerates from pinned data and versioned code, every requirement descends to a test in a 52-check suite, and the hardware campaign reports not only what passed but what changed on hardware and why. This culture is transferable to any controller, learned or otherwise, and the thesis commends it to the field as the practical bridge over the theory-to-practice gap that motivated the work. Learning-based control will earn its place in power systems in exactly the measure that its claims are measurable, and the machinery for making them measurable now exists and has been demonstrated.

The remaining work is substantial and clearly marked. Placement optimisation, seasonal evaluation, learned dynamics planning, multi-area coordination, and an adversarial resilience study are the five extensions that would convert a validated prototype into a deployable product family, and each extends the present design rather than replacing it. That is the intended final image of this thesis: not a finished controller, but a framework whose limitations are seams, whose evidence is auditable, and whose next steps are engineering rather than speculation. The gap this thesis set out to close has been crossed for one controller on one target; the crossing is now a method, and methods scale.
