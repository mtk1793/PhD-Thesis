# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Evolution of AI Applications in Power Systems

Artificial intelligence entered power engineering well before the current deep learning wave, and the history of that entry conditions what the field expects of AI today. In the 1980s, expert systems encoded the diagnostic knowledge of experienced operators into rule bases; the DELTA system, developed for alarm processing and fault diagnosis, became the emblematic example of the genre. Early artificial neural network work followed in 1989, when dynamic security assessment was among the first power-system tasks delegated to a learned classifier, anticipating by decades the modern fashion for machine-learning security screens. These first systems demonstrated genuine value in narrow tasks, yet they also exposed the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck, since every rule had to be elicited from a human expert, and the brittleness of rule bases when operating conditions drifted outside the envelope their authors had imagined.

A second generation of methods softened the rigid rule-based paradigm. Fuzzy inference allowed control actions to be graded rather than binary, reflecting the position, argued influentially in the cognitive-computing literature, that intelligent behaviour may emerge from many simple interacting processes rather than from a single formal logic core [20]. Evolutionary computation supplied search where gradients were unavailable, with genetic algorithms formalised in the foundational texts of Holland and Goldberg [64, 65], and particle swarm optimisation added a socially inspired optimiser that became a workhorse for tuning and placement problems [66]. These methods spread across network reconfiguration, capacitor placement, controller tuning, and unit commitment support. Their limitation, apparent already in the 2000s, was computational cost and their orientation toward offline planning rather than online control. Hybridisation, in retrospect, began in this period, as authors combined fuzzy rules with evolutionary tuning and neural predictors with expert-system wrappers.

Two lessons from this middle period deserve preservation. First, search-based and rule-based methods did not disappear when learning methods arrived; they migrated into preprocessing and postprocessing roles where their transparency is an asset, and they still back much of the planning software in daily industrial use. Second, the community learned that benchmark difficulty matters more than algorithmic elegance, because methods that impressed on toy networks frequently failed to survive contact with full-scale cases. Both lessons inform the design choices of later chapters, in which search, learning, and physics are assigned to the roles they perform best.

The modern machine-learning assessment literature grew out of better data rather than better rules. Phasor measurement units, smart meters, and open datasets gave classifiers and regressors training material of a scale the 1990s could not imagine, and transient stability assessment became a benchmark task for big-data methods, with core vector machines and related techniques achieving high screening accuracy at scale [87]. Comparable results were reported for real-time dynamic security assessment with reliable intelligent systems that combine learning with decision logic suited to operational deployment [88]. The caveat that accompanies these successes is that the models are trained offline against simulations of a particular network, and their accuracy claims degrade, sometimes silently, when topology or generation mix drifts from the training distribution.

The deep reinforcement learning (DRL) era then changed the ambition level from assessment to control. Deep Q-networks learned control policies directly from high-dimensional observations in Atari environments [23], continuous-control algorithms such as DDPG extended the approach to action spaces more like those of a power grid [24], twin-delayed methods corrected the overestimation pathologies of actor-critic training [25], and self-play systems demonstrated superhuman performance in games of perfect information [26]. Reviews of the field catalogued a rapid migration of these techniques into power-system control problems, from frequency regulation to optimal power flow assistance [27, 28]. ## 2.2 FACTS Devices and Their Applications

The FACTS concept, as consolidated by Hingorani and Gyugyi, classifies power-electronic transmission controllers by where and how they connect to the alternating current network [4]. Shunt devices inject or absorb reactive current at a bus, series devices modulate the effective impedance of a line, and combined devices couple a shunt and a series element through a common direct current link to control both bus voltage and power flow. Figure 2.2 presents this classification as a tree, with the principal devices of each family named. The classification matters for control design because the three families act on different physical quantities, on different timescales, and with different interaction risks, so a coordinator that treats them as interchangeable will misapply them.

![Figure 2.2: Classification of FACTS devices and their operating principles.](figures/fig_facts_classification.png)

Shunt compensation begins with the static var compensator, which switches thyristor-controlled reactors and capacitor banks to hold a target voltage, and continues with the static synchronous compensator, which synthesises reactive current from a voltage-source converter without passive var banks. The STATCOM responds faster than the SVC and maintains its characteristic at depressed voltages, which is why modern grid codes prefer it for weak nodes; both devices are modelled in power flow as controllable shunt susceptances, and the monographs of Acha et al. and Zhang et al. give the steady-state and dynamic formulations used throughout this thesis [5, 6]. Series compensation begins with the thyristor-controlled series capacitor, whose effective reactance follows the compensation law used in Chapter 3, and whose controller design must respect the stability limits analysed by Del Rosso, Cañizares, and Doña [7]. The static synchronous series compensator (SSSC) performs the same duty with a converter-sourced series voltage rather than a passive capacitor. The unified power flow controller combines a shunt and a series converter and is the most capable device of the family, controlling voltage, active flow, and reactive flow simultaneously, at the price of the highest cost and the most involved modelling [4, 5].

Applications of FACTS in the renewable era extend beyond the classical voltage-support duty [50]. Wind-rich and solar-rich corridors exhibit flows that swing with weather, so series devices find a new role in flow steering, and shunt devices in smoothing the voltage excursions that variable injection causes at electrically remote buses [50]. The devices also provide damping of electromechanical oscillations through supplementary modulation controllers, a function that remains valuable as synchronous inertia declines. What the literature has largely not provided, and what this thesis argues is now needed, is an account of FACTS operation coordinated with DER assets under one controller, rather than as a separately tuned layer. The benchmark network of this thesis therefore uses one device from each family so that the coordination question is posed across the full classification of Figure 2.2: an SVC at bus 14 with a susceptance range of ±0.5 p.u., a STATCOM at bus 39 with a range of ±1.0 p.u., a TCSC on the branch between buses 16 and 17 with an operational compensation degree in [0.3, 0.7], and a UPFC at bus 26 combining a shunt element of ±0.5 p.u. with a series element acting on the branch between buses 26 and 28. These placements follow the convention of the test-system literature in stressing the corridor that carries the heaviest transfer. In power flow terms the shunt devices appear as controllable susceptances and the series devices as reactance or injected-power modifications, which is exactly the level of fidelity at which the controllers of Chapters 3 and 4 operate.

Table 2.2 summarises the five principal devices with their type, defining feature, typical application, and response order.

**Table 2.2: Comparison of FACTS device characteristics and applications.**

| Device | Type | Key feature | Typical application | Response time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SVC | Shunt | Thyristor-switched reactor and capacitor banks | Voltage support at transmission buses | Of the order of 20–40 ms |
| STATCOM | Shunt | Voltage-source converter synthesising reactive current | Fast voltage support in weak grids | Of the order of 5–20 ms |
| TCSC | Series | Controllable series compensation degree | Power flow control and oscillation damping | Of the order of 10–100 ms |

The response orders in Table 2.2 are reported in the FACTS literature as typical values and should be read as indicative rather than as guaranteed figures for any specific installation [4, 6]. They nonetheless make the control-relevant point that FACTS devices are an order of magnitude faster than dispatch-level assets such as EV fleets, which is why any coordinating architecture must carry an explicit notion of timescale.

## 2.3 AI Approaches for Decision-Making in Power Grids

Reinforcement learning frames sequential decision-making as an agent maximising accumulated reward through interaction with an environment, and its theoretical foundations are established in the standard text of Sutton and Barto [22]. A power network fits the frame naturally: the state is the operating point, actions are setpoints for FACTS devices and EV stations, and the reward encodes voltage deviation, losses, and constraint violations. The appeal of the frame is that a controller can, in principle, be learned from data rather than designed from a model. The appeal is also the danger, because everything depends on how the interaction data are obtained and what the learned policy does in states the training never visited.

Deep reinforcement learning made the frame scalable to high-dimensional states. The deep Q-network established that a convolutional agent could learn competent policies from raw observations [23], DDPG brought deterministic policy gradients to continuous action spaces of the kind a power grid presents [24], and TD3 addressed the value-overestimation that destabilises actor-critic training on continuous problems [25], while the self-play results of AlphaGo Zero demonstrated the raw capability of learned search [26]. Power-system applications quickly followed, and the surveys by Glavic and by Cao et al. document their spread across frequency control, voltage regulation, and energy scheduling, alongside the recurring caveats of sample inefficiency, reward shaping sensitivity, and the difficulty of certifying learned policies [27, 28]. A further practical limitation receives less attention than it deserves: most DRL controllers are trained against a fixed simulated environment, so their competence is conditioned on a topology and resource mix that real networks no longer hold constant.

Safe reinforcement learning responds directly to the constraint-violation risk. Constrained policy optimisation restricts policy updates to regions where expected constraint costs remain within bounds [29], and Lyapunov-based methods provide per-state safety certificates that keep the learning trajectory inside a certified set [30]. These methods reduce the frequency of dangerous exploration, but they rest on constraint models that are themselves approximations, and they typically trade off learning speed against conservatism. For an operator, the residual risk remains difficult to quantify, which motivates embedding physical law directly in the learner.

Model predictive control (MPC) is the classical counterpoint, optimising over a receding horizon with constraints stated explicitly in the formulation [67, 68]. Its strengths are transparency and constraint handling, and its weaknesses are model dependence and the computational cost of nonconvex alternating current formulations at fast rates. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) take a different route to physics, embedding the governing equations in the training loss so that predictions violate the physics only to the extent that the loss permits [31]. In power-system dress, PINN-style training promises constraint compliance by construction, and it reduces the data hunger of purely data-driven models, although training cost and behaviour outside the training envelope remain open questions.

Between the learning-based and model-based families sits imitation, which trains a controller to reproduce a demonstrator and thereby inherits whatever safety the demonstrator possesses while remaining fast at execution. Behaviour cloning reduces imitation to supervised learning and is attractively simple, but it suffers from distribution shift, since the learned policy eventually encounters states the demonstrator would never have visited; dataset aggregation addresses the shift by querying the demonstrator on the states the learner actually reaches [112]. Sequence models supply the architectural substrate for such controllers, with long short-term memory networks carrying state across observations [32] and attention mechanisms allowing a policy to weight the parts of a long history that matter for the present decision [34]. These ingredients matter to this thesis because its reflexive path is precisely a cloned, attention-equipped sequence model, and the limitation identified here, namely the dependence on demonstrator quality, motivates the deliberative layer that surrounds it.

Machine-learning stability assessment, finally, offers speed where model-based screening is too slow, with core vector machines and intelligent assessment systems achieving high accuracy on transient stability screening [87, 88]; their limitation is the same distribution-shift exposure noted above, since a classifier trained on one topology is not, by itself, a monitor of another.

The synthesis this chapter draws from these families is that each is incomplete alone: DRL adapts but violates, safe RL protects but constrains, MPC respects physics but needs models and time, PINNs respect physics but must still be validated, and learned assessors are fast but fragile under drift. This pattern motivates an architecture that combines families under an explicit arbiter rather than expecting one method to carry the whole control problem.

## 2.4 EV and V2G/G2V Architectures

The foundational V2G analysis of Kempton and Tomić established the two questions that still organise the field: how much power and energy does a connected fleet represent, and who would pay for its services [8, 9]. The first question has an encouraging answer, since even a modest fraction of the light-duty fleet, if connected through bidirectional chargers, represents gigawatts of fast reserve; the second is subtler, because revenue must cover battery wear and the inconvenience cost of scheduling. The companion implementation analysis concluded that frequency regulation and spinning reserve, rather than bulk energy arbitrage, are the services where V2G economics are most favourable [9]. These conclusions have aged well, and they explain why aggregation sits at the centre of every practical V2G architecture.

Charging strategy research then addressed how a fleet should behave once connected. Sortomme and El-Sharkawi compared charging objectives for unidirectional V2G and showed that schedules minimising cost, emissions, or load variance differ in character but that all managed charging outperforms uncontrolled charging [10]. The fleet-management review of Hu et al. systematised the services an aggregator can sell, from regulation to congestion management, and the optimisation and control structures that deliver them [11]. On the physical layer, Yilmaz and Krein classified charger topologies and power levels, distinguishing single-stage and two-stage bidirectional converters and the galvanic isolation choices that separate them [12]. Beyond the bulk grid, Liu, Chau, Wu, and Gao mapped the vehicle-to-home, vehicle-to-vehicle, and vehicle-to-grid space, showing that the same hardware serves different value streams depending on where the energy is directed [13].

Two further literatures complete the picture. The first is aggregation and scheduling under uncertainty: state-estimation-based control of large populations of flexible loads, developed initially for thermostatic devices, provides the mathematical machinery for commanding thousands of private batteries from a single signal [82], and decentralised control schemes for thermostatic loads establish what can be achieved without full telemetry [83]. The same machinery transfers to EV fleets with the modification that mobility makes availability stochastic. The second is degradation: operating a battery harder shortens its life, and degradation-aware optimisation of storage operation attaches an explicit cost to cycling so that schedules weigh revenue against wear [84]. The unmanaged alternative is unattractive on the grid side as well, since residential studies show that coincident charging can breach feeder limits [14, 15]. Deployment experience has accompanied the maturation of this literature, though it remains uneven. Bidirectional charging requires grid codes that permit export, metering that settles it, and interconnection agreements that define who may command the fleet, and several jurisdictions have amended all three to open the door to V2H and V2G pilots. Early commercial experience suggests that the value stack of an electrified fleet is governed more by availability than by raw capacity, since vehicles are parked and pliable for most of the day but cannot be commanded while driving. It appears that the practical constraint on V2G is institutional more than technical, a conclusion that shifts attention from converter design, which the literature has largely settled [12], to the coordination layer this thesis addresses. ## 2.5 Brain-Inspired AI Frameworks

Cognitive psychology offers an account of human decision-making that has proven unexpectedly fertile for control engineering. Kahneman's dual-process theory distinguishes fast, automatic, low-effort System 1 thinking from slow, deliberate, effortful System 2 reasoning [16], and the distinction has since been sharpened by formal analysis of how the two processes interact and override one another [17]. The conditions under which intuition serves an expert well have also been studied: Kahneman and Klein argue that intuitive expertise is trustworthy only in environments that are sufficiently regular to be learned and that provide prompt, clear feedback, a description that fits steady-state power system operation better than it fits rare catastrophic events [18]. The dual-process framing is not without internal debate, and the debate is instructive for engineers who borrow its vocabulary. Evans and Stanovich distinguish default-interventionist readings, in which fast intuition answers first and slow reasoning intervenes when needed, from parallel-competitive readings, in which both processes answer independently and a conflict is resolved afterwards [17]. The two readings imply different arbitration designs: the first suggests a supervisor that monitors the reflex answer and vetoes it, while the second suggests a weighting mechanism that fuses both answers continuously. This thesis adopts a design compatible with both readings, in that its arbiter can both override and blend, and Chapter 4 shows that the blended form subsumes the override form at its limits. This body of theory matters here because it supplies the division of labour that an autonomous grid controller needs: reflexes for the regular, deliberation for the rare, and a supervisory judgement about which mode applies.

A second strand of brain-inspired computing concerns emotion and motivation as control signals rather than as noise. Picard's affective computing programme treats emotional state as information that modulates cognition and priorities [19], and Minsky's emotion machine proposes that minds operate as a society of simpler processes whose activation is governed by cascades of affective and metacognitive labels [20]. Lake, Ullman, Tenenbaum, and Gershman distil what learning systems would need in order to build theories like people do, listing compositionality, causality, and learning-to-learn among the core ingredients [21]. For grid control, the practical import of this strand is that a controller benefits from an explicit, self-monitoring layer that labels its own operating condition, stressed or calm, novel or familiar, and adjusts its reliance on fast versus slow processing accordingly.

Cognitive architectures translate these ideas into engineered systems. In the control reading adopted by this thesis, a reflex layer implements System 1 as a fast neural mapping from observations to actions; a deliberative layer implements System 2 as candidate generation and evaluation over a slower horizon; and a metacognitive layer implements the supervisory judgement as an arbitration between the two, with an explicit safe mode for conditions neither process handles well. This layered reading is a design pattern rather than a simulation of neuroscience, and its value is architectural: it separates concerns that monolithic controllers blur, namely speed, foresight, and supervision. ## 2.6 Assessment of Existing Technologies for DER Integration

The architectures proposed for coordinating DERs fall into four broad families. Centralised schemes collect all measurements at one optimiser, which is conceptually simple and globally optimal when the model is right, but which scales poorly and creates a single point of failure. Hierarchical schemes partition the problem into tiers, with device-level control, regional aggregation, and system-level optimisation, trading a measure of optimality for tractability. Distributed schemes solve the coordination problem through peer-to-peer iteration among neighbouring controllers, with the survey of Molzahn et al. cataloguing the alternating direction method of multipliers and consensus approaches as the principal instruments [44, 46], and peer-to-peer energy trading adds a market dimension to the same technical substrate [47]. The smart grid literature frames all four families as answers to the same question, namely how much coordination to centralise given the communication and computation available [48, 49].

What works, on the evidence assembled above, is aggregation with explicit constraints. Thermostatic load control demonstrated that large populations of flexible devices can be commanded through estimated aggregate state rather than device-by-device telemetry [82, 83], and the same principle underwrites EV fleet scheduling [10, 11]. What works less well is any single-family architecture applied to a mixed-asset network: centralised optimisers stall at the timescale of the slowest asset, and purely local controllers cannot see the interactions that make coordination valuable in the first place. It appears that the practical frontier is a hybrid, hierarchical-distributed arrangement in which fast local control is supervised by slower coordination layers, which is the arrangement this thesis adopts for its benchmark.

Two institutional constructs overlay these technical families. The virtual power plant aggregates geographically dispersed resources into a single schedulable entity, so that a portfolio of PV, storage, and flexible load can bid into markets as one unit, and it depends technically on exactly the aggregation machinery cited above. Transactive energy goes further and embeds coordination in economic signals exchanged among agents, for which the bilateral contract markets studied by Morstyn, Teytelboym, and McCulloch provide one concrete mechanism [47]. Both constructs are, at bottom, coordination architectures, and both inherit the scalability and communication constraints of the family they are built upon, which is why the assessment of Table 2.5 evaluates them implicitly through the DER types they serve.

Assessment by DER type sharpens the conclusion, because different assets stress different parts of an architecture. Solar PV is forecast-limited but predictable at the daylight scale, so it rewards local voltage control and good forecasting. Wind is dominated by ramp behaviour, so it rewards stochastic dispatch and explicit curtailment rules. EV fleets are mobile and user-constrained, so they reward aggregation and degradation-aware scheduling. Battery energy storage is fast but energy-limited, so it rewards services that exploit speed without exhausting energy. Flexible loads are the cheapest flexibility available but are bounded by comfort and by telemetry gaps. ## 2.7 Gap Analysis and Motivation for Hybrid Frameworks

The literature surveyed in Sections 2.1 through 2.6 can now be brought to bear on the five gaps stated in Chapter 1, and the exercise shows that each gap is corroborated from at least two independent directions. Gap 1, the absence of control architectures for heterogeneous systems, is visible both in the DER integration assessment, which found that no single architecture family suits mixed assets, and in the FACTS literature, which coordinates devices among themselves but not with DERs. Gap 2, real-time decision-making under uncertainty, is documented on the algorithmic side by the sample inefficiency and constraint-violation caveats that the DRL surveys attach to their enthusiasm [27, 28], and on the methods side by the conservatism that safe reinforcement learning buys its guarantees with [29, 30]. Gap 3, FACTS–DER coordination, follows from Section 2.2, where devices act on local objectives [4, 6], together with Section 2.4, where fleets are optimised on horizons that ignore network physics [10, 11].

Gap 4, fault detection and localisation in resource-rich environments, is corroborated by the stability-assessment literature, whose offline classifiers degrade under the topology drift that abundant telemetry reveals [87, 88], and by the cyber-physical security literature, which shows that false data injection defeats detectors designed for a smaller observation space [75]. Gap 5, the theory-to-practice divide, is the subject of Section 2.8 and is stated here only in preview: the AI-in-power-systems literature of Table 2.1 ends, in almost every entry, at offline validation. The five gaps are therefore not artefacts of one survey or one viewpoint; they are the consistent residue of two decades of progress that advanced algorithms faster than architectures and validation.

The case for hybrid frameworks rests on complementarity rather than on novelty for its own sake. Complementary strengths argue for combining families whose weaknesses do not coincide: a reflexive neural path supplies speed where optimisation is too slow, a candidate-based deliberative layer supplies foresight where reflexes are myopic, and physics-informed structure supplies constraint discipline where pure data-driven methods wander [31]. Multi-timescale necessity argues for the same combination, since Section 2.2 established that FACTS devices respond in tens of milliseconds while EV dispatch evolves over minutes and hours, so one controller cannot serve both without an explicit notion of timescale. Balanced exploration and exploitation argue for deliberation by candidates, in which a bounded set of alternatives is evaluated against the current network state rather than sampling blindly; the quantum-inspired mechanism adopted in Chapter 3 formalises exactly this balance. Resilience argues for mode arbitration, because a controller that can retreat to a safe mode when stressed degrades gracefully in the way Table 2.4 anticipates. Transitional implementation, finally, argues that a hybrid can be introduced atop existing energy management and distribution management systems, layer by layer, rather than demanding a wholesale replacement of certified infrastructure.

The literature also indicates what a hybrid framework must demonstrate to be credible, and this sets the acceptance criteria for the rest of the thesis. It must show that the reflex path meets a protection-grade timing budget, that the deliberative path converges within a dispatch-relevant budget, that arbitration between them does not oscillate, and that the whole composition respects network constraints at every step. It must, in addition, demonstrate these properties on hardware, which is the requirement that most of the surveyed literature does not meet and that the following section examines in detail.

## 2.8 Real-Time Simulation and HIL Validation in Power Systems

Real-time simulation exists because some questions about control equipment cannot be answered offline. A protective relay, a phasor measurement unit, or a learned controller does not merely compute an answer; it computes an answer within a deadline, from finite-precision inputs, across physical interfaces whose latency and noise are part of the experiment. Real-time digital simulation addresses this by solving the network equations on specialised hardware in lockstep with wall-clock time, so that a device under test can be coupled to a simulated network and behave exactly as it would in the field. The technique was established for relay testing by McLaren et al. with the real-time digital simulator developed for that purpose [53], and real-time PC-based simulation of circuits and drives extended the practice beyond dedicated mainframes [54].

Within the real-time family, the hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) hierarchy distinguishes what is real and what is simulated. Model-in-the-loop (MIL) runs the controller as a model beside the plant model; software-in-the-loop (SIL) runs the controller code on a non-real-time processor; processor-in-the-loop (PIL) runs the code on the target processor with simulated inputs; Controller-HIL (CHIL) couples the full controller hardware to the simulator through signal-level interfaces; and Power-HIL (PHIL) couples actual power through amplifiers, adding the stability and safety questions of the interface itself. The characteristics and design constraints of PHIL, in particular the interface compensation required to keep the coupled system stable, are analysed by Lauss et al., whose treatment also delimits CHIL from PHIL by the level at which the controller is coupled [51]. The broader survey of real-time simulation technologies by Faruque et al. maps the platforms and applications across the hierarchy and remains the standard reference for what each level of the hierarchy can and cannot prove [52].

The OPAL-RT family of simulators is the platform used in this thesis and is representative of the state of the art. Its RT-LAB environment distributes a Simulink model across processing cores that execute in lockstep, with the vendor documentation describing the toolchain and its deployment flow [55, 56]. Electromagnetic transient solvers execute at steps in the tens of microseconds, in this project 50 µs, while phasor-domain solvers execute at millisecond steps, in this project 1 ms, and the two can be coupled where a study needs both detail and reach. Community and academic model repositories supply ready-made networks in this toolchain; the IEEE 39-bus model published by the Distributed Electrical Systems Laboratory at EPFL is the basis of the real-time network used in Chapters 10 and 11 [57]. Communication with the controller under test proceeds through standard protocols, with synchrophasor streaming defined by IEEE C37.118 [89], substation automation by IEC 61850 [91], and time synchronisation by the Precision Time Protocol [92].

Two adjacent literatures show how real-time infrastructure serves research beyond controller testing. Cyber-physical security testbeds couple real-time grid simulation with real network equipment and attack tools, so that intrusions and defences are studied at physically meaningful timescales; the testbed architecture and evaluation methodology of Hahn et al. is the template [76]. Digital twins bind a real-time model to a physical asset over its lifetime, so that monitoring, what-if analysis, and controller updates are performed against a live surrogate; the concept originated in aerospace life-prediction practice [77] and its modelling challenges and enablers are surveyed by Rasheed, San, and Kvamsdal [78]. Both literatures depend on the same substrate this section describes, namely a real-time network model that is faithful enough for the conclusions drawn on it to transfer to the field.

Against this background, the validation practice of the AI-in-power-systems literature stands out by its absence. The survey bodies assembled in Section 2.1 [27, 28] and the method families of Section 2.3 describe training and evaluation in offline simulation almost exclusively: reward curves, violation counts, and accuracy figures are computed in non-real-time Python or MATLAB environments, with solver iteration unconstrained by any deadline. The real-time and HIL literature, conversely, demonstrates exquisite validation rigour for conventional controllers, relays, and protection schemes [51]–[53], but its subjects are rarely learning-based controllers, and when they are, the cognitive layer is usually a single network rather than an architecture with arbitration. Between the two literatures lies a gap that is easy to state and consequential to fill: most AI power-system papers never validate on real-time hardware, and no established practice exists for validating a dual-process cognitive controller whose reflex path, deliberative path, and arbiter each carry distinct timing budgets.

The consequences of skipping real-time validation are not cosmetic, and the literature on each failure mode makes this plain. A controller that meets its inference budget offline may miss the corresponding deadline on hardware once solver coupling, input-output latency, and operating-system jitter are added; a policy trained against a non-real-time environment has never faced the sampling rhythm at which it must act; and an arbitration law tuned offline has never been exercised at the interrupt granularity of a real target. It appears that conclusions about accuracy transfer poorly to conclusions about deployability, and it is deployability that the five gaps of this thesis are ultimately about. The missing link in the literature is therefore real-time hardware validation of cognitive controllers, performed at the same standard of rigour the HIL community applies to relays.

CAPSM's three-stage validation pipeline is designed to close exactly this link, and the design principle is traceability from stage to stage. Stage 1 builds the offline benchmark in Python with PYPOWER quasi-static time series, where algorithms are trained and compared; Stage 2 prepares the Simulink model of the same network, with FACTS and EV subsystems, for the real-time toolchain; Stage 3 executes the framework as Controller-HIL on a four-core OPAL-RT target, with the reflexive path, the deliberative path, and the arbiter assigned to cores and to explicit timing budgets. The progression MIL, SIL, PIL, CHIL is thereby traversed deliberately rather than rhetorically, in the direction recommended by the real-time simulation literature [51, 52], and the offline-to-hardware consistency of the framework is measured rather than assumed. The thesis accordingly treats Stage 3 not as a demonstration but as an experiment with stated acceptance criteria, and Chapter 10 specifies them before Chapter 11 reports whether they were met.

Nothing in this list of acceptance criteria is exotic, which is rather the point. Each criterion is testable with instruments the real-time simulation community already possesses, and none requires that the controller surrender its learning-based character. What has been missing is the joint demonstration, and the framework of the next chapter is constructed so that the demonstration can be staged, budget by budget, through the pipeline described in this section.
