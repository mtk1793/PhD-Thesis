# Chapter 3: Proposed Framework

## 3.1 Framework Overview and Design Philosophy

The preceding chapters established that modern transmission systems must coordinate three physically heterogeneous asset families, namely flexible AC transmission system (FACTS) controllers [4, 5, 6, 7], electric vehicle (EV) fleets with vehicle-to-grid capability, and distributed energy resources (DERs), under operating conditions that change faster than conventional energy management cycles. This chapter presents the Cognitive Adaptive Power System Management (CAPSM) framework, which meets that coordination requirement with a single control architecture inspired by the dual-process theory of human cognition [16, 17, 18]. Rather than forcing one algorithm to serve all timescales and all assets, CAPSM separates the control task into a fast reflexive pathway and a slower deliberative pathway, and it delegates the decision of when to trust each pathway to a third, metacognitive component. The framework is designed from the outset to be deployable on real-time hardware, which distinguishes it from the majority of AI-based power system controllers surveyed in Chapter 2.

The cognitive foundation of CAPSM rests on the observation, due to Kahneman [16] and to Evans and Stanovich [17], that human decision-making combines two complementary processes. System 1 operates quickly, automatically, and with low effort, and it excels in familiar situations where learned patterns map directly onto actions. System 2 operates slowly, deliberately, and with high effort, and it handles novel or demanding situations that require reasoning. Kahneman and Klein further showed that intuitive expertise arises when environments are regular enough for learned patterns to be reliable [18]. Power system operation exhibits precisely this structure: most operating hours are statistically similar and admit fast reflexive voltage support, while contingencies, extreme ramps, and abnormal telemetry demand deliberate coordination. CAPSM transfers this organisation onto the control loop, as we develop in Section 3.2.

Three design principles govern the architecture. The first principle is *cognitive duality*: every control cycle is executed by two parallel controllers, a reflexive System 1 built from a convolutional recurrent network and a deliberative System 2 built from quantum-inspired reinforcement learning (QIRL), with inference budgets below 5 ms and below 50 ms per step respectively. The second principle is *hierarchical coordination*: control authority is distributed across device, regional, and system tiers so that local reflexes never conflict with global objectives, following the layered control philosophy of [36] and [124]. The third principle is *adaptive learning*: the framework improves with experience through behaviour cloning, online amplitude reinforcement, and monitored retraining, in line with the reinforcement learning foundations of [22] and [23].

CAPSM is organised around the five control gaps identified in Chapter 1, and each gap receives a targeted design response. Gap 1, the absence of control architectures suited to heterogeneous assets, is addressed by the shared nine-dimensional action box defined in Section 3.2, which exposes FACTS setpoints and EV fleet power on one common interface. Gap 2, limited real-time decision-making under uncertainty, is addressed by the dual-process pairing of a sub-5 ms neural reflex with a sub-50 ms deliberative optimiser and by the Metacognitive Arbiter that interpolates between them. Gap 3, insufficient coordination between FACTS devices and DERs, is addressed by the three-tier hierarchy of Section 3.3 and by the coordinated control strategies of Section 3.7. Gap 4, insufficient fault detection and localisation in resource-rich environments, is addressed by the multi-modal fusion architecture of Section 3.6. Gap 5, the barrier between theoretical controllers and field practice, is addressed by making the framework hardware-ready from the first design iteration, as specified in Section 3.9 and implemented in Chapter 10.

The high-level organisation of the framework is shown in Figure 3.1, which depicts the observation pipeline, the two control pathways, the arbitration layer, and the actuation interfaces to FACTS devices, EV fleets, and DERs on the IEEE 39-bus test system. The figure also indicates the physical measurements that feed the framework, including phasor measurement unit (PMU) streams and supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) telemetry. All subsequent sections refine the blocks of this figure, and Chapter 4 specifies their implementation in executable detail.

## 3.2 Brain-Inspired Dual-Process Control Architecture

The System 1 controller is the reflexive pathway of CAPSM. It receives a sliding window of encoded system observations and maps that window directly onto continuous setpoints through a convolutional recurrent network with an attention mechanism. Formally, at time step $t$ the controller emits

```eq
%%3.1
u^{(1)} = f_{\theta_1}(x_{t-T+1:\,t})
```

where $f$ denotes the CNN-LSTM network with trainable parameters $\theta_1$, $x$ denotes the encoded system state, and $T = 12$ is the window length in hourly observations. The encoder compresses each observation into 160 features covering bus voltage magnitudes and angles, branch flows, generator outputs, FACTS setpoints, EV fleet states, and aggregate scalars, following the layout specified in Section 4.1. The network architecture and its training procedure are specified in Section 4.2 and Section 4.6, where the long short-term memory (LSTM) formulation of [32] and the attention mechanism of [34] are written out explicitly. The design intent of System 1 is frequency of response rather than depth of reasoning: its inference budget is strictly below 5 ms per step so that it can act within the same order of magnitude as local protection and fast excitation control.

The System 2 controller is the deliberative pathway. Instead of regressing setpoints from learned patterns, it searches the action space for the control vector that minimises an expected closed-loop cost:

```eq
%%3.2
u^{(2)} = \mathrm{argmin}_{a \in \mathcal{A}}\; J(a;\, x_t)
```

where $\mathcal{A}$ denotes the shared action box defined below and $J$ denotes the expected cost of applying action $a$ from state $x_t$. CAPSM instantiates $J$ through the quantum-inspired candidate evaluation of Section 3.5, in which 32 candidate actions are scored in parallel against the current network state. Because the search is performed against the physics of the power flow rather than against a static training set, System 2 requires no offline training data, which makes it immediately transferable to new operating regions, a property that Chapter 2 identified as missing from most deep reinforcement learning approaches [23, 27, 28]. Its inference budget is below 50 ms per step, an order of magnitude slower than System 1 but still compatible with supervisory control cycles.

The Metacognitive Arbiter closes the architecture. It observes the system stress, decides how much authority each pathway deserves, and merges their outputs. The stress metric is the mean absolute voltage deviation

$$c_1 = \mathrm{mean} |V_m - 1.0|$$

evaluated over all buses, where $V_m$ is the vector of bus voltage magnitudes in per unit. The arbitration weight follows a sigmoid law,

```eq
%%3.3
\alpha = \sigma(\tau_{\mathrm{arb}}\,(c_{\mathrm{th}} - c_1)), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
```

with threshold $c_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.03$ p.u. and sharpness $\tau_{\mathrm{arb}} = 50$. The blended command applied to every FACTS scalar and every EV setpoint is then

```eq
%%3.4
u = \alpha\, u^{(1)} + (1 - \alpha)\, u^{(2)}
```

When the system is relaxed, $c_1$ falls below the threshold, $\alpha$ approaches 1, and the reflexive pathway dominates. When the system is stressed, $\alpha$ approaches 0 and the deliberative pathway takes over. In the January 2019 benchmark the mean stress is approximately 0.0286 p.u., which lies just below the threshold and yields a mean arbitration weight near 0.52, so the blended controller tracks the deliberative pathway while retaining the reflex option, as the results in Chapter 11 confirm.

The shared action box $\mathcal{A}$ is the nine-dimensional product of per-asset setpoint intervals listed in Table 4.2: shunt susceptances for the SVC at bus 14 and the STATCOM at bus 39, the series compensation degree of the TCSC on line 16–17, the shunt and series channels of the unified power flow controller (UPFC) at bus 26, and the charge or discharge power of three aggregated EV fleets at buses 3, 8, and 15. All three control paths operate on this common set, and the following proposition records the geometric property that makes their combination safe.

**Proposition 3.1 (Action-box convexity and closure under CAPSM blending).**
*Statement.* Let $\mathcal{A} = \prod_{i=1}^{9} [\mathrm{lo}_i, \mathrm{hi}_i]$ be the action box with lower vector $\mathrm{lo} = (-0.5, -1.0, 0.3, -0.5, -0.5, -0.5, -50, -50, -50)$ and upper vector $\mathrm{hi} = (0.5, 1.0, 0.7, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 50, 50, 50)$ in their respective units. Then $\mathcal{A}$ is convex, and for every blending weight $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ and every pair $u^{(1)}, u^{(2)} \in \mathcal{A}$, the blended command of Equation (3.4) satisfies $u \in \mathcal{A}$.

*Proof.* The box is the intersection of the half-spaces $\{u : \mathrm{lo}_i \leq u_i\}$ and $\{u : u_i \leq \mathrm{hi}_i\}$ for $i = 1, \dots, 9$, and an intersection of convex sets is convex, so $\mathcal{A}$ is convex. For the closure property, fix a component $i$ and write $u_i = \alpha\, u^{(1)}_i + (1-\alpha)\, u^{(2)}_i$. Because $\alpha \geq 0$, $(1-\alpha) \geq 0$, and the two coefficients sum to one, the combination lies between $u^{(1)}_i$ and $u^{(2)}_i$. Both endpoints obey $\mathrm{lo}_i \leq u^{(1)}_i, u^{(2)}_i \leq \mathrm{hi}_i$, hence $\mathrm{lo}_i \leq u_i \leq \mathrm{hi}_i$. Since this holds for every component, $u \in \mathcal{A}$, which completes the proof.

Proposition 3.1 has a practical consequence that shapes the whole implementation. System 1 applies a componentwise clip to its raw network output, System 2 samples its candidates uniformly inside the box, and therefore both inputs of the arbiter always lie in $\mathcal{A}$; the blend then remains in $\mathcal{A}$ without any further projection step. Feasibility of the setpoints with respect to the AC power flow follows from the device models of Section 3.4, and the January 2019 evaluation indeed converged at every one of the 721 hourly steps for all controllers, a result reported in Section 11.1. The same property underlies the mode classification of the arbiter in Chapter 4, because any of the five arbitration modes can be entered and exited without leaving the feasible action set.

## 3.3 Multi-Layer Coordination Architecture

Coordinated control of heterogeneous assets requires an explicit answer to the question of which controller decides what, and at which speed. CAPSM answers with a three-tier hierarchy, illustrated in Figure 3.2, that separates device-level reflexes, regional coordination, and system-wide optimisation. The tiering follows the classical hierarchical control doctrine for power systems [36], [124], but each tier is populated with the learning-based components developed in this thesis rather than with purely analytical regulators alone.

![Figure 3.2: Hierarchical control structure in the CAPSM framework.](figures/fig_capsm_layers.png)

The *device tier* comprises the fastest, most local loops: the FACTS shunt and series controllers regulating their bus voltages and branch flows [4], [6], and the local state-of-charge (SoC) supervision of each EV station that enforces the battery limits derived in Section 3.4. The *regional tier* groups buses into coherent zones, in the case of the IEEE 39-bus system the northern buses 1 to 19 and the southern buses 20 to 39, and resolves interactions that individual devices cannot see, such as reactive power competition between neighbouring shunt devices. The *system tier* hosts the dual-process controllers of Section 3.2 and the global objectives of Section 3.7, and it is the only tier with visibility of the full network state.

Information moves through the hierarchy in five recurring steps. First, device and PMU telemetry are acquired at the device tier and time-stamped against the synchronised clock. Second, the regional tier aggregates the telemetry, aligns it through the state estimation described in Chapter 4, and compresses it into the 160-feature observation vector. Third, the system tier evaluates the observation through System 1 and System 2 and produces candidate setpoints. Fourth, the Metacognitive Arbiter blends the candidate setpoints and broadcasts them down the hierarchy, where regional coordination resolves any residual conflicts between neighbouring assets. Fifth, the devices actuate and the resulting network response is measured again, closing the loop. The five steps repeat at every control cycle, and each step carries the time budget assigned to it in Section 3.9.

Because the three tiers cannot run at one common rate without either starving the devices or overloading the supervisory layer, CAPSM adopts a multi-rate scheme, shown in Figure 3.3. The electromagnetic transient solver on the real-time simulator advances at 50 microseconds, the phasor solver at 1 ms, the System 1 reflex at a 5 ms period, the arbiter at a 10 ms period, and the System 2 deliberative loop at a 50 ms period, while the offline quasi-static time-series (QSTS) evaluation used for validation advances in hourly steps. Rate-monotonic schedulability of these periodic tasks on a fixed set of processors can be assessed with the classical utilisation bound of Liu and Layland [45], and Chapter 10 performs that assessment for the four-core deployment. The multi-rate structure also mirrors the temporal coordination of services discussed in Section 3.7, where each asset family contributes on the timescale at which it is physically effective.

## 3.4 Mathematical Modelling of Integrated Components

This section assembles the mathematical models on which the controllers of Section 3.2 operate. The models are deliberately kept at the steady-state and quasi-static level, because the framework is validated on quasi-static time series with hourly resolution and on phasor-domain real-time simulation; electromagnetic transient detail is introduced only where the hardware deployment requires it in Chapter 10. All models are implemented exactly as written here in the open code base, which guarantees that the analysis, the proofs, and the reported results refer to the same equations.

### 3.4.1 AC Power Flow and the Newton Solution

The network coupling is described by the rectangular balance of active and reactive power at every bus. For bus $i$ of an $n$-bus system with voltage magnitudes $V$ and angles $\theta$, the injected powers are

```eq
%%3.5
P_i = V_i \sum_{j=1}^{n} V_j \,(G_{ij} \cos\theta_{ij} + B_{ij} \sin\theta_{ij})
```

```eq
%%3.6
Q_i = V_i \sum_{j=1}^{n} V_j \,(G_{ij} \sin\theta_{ij} - B_{ij} \cos\theta_{ij})
```

where $G_{ij}$ and $B_{ij}$ are the conductance and susceptance entries of the bus admittance matrix and $\theta_{ij} = \theta_i - \theta_j$ [38], [122]. Collecting the mismatch equations for all non-slack buses yields a nonlinear system $F(x) = 0$ in the state vector $x$ of voltage magnitudes and angles, which the Newton-Raphson method solves by the iteration

```eq
%%3.7
x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - [J(x^{(k)})]^{-1} F(x^{(k)})
```

with $J$ the Jacobian of the mismatch equations [39], [40]. The implementation uses the PYPOWER solver [41], and the convergence behaviour of this iteration underpins every result in the thesis, so its local property is recorded formally.

**Lemma 3.1 (Local convergence of the Newton-Raphson power flow).**
*Statement.* Let $F$ be continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of a solution $x^{*}$ with $F(x^{*}) = 0$ and $J(x^{*})$ nonsingular. Then there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for every start $x^{(0)}$ with $\Vert x^{(0)} - x^{*} \Vert < \varepsilon$ the iteration of Equation (3.7) is well defined and converges to $x^{*}$, and there is a constant $C > 0$ with $\Vert x^{(k+1)} - x^{*} \Vert \leq C \Vert x^{(k)} - x^{*} \Vert^{2}$, that is, the convergence is quadratic.

*Proof sketch.* Taylor expansion of $F$ around $x^{(k)}$ gives $F(x^{*}) = F(x^{(k)}) + J(x^{(k)})(x^{*} - x^{(k)}) + R$, with the remainder bounded by $K \Vert x^{*} - x^{(k)} \Vert^{2}$ since $F$ is twice differentiable in the neighbourhood. Substituting the Newton step of Equation (3.7) and using $F(x^{*}) = 0$ yields $x^{(k+1)} - x^{*} = -[J(x^{(k)})]^{-1} R$. By continuity of the inverse near the nonsingular $J(x^{*})$, the norm of $[J(x^{(k)})]^{-1}$ is bounded by some $M$ for all $x^{(k)}$ close enough to $x^{*}$, so $\Vert x^{(k+1)} - x^{*} \Vert \leq M K \Vert x^{(k)} - x^{*} \Vert^{2}$, and taking $C = MK$ with $\varepsilon$ small enough that $C \varepsilon < 1$ gives both convergence and the quadratic rate. The matrix analysis underlying the inverse bound follows [121], and the full classical treatment is given in [39] and [123]. This completes the proof sketch.

The uncontrolled IEEE 39-bus power flow solves in approximately 7.8 ms per step on the reference workstation, which fixes the computational floor of any controller that embeds a full AC solution in its evaluation loop, a fact that motivates the candidate evaluation design of Section 3.5.

### 3.4.2 FACTS Device Models

The FACTS family is represented at the steady-state level as controllable modifications of the bus admittance structure, which is the standard quasi-static representation for shunt and series devices [4], [5], [6]. The SVC at bus 14 and the STATCOM at bus 39 act as controllable shunt susceptances. Writing the commanded increment as $\Delta b$, the applied susceptance is

```eq
%%3.8
B_{\mathrm{sh}} = \min\{\max(B_0 + \Delta b,\; B_{\min}),\; B_{\max}\}
```

with the range $B_{\mathrm{sh}} \in [-0.5, 0.5]$ p.u. for the SVC and $[-1.0, 1.0]$ p.u. for the STATCOM. The clamp in Equation (3.8) guarantees that no controller command, learned or otherwise, can push the device outside its rating. The TCSC on line 16–17 modifies the series reactance of the branch according to

```eq
%%3.9
X_{\mathrm{eff}} = (1 - k)\, X, \qquad k \in [0,\, 0.7]
```

where $X$ is the natural branch reactance and $k$ is the compensation degree; the operational setpoint range used by the controllers is $k \in [0.3, 0.7]$, which keeps the device in its designed compensation band as discussed in Section 4.1 [7]. The UPFC at bus 26 combines both principles: a shunt susceptance at bus 26 within $\pm 0.5$ p.u. and a series channel on branch 26–28 with compensation degree $k \in [0, 0.5]$ and an injected series power of magnitude at most 0.5 p.u. Its admissible command set is therefore the product

```eq
%%3.10
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{UPFC}} = \{(B_{\mathrm{sh}},\, k,\, P_s):\; |B_{\mathrm{sh}}| \leq 0.5,\; k \in [0,\, 0.5],\; |P_s| \leq 0.5\}
```

which makes the UPFC the richest single actuator in the action box and, at the same time, the device whose channels must be coordinated most carefully by the arbiter. All FACTS models apply their modifications directly to the power flow input data before each solution, so the device limits in Equations (3.8) to (3.10) are enforced by construction rather than by post-hoc correction.

### 3.4.3 EV Fleet Aggregation and State-of-Charge Dynamics

Each of the three EV stations at buses 3, 8, and 15 represents an aggregated fleet of bidirectional vehicles, a standard reduction for transmission-level studies that preserves the aggregate power and energy capability while abstracting from individual parking events. Station $b$ is characterised by its power limit $p_{\mathrm{max}} = 50$ MW, its usable energy capacity $E_{\mathrm{cap}} = 100$ MWh, its one-way efficiency $\eta = 0.92$, and its SoC limits $[0.2, 0.9]$ with initial value 0.5, and the time step is $\Delta t = 1$ h. Charging with power $P_t > 0$ advances the state of charge according to

```eq
%%3.11
\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SoC}_t + \frac{\eta\, P_t\, \Delta t}{E_{\mathrm{cap}}}
```

while discharging with $P_t < 0$ loses an additional fraction to the round trip and obeys

```eq
%%3.12
\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SoC}_t + \frac{P_t\, \Delta t}{\eta\, E_{\mathrm{cap}}}
```

The dispatch rule that precedes the update applies four steps in fixed order: the commanded power is clamped to $[-p_{\mathrm{max}}, p_{\mathrm{max}}]$; charging commands are zeroed when the station is already at or above the upper SoC bound; discharging commands are zeroed at or below the lower bound; and the resulting SoC is projected onto $[0.2, 0.9]$. The efficiency asymmetry between Equations (3.11) and (3.12) reflects energy lost in the power electronics and the battery during each one-way transfer, and it implies that a full charge-discharge cycle returns at most $\eta^2 \approx 0.85$ of the withdrawn energy, a factor that the service valuation of Section 3.7 must respect. The rule set looks innocuous, but it is what guarantees that the fleet can never damage its batteries by following learned commands, so it deserves a formal statement.

**Proposition 3.2 (Invariance of the SoC trajectory under the dispatch rule).**
*Statement.* Consider any station with $E_{\mathrm{cap}} > 0$, $\eta \in (0, 1]$, bounds $[\mathrm{SoC}_{\mathrm{lo}}, \mathrm{SoC}_{\mathrm{hi}}] = [0.2, 0.9]$, and initial state $\mathrm{SoC}_0 \in [0.2, 0.9]$, operated under the dispatch rule consisting of (i) clamping the command to $[-p_{\mathrm{max}}, p_{\mathrm{max}}]$, (ii) zeroing charging commands at $\mathrm{SoC} \geq \mathrm{SoC}_{\mathrm{hi}}$ and discharging commands at $\mathrm{SoC} \leq \mathrm{SoC}_{\mathrm{lo}}$, (iii) the updates of Equations (3.11) and (3.12), and (iv) projection of the updated SoC onto $[0.2, 0.9]$. Then $\mathrm{SoC}_t \in [0.2, 0.9]$ for all $t \geq 0$.

*Proof.* The proof proceeds by induction on $t$. Base case: $\mathrm{SoC}_0 \in [0.2, 0.9]$ holds by assumption. Inductive step: assume $\mathrm{SoC}_t \in [0.2, 0.9]$ for an arbitrary $t$. If rule (ii) zeroes the command, then $\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SoC}_t$ and the claim holds. Otherwise, if the station charges, the increment $\eta P_t \Delta t / E_{\mathrm{cap}}$ in Equation (3.11) is non-negative, so the provisional value satisfies $\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} \geq \mathrm{SoC}_t \geq 0.2$, and rule (iv) caps it at 0.9. If the station discharges, the increment $P_t \Delta t / (\eta E_{\mathrm{cap}})$ in Equation (3.12) is non-positive, so the provisional value satisfies $\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} \leq \mathrm{SoC}_t \leq 0.9$, and rule (iv) floors it at 0.2. In every case $\mathrm{SoC}_{t+1} \in [0.2, 0.9]$, which closes the induction and completes the proof.

The proposition shows that the battery envelope is an invariant set of the closed-loop dispatch system, independently of how aggressive the learned commands are. In the implementation, rule (iv) is active in practice whenever a large charge command meets a nearly full station, for example a 50 MW command at $\mathrm{SoC} = 0.89$ would provisionally reach $0.89 + 0.46$ without the projection, so both the zeroing rules and the projection contribute to the guarantee. Chapter 8 reuses this result when valuing the energy throughput of the fleets, and it cross-references the proof rather than repeating it.

### 3.4.4 DER Integration and Environment Dispatch Rules

The renewable generation sites inject real power as negative load at their buses, with the national wind and solar profiles of Chapter 2 disaggregated onto the network; on the IEEE 39-bus system the wind sites sit at buses 4 and 14 and the solar sites at buses 8 and 15. Reactive demand scales with the active demand at constant per-bus power factor, $Q = Q_0 (P / P_0)$, so that load changes preserve the power factor structure of the base case. Two environment rules then keep the dispatch physically meaningful, and Proposition 3.3 establishes their internal consistency; the rules are reproduced exactly from the environment implementation so that the proof refers to code-level behaviour.

The first rule is the renewable curtailment cap: if the total renewable injection exceeds $0.90$ times the load, all renewable sites are scaled down proportionally to the cap. In January 2019 this rule activates in 15 of the 721 hours, about 2.1 percent of the month, and it exists because transmission-level dispatch cannot assume that surplus inverter production is exportable beyond the design of the corridor. The second rule is the proportional redispatch: every non-slack generator setpoint is multiplied by the factor $\kappa = (P_{\mathrm{net}} + L_{\mathrm{est}}) / (P_{\mathrm{base}} + L_{\mathrm{est}})$, where $P_{\mathrm{net}}$ is the net load after renewable and EV adjustments, $L_{\mathrm{est}}$ is the base-case loss estimate, and $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ is the base-case load, after which $\kappa$ is clipped to $[0.2, 1.5]$ and each setpoint is capped at its maximum $P_{\mathrm{max}}$; the slack machine absorbs the residual imbalance. In the base-case preparation, generator voltage setpoints above 1.04 p.u. are capped at that value, which removes approximately 2386 false-positive violating bus-hours from the raw IEEE case data and leaves 2847 genuine violation bus-hours in the uncontrolled January 2019 benchmark, the reference figure used throughout Chapter 11.

**Proposition 3.3 (Feasibility of the environment dispatch rules).**
*Statement.* Under the curtailment rule with cap factor $0.90$ and the proportional redispatch rule with $\kappa$ clipped to $[0.2, 1.5]$ and setpoints capped at $P_{\mathrm{max}}$, every non-slack generator output satisfies $0 \leq P_g \leq P_{\mathrm{max}}$, every renewable injection satisfies $P_{\mathrm{re}} \leq 0.90\, P_{\mathrm{load}}$, and the slack machine absorbs only the residual mismatch left by those bounded adjustments, so the solved dispatch remains inside the designed operating envelope of the benchmark.

*Proof.* The proof is by construction, following the exact order of operations in the environment. First, the curtailment rule multiplies every renewable injection by the scale $s = \min(1, 0.90\, P_{\mathrm{load}} / P_{\mathrm{re}})$, and since $s \geq 0$ the scaled injection is non-negative and at most $0.90\, P_{\mathrm{load}}$, giving the injection bound. Second, EV dispatch adds only powers already bounded by Proposition 3.2 and the clamp to $\pm p_{\mathrm{max}}$, so the net load $P_{\mathrm{net}}$ is finite and non-negative. Third, each non-slack setpoint becomes $P_g = \min(\kappa\, P_{g,0},\, P_{\mathrm{max}})$ with $\kappa \in [0.2, 1.5]$ and $P_{g,0} \geq 0$, hence $0 \leq P_g \leq P_{\mathrm{max}}$, giving the generator bound. Fourth, because every adjustment except the slack is deterministic and bounded as above, the remaining power imbalance is a bounded residual that the slack machine balances by construction of the slack bus type. The clip interval $[0.2, 1.5]$ bounds the requested redispatch excursion so that the residual stays within the range exercised by the benchmark, and the empirical confirmation is that all 721 hourly power flows of January 2019 converge for every controller tested, as reported in Section 11.1. This completes the proof.

Together, Propositions 3.2 and 3.3 establish that the learned control layer operates on top of a dispatch layer whose feasibility does not depend on the learned layer behaving well. This division of responsibility, physics-bounded envelopes with intelligence inside them, recurs in the fault detection design of Section 3.6 and in the safety analysis of Chapter 10. The loading margin assessment used alongside these rules applies a bisection search over the loading scale $\lambda \in [1, 3]$ with a coarse step of 0.05 and eight refinement iterations, following the continuation concepts of [125] in the form implemented in the stability module.

## 3.5 Quantum-Inspired Reinforcement Learning

System 2 requires a search mechanism that evaluates many coordinated actions per step, reinforces the ones that perform well, and still explores sufficiently to escape poor regions of the action space. CAPSM adopts a quantum-inspired formulation, drawing on the quantum computation formalism of [58] and the quantum reinforcement learning line of work in [59], [60], [61]. The approach treats the 32 candidate actions of the current step as computational basis states of a register and stores the decision state as a superposition over them, so that every candidate carries an amplitude whose squared magnitude is its selection probability.

The quantum state over the candidate set $\mathcal{A}_c$ is written as

```eq
%%3.13
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}} \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}_c} \sqrt{\exp(\beta Q(s, a))}\; |s, a\rangle, \qquad Z = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}_c} \exp(\beta Q(s, a))
```

where $Q(s, a)$ is the action-value estimate, $\beta$ is the inverse temperature, and $Z$ is the partition function that normalises the state. The state has 32 amplitudes $\alpha_i$, one per candidate, initialised uniformly as $\alpha_i = 1/\sqrt{32}$, and the candidates themselves are initialised by uniform sampling inside the action box of Proposition 3.1. Measurement of the state selects an action according to the Born rule,

```eq
%%3.14
P(a_i) = |\alpha_i|^2, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{32} |\alpha_i|^2 = 1
```

which is the standard probability postulate of quantum mechanics applied here to a classical, amplitude-based decision state [58]. The Bloch sphere representation in Figure 3.4 illustrates how a two-level projection of the state visualises the balance between exploitation of high-amplitude candidates and exploration of the remainder; the figure is an intuition aid, since the implemented register is 32-dimensional and purely classical.

![Figure 3.4: Quantum-inspired state representation.](figures/fig_bloch_qirl.png)

Reinforcement enters through the amplitudes. After every evaluation step, each candidate $a_i$ receives the immediate reward

```eq
%%3.15
r_i = -\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{bus}}} \sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mathrm{bus}}} |V_{m,k} - 1| - 0.05\, n_{\mathrm{viol}} - 0.001\, \Vert a_i \Vert_2^2
```

which combines the mean voltage deviation, a penalty of 0.05 per violating bus in the current step, and a quadratic effort term that discourages pointless actuation; the reward shares its structure with the System 1 training reward of Section 3.7 but is computed per candidate rather than per step. The amplitude update then reinforcement-shifts the state towards well-rewarded candidates:

```eq
%%3.16
\alpha_i(t+1) = \frac{\exp(\beta r_i / 2)}{\sqrt{\sum_{j} \exp(\beta r_j)}}
```

The implementation evaluates Equation (3.16) after shifting all rewards by their maximum, that is, with $r_i$ replaced by $r_i - r_{\mathrm{max}}$, which leaves every probability ratio unchanged while keeping the exponentials inside floating-point range; Section 4.2 proves that the update preserves the unit norm of the amplitude vector exactly. Selection samples a candidate from the Born distribution of Equation (3.14), and the selected candidate becomes $u^{(2)}$ for Equation (3.4).

Exploration is provided by a tunnelling mechanism that mimics the passage of quantum states through classically forbidding barriers. After each selection, a fixed fraction of the candidate pool is discarded and re-sampled uniformly from the action box regardless of its current amplitude, with the fraction set to a tunnel rate of 0.1, which for 32 candidates re-randomises 3 candidates per step. Tunnelling prevents the amplitude update from collapsing the state onto a single candidate in the first steps of an episode, and it repeatedly injects diversity into regions of the action box that gradient-free selection would otherwise never revisit. The planning horizon parameter is set to 6 steps but is reserved in the Stage-1 implementation, which evaluates candidates single-step against the current power flow state; the extension to genuine multi-step rollouts is listed among the future work items in Chapter 13.

The single most important design parameter is the inverse temperature $\beta = 5.0$, and the following theorem makes its role precise.

**Theorem 3.1 (Exploitation-exploration balance of the amplitude update).**
*Statement.* Let $P(a_i)$ and $P(a_j)$ be the Born-rule probabilities of any two candidates $a_i, a_j \in \mathcal{A}_c$ induced by the amplitude update of Equation (3.16). Then

```eq
%%3.17
\frac{P(a_i)}{P(a_j)} = \exp(\beta\,(r_i - r_j))
```

so the ratio is bounded between $\exp(-\beta \Delta)$ and $\exp(\beta \Delta)$, where $\Delta = r_{\mathrm{max}} - r_{\mathrm{min}}$ is the reward spread of the current candidate pool. The bound is invariant to any common reward offset $r_i \mapsto r_i + c$.

*Proof.* Squaring Equation (3.16) gives $|\alpha_i|^2 = \exp(\beta r_i) / \sum_{m} \exp(\beta r_m)$, because the numerator squares to $\exp(\beta r_i)$ and the denominator squares to the sum. Taking the ratio for candidates $i$ and $j$, the normalising sums cancel and Equation (3.17) follows directly. The bounds follow from $r_i - r_j \in [-\Delta, \Delta]$. For the offset invariance, substituting $r_i + c$ into Equation (3.17) contributes a factor $\exp(\beta c)$ to numerator and denominator alike, which cancels, so the ratio is unchanged. This completes the proof.

Theorem 3.1 shows that the selection law is exactly a softmax with inverse temperature $\beta$, so $\beta$ alone governs the exploitation-exploration trade-off. With $\beta = 5.0$, a reward advantage of 0.1 yields a probability ratio of $\exp(0.5) \approx 1.65$, which keeps the distribution reasonably flat and preserves diversity among near-optimal candidates; an advantage of 0.5 yields $\exp(2.5) \approx 12.2$; and an advantage of 1.0 yields $\exp(5) \approx 148$, which effectively commits selection to the dominant candidate. The chosen value therefore implements a graded, rather than binary, preference structure, and because of the offset invariance the same behaviour is obtained whether or not the max-shift of the implementation is applied. The frame-level behaviour of the resulting controller on the January 2019 benchmark, including its 2810 violation bus-hours and 21.1 ms mean inference time in the standalone evaluation, is reported in Section 11.1, and the convergence-rate comparison against deep Q-networks, a 58 to 59 percent design target from the framework specification, is discussed with the other design targets in Section 11.4.

## 3.6 Multi-Modal Fault Detection and Localisation

Coordination at the speed of Section 3.7 is only useful if the framework also knows when the observed system departs from its expected behaviour. CAPSM therefore includes a multi-modal fault detection and localisation layer that fuses heterogeneous measurements into a single situational picture, following the anomaly detection concepts of [71] and the false data injection analysis of [72], with localisation methodology in the tradition of [73]. The layer is deliberately multi-modal because no single measurement family covers all fault signatures: PMU streams resolve dynamics at the synchrophasor rate, SCADA and IEC 61850 process bus traffic resolve switching and device states [91], smart meters resolve distribution-level anomalies, weather feeds explain renewable-driven excursions that would otherwise look like faults, and protection relay operations provide authoritative event stamps.

The fusion architecture, shown in Figure 3.5, operates at three levels. At the *signal level*, raw streams are aligned in time against the precision time protocol of [92], conditioned, and converted to per-mode feature sequences. At the *feature level*, the per-mode features $\phi_i(x_i)$ extracted from each source $i$ are combined into a single fused representation

```eq
%%3.18
z = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \alpha_i\, \phi_i(x_i), \qquad \alpha_i \geq 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^{M} \alpha_i = 1
```

where the attention weights $\alpha_i$ are learned so that trustworthy and informative modes dominate the representation in the prevailing conditions; the weighted form mirrors the attention mechanism used inside System 1 and keeps the fusion differentiable end to end. At the *decision level*, the fused representation feeds classifiers and residual tests: a supervised classifier assigns fault classes, an autoencoder-style reconstruction score flags unseen anomalies, and the weighted residual statistic of the state estimator, developed in Chapter 7, provides a false data injection screen with a chi-squared threshold.

![Figure 3.5: Multi-modal data fusion architecture.](figures/fig_fusion_architecture.png)

The detection layer interacts with the control layer through the Metacognitive Arbiter. A confirmed anomaly raises the effective stress of the system, which shifts arbitration authority towards System 2, and a severe or safety-critical event can trigger the safe-mode classification of the arbiter described in Section 4.2. Localisation estimates the fault zone from the pattern of residuals and relay operations, which lets the coordination layer of Section 3.7 re-dispatch around the affected corridor instead of applying a blanket stress response. The framework specification sets the design targets for this layer at 95.8 percent fault detection and localisation accuracy and a 74 percent reduction in false positives relative to conventional thresholding; these are design targets from the specification rather than Stage-1 measurements, and the hardware detection latency achieved in the HIL campaign is reported in Chapter 10.

## 3.7 Coordinated Control Strategies

The coordination layer translates the architectural machinery of the previous sections into operating policy. Its objectives are sixfold: voltage regulation within 0.95 to 1.05 p.u., congestion relief on overloaded corridors, loss reduction, frequency response support, renewable absorption, and resilience under N-1 contingencies. These objectives are not equally achievable by every asset family, and a central design decision of CAPSM is to make the assignment of assets to objectives explicit rather than implicit. The reward structure of the learning layer encodes the priority: the System 1 training reward weights voltage deviation at 1.0, normalised losses at 0.5 with a 50 MW base, and adds a penalty of −10 per violating bus in the current step, and the System 2 candidate reward of Equation (3.15) uses the same voltage and violation terms with a quadratic effort penalty, so both pathways score their actions against the same control objectives.

Temporally, the coordination follows the multi-rate structure of Figure 3.3. On the fast scale, FACTS shunt devices and the System 1 reflex respond within their 5 ms budget to voltage excursions, matching the speed class of classical FACTS control [4], [7]. On the intermediate scale, the arbiter and the regional coordination loop, running at 10 ms and upward, reconcile device-level actions with regional reactive power balance. On the slow scale, System 2 coordinates FACTS setpoints jointly with EV fleet dispatch within its 50 ms budget, and the QSTS layer captures the hourly evolution over months. Each asset thus contributes on the timescale at which it is physically effective, and no asset is asked to deliver a service its response time cannot support.

Spatially, the coordination assigns each objective to the narrowest tier that can serve it, following the hierarchy of Figure 3.2. Local voltage excursions are corrected by the nearest shunt device, corridor overloads by the series devices and regional redispatch, and system-wide imbalances by the system tier with slack participation, which keeps communication requirements local wherever possible. The ancillary service capability that results from this assignment is summarised in Figure 3.6 and Table 3.1. The table aggregates the heatmap ratings into the three asset families used throughout the thesis, and the rating column reflects the capability of the coordinated portfolio rather than of any single asset, which is precisely the argument for coordination: individually weak service providers become collectively strong when their response times and locations complement each other.

## 3.8 Practical Implementation Considerations

A framework intended for field deployment must specify where its computation lives. CAPSM follows a central-fog-edge partition. The *edge* layer hosts the device-tier reflexes and the protection-grade measurement electronics, where determinism matters more than optimality. The *fog* layer, implemented on substation and regional controllers, hosts the regional coordination, the state estimation, and the fast parts of the dual-process loop. The *central* layer hosts the slow scheduling, the retraining pipelines, and the human-machine interface. The partition maps directly onto the four-core real-time deployment of Section 3.9, so that the same partition is exercised in simulation, in hardware-in-the-loop testing, and eventually in the field.

The communication infrastructure uses three standards with distinct roles. Substation-internal device communication follows IEC 61850 [91], which provides the process bus and the generic object-oriented substation event mechanisms used for relay-grade signalling. Synchrophasor transfer follows IEEE C37.118 [89], which defines the reporting format and rate for the PMU streams consumed by the fusion layer of Section 3.6. Time synchronisation follows the precision time protocol of IEEE 1588 [92], which aligns all measurements and control stamps to a common clock, an indispensable service for any fusion or arbitration logic that compares quantities measured at different stations. DER interconnection assumptions follow IEEE 1547 [90], which constrains how inverter-based resources respond to voltage and frequency conditions.

Integration with existing energy management systems (EMS) and distribution management systems (DMS) is achieved through the state estimation interface: CAPSM consumes the estimated state produced by the EMS state estimator, or its own weighted least squares estimator when operating autonomously as described in Chapter 4, and returns setpoints through the same supervisory channels the EMS already uses. This design lets utilities adopt the framework incrementally, first in advisory mode where its commands are displayed rather than executed, then in closed loop for selected assets. Scalability was verified along two axes: the offline QSTS evaluation covers systems from 9 to 300 buses with the per-step power flow cost growing from approximately 2 ms for IEEE 9 to approximately 15 ms for IEEE 118, and the real-time parallelisation achieved 86.7 percent parallel efficiency at 16 compute nodes in the HIL campaign for the larger system, with a design target of 60 percent computation reduction through the distributed optimisation layer of Chapter 9. The framework therefore scales in both the analytical and the deployment sense, although the multi-area optimisation needed for very large systems remains future work beyond the validated scope.

## 3.9 HIL-Ready Architecture Design

The final design principle of CAPSM is that every component must have a named destination on real-time hardware, which is the response to Gap 5 of Chapter 1. Figure 3.7 shows the deployment topology for controller hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing on the four-core OPAL-RT simulator. The plant, the IEEE 39-bus network with its FACTS devices, EV stations, and renewable sites, is solved by the real-time simulators, while the CAPSM control tasks run as scheduled real-time processes with defined communication paths to the plant model and to the monitoring workstation.

![Figure 3.7: OPAL-RT 4-core deployment topology for Controller-HIL.](figures/fig_opal_topology.png)

The allocation follows the natural partition of Section 3.3. Core 1, the master core, integrates the northern region (buses 1 to 19) and the System 1 CNN-LSTM task at a 5 ms period with the highest scheduling priority, because the reflex path is protection-adjacent. Core 2 integrates the southern region (buses 20 to 39) and the System 2 QIRL task at a 50 ms period. Core 3 hosts the Metacognitive Arbiter and the inter-core communication at a 10 ms period. Core 4 hosts input-output handling, logging, and the HIL interface at a 1 ms period. The solver stack pairs the ARTEMIS electromagnetic transient solver at a 50 microsecond step with the ePHASORSIM phasor solver at a 1 ms step, following the RT-LAB toolchain [56], and the network model derives from the IEEE 39-bus Simulink implementation of [57]. The CNN-LSTM is a fixed graph of roughly 0.14 million parameters, as derived in Section 4.2, which is small enough for embedded realisation, and the QIRL loop is a vectorised arithmetic kernel with no neural network forward pass at all, as Section 4.7 quantifies.

The real-time constraints are stated as period inequalities on the two control pathways,

```eq
%%3.19
\tau_1 < 5\;\mathrm{ms}, \qquad \tau_2 < 50\;\mathrm{ms}
```

where $\tau_1$ is the end-to-end latency of the System 1 path from measurement stamp to actuation command and $\tau_2$ the corresponding latency of the System 2 path. Equation (3.19) is the contract that the offline implementation must honour after porting, and Table 3.2 decomposes it into the per-layer budget that the deployment must meet. Figure 3.8 visualises the same budget against the four control layers and makes visible why the reflex path alone can meet protection-grade deadlines while the deliberative path cannot and should not.

![Figure 3.8: Real-time constraint budget across CAPSM control layers.](figures/fig_timing_budget.png)

**Table 3.2: HIL latency budget across CAPSM control layers.**

| Layer | Core | Period (budget) | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electromagnetic solver | 1 to 2 | 50 microseconds | ARTEMIS EMT step |
| Phasor solver | 1 to 2 | 1 ms | ePHASORSIM step |
| System 1 reflex | 1 | 5 ms | CNN-LSTM inference and clip |
| Arbiter and comms | 3 | 10 ms | Blending, mode logic, inter-core exchange |
| System 2 planning | 2 | 50 ms | QIRL candidate evaluation and selection |
| I/O and logging | 4 | 1 ms | Measurement acquisition, command output, recording |

The schedulability of this task set under rate-monotonic scheduling is checked against the utilisation bound of [45] in Chapter 10, using the measured execution times of Section 4.7, and the design target for the hardware campaign is that the reflex path meets Equation (3.19) with margin on all four cores. The HIL campaign reported in Chapter 10 achieved sub-10 ms fault detection on hardware and met the timing budget on the four cores, which closes the loop that this section opened: the architecture was designed against the hardware envelope, and the hardware measurements vindicated the design. Any future change to the architecture, for example a larger CNN or a longer QIRL candidate pool, must re-enter the design through this section, restate the budget, and re-verify the allocation, which is the discipline that keeps the framework HIL-ready rather than merely HIL-compatible.
