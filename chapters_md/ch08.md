# Chapter 8: EV Integration and DER Management

## 8.1 EV Fleet Modelling Approaches

The mass deployment of electric vehicles (EVs) changes the character of electrical load in two directions at once. Charging adds a load component that can concentrate in evening hours if left uncontrolled, while the parked fleet represents a distributed storage resource that can discharge back to the network under vehicle-to-grid (V2G) contracts. Kempton and Tomic quantified both aspects in their foundational analysis of V2G capacity and net revenue, showing that even a modest fraction of the vehicle fleet provides gigawatt-scale flexibility if it is aggregated and dispatched [8], [9]. The design question for a control framework such as CAPSM is therefore not whether EV flexibility exists, but how it should be modelled so that control policies validated against the model transfer credibly to real-time hardware.

Two modelling resolutions are available. The individual-vehicle approach represents every vehicle as a battery pack with its own capacity, charger rating, efficiency, state of charge (SoC), and trip schedule. This resolution captures behavioural heterogeneity and is well suited to distribution-level studies, where the coincidence of uncontrolled residential charging can erode feeder voltage margins [15]. Its drawback for transmission-level work is combinatorial: a fleet consistent with the load scale of the IEEE 39-bus benchmark comprises thousands of vehicles, and carrying thousands of individual states through every quasi-static time-series (QSTS) step and every learning episode adds cost without adding information at the granularity at which the controller acts.

The thesis therefore adopts the aggregated station model, which is a standard reduction for transmission-level EV studies [14]. As implemented in the Stage-1 environment, three aggregated stations are connected at buses 3, 8, and 15 of the IEEE 39-bus system. Each station has a power limit of 50 MW, an energy capacity of 100 MWh, a one-way efficiency of eta = 0.92, SoC bounds [0.2, 0.9] with initial value 0.5, and a scheduling interval of dt = 1 h. Positive net power denotes grid-to-vehicle (G2V) charging and negative net power denotes V2G discharge, so each station appears to the network as a dispatchable, bidirectional load at its bus of connection.

The aggregation is defensible on behavioural-coherence grounds. At representative per-vehicle figures of 40 kWh of usable energy and charging powers between 19.2 kW and 40 kW, a 50 MW / 100 MWh station corresponds to roughly 1,250 vehicles charging at 40 kW, or approximately 2,500 vehicles with 40 kWh packs. Within such a population, individual departure times and SoC states average out, and the station-level envelope, rather than any single vehicle schedule, is what constrains dispatch. The controller commands station setpoints only; the internal composition of the fleet is invisible to the network model and is represented statistically through the availability and degradation models to which we turn next.

Availability is modelled stochastically because connection times are behavioural rather than deterministic. Trip completions arrive at a station according to a non-homogeneous Poisson process with intensity lambda(t) that peaks in the early evening for residential clusters and in the morning for workplace clusters. Each arrival plugs in with a probability that depends on the cluster and on the battery state, and the resulting dwell duration is drawn from a cluster-specific distribution. Integrating connected capacity over the day yields the realisable power envelope within which the dispatcher may command charge or discharge. Figure 8.1 shows the resulting availability patterns across charging locations; the evening residential peak and the long workplace plateau delimit the hours in which V2G services can realistically be offered.

![Figure 8.1: Typical EV availability patterns across charging locations.](figures/fig_ev_availability.png)

Charging behaviour falls into a small number of recurrent patterns, and these patterns determine both the flexibility that a station can offer and the price signal to which it responds. Table 8.1 summarises the four clusters used throughout the thesis. Overnight residential charging is long and price-sensitive; workplace charging is long but bounded by working hours; public fast charging is short, expensive, and largely inflexible; and fleet-depot charging is contracted and therefore the most dispatchable. The cluster view maps directly onto the aggregated stations of the benchmark: bus 15 is dominated by workplace demand, bus 8 by mixed public and workplace demand, and bus 3 by fleet and residential demand.

**Table 8.1: EV charging behaviour clusters.**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

V2G operation is not free, because every discharge cycle adds throughput to the battery and consumes cycle life. Semi-empirical ageing models express capacity fade as a function of cumulative energy throughput, in the style of Equation (8.1), where Q_loss is the fractional capacity lost, E_th is the cumulative throughput, and k_e is a cell-chemistry coefficient calibrated from laboratory cycling data. Throughput-based fade is the dominant term under V2G duty, and degradation-limiting dispatch that trades energy revenue against ageing cost is an active research direction [84]. Figure 8.3 illustrates capacity fade under different V2G usage scenarios: an uncontrolled daily charge-discharge cycle retires the pack measurably faster than frequency-regulation duty, whose shallow cycles accumulate less throughput per delivered service. The economic dispatch of Section 8.2 therefore prices degradation per unit of discharged energy rather than treating the battery as a free reservoir.

```eq
%%8.1
Q_{loss} = k_e \sqrt{E_{th}}
```

## 8.2 Bidirectional Power Flow Management (V2G/G2V)

Bidirectional power exchange is enabled by the charger power-electronic interface. Figure 8.2 shows the topology adopted for the thesis models, following the classification of charger topologies, charging power levels, and infrastructure in [12]. A grid-side active front end rectifies and inverts between the AC network and the DC link, and a bidirectional DC-DC stage regulates battery current and voltage in both directions. Three control loops coordinate the stages: a phase-locked loop (PLL) synchronises the converter to the grid voltage, an inner current loop tracks the reference dq-axis currents, and an outer power or SoC loop generates those references from the dispatcher setpoint. The same physical interface serves vehicle-to-home and vehicle-to-vehicle modes, which broaden the service menu but lie outside the transmission-level scope of this work [13].

![Figure 8.2: Bidirectional charger topology with control loops.](figures/fig_charger_topology.png)

Energy accounting must respect converter efficiency in both directions, because the losses differ between charging and discharging. Let |P| denote the magnitude of the AC-side power exchanged over an interval dt. Charging draws energy from the grid in excess of what is stored, while discharging delivers to the grid less than what leaves the battery. Equations (8.2) and (8.3) give the grid-side energy for the two directions, with eta = 0.92 the one-way efficiency of the implemented stations.

```eq
%%8.2
E_{grid}^{ch} = \frac{|P|\,\Delta t}{\eta}
```

```eq
%%8.3
E_{grid}^{dis} = |P|\,\Delta t\,\eta
```

The two directions combine into a round-trip loss expression that the dispatch layer can evaluate in closed form.

**Proposition 8.1 (Round-trip energy accounting).** Let a station exchange AC-side energy |P| dt in charging and subsequently return the same stored energy |P| dt in discharging, with one-way efficiency eta in (0, 1). Then the net energy drawn from the grid over the round trip is

```eq
%%8.4
E_{loss}^{rt} = |P|\,\Delta t\left(\frac{1}{\eta} - \eta\right)
```

*Proof.* Charging stores E_b = |P| dt in the battery while drawing E_grid^ch = |P| dt / eta from the grid, so the converter dissipates |P| dt (1/eta − 1). Discharging releases E_b = |P| dt from the battery and delivers E_grid^dis = eta |P| dt to the grid, dissipating |P| dt (1 − eta). The net grid-side deficit over the round trip is E_grid^ch − E_grid^dis = |P| dt (1/eta − eta), which is exactly the sum of the two conversion losses stated in Equation (8.4). This completes the proof.

With eta = 0.92 the round-trip efficiency is eta squared = 0.8464, so 15.4% of the stored energy is lost per full cycle; equivalently, the bracket (1/eta − eta) ≈ 0.167 in Equation (8.4) states the loss per unit of one-way throughput. The factor 1/0.8464 ≈ 1.18 is the multiplier on energy purchased per unit eventually delivered, and any arbitrage margin must exceed this multiplier times the charging price before degradation costs are even considered. These closed-form factors are used directly by the economic dispatch below and by the reward terms that price V2G duty in Chapter 4.

The station SoC evolves according to Equation (8.5), which applies the direction-dependent efficiency to the signed power: charging with P > 0 accumulates eta P dt of stored energy per interval, while discharging with P < 0 removes P dt / eta of stored energy for the same AC-side exchange.

```eq
%%8.5
SoC_{t+1} = SoC_t + \frac{\eta P_t \Delta t}{E_{cap}}\;(P_t > 0), \quad SoC_{t+1} = SoC_t + \frac{P_t \Delta t}{\eta E_{cap}}\;(P_t < 0)
```

SoC bounds are part of the station contract, not an afterthought. Proposition 3.2 in Chapter 3 establishes that the bounds [0.2, 0.9] are invariant under the implemented dispatch rule, by induction over dispatch steps, and we do not repeat that proof here. The practical consequence is that any setpoint sequence accepted by the dispatcher leaves every station inside its contractual window, which is precisely the property that the hardware campaign in Section 8.6 relies upon.

Algorithm 8.1 states the implemented mode-transition and accounting logic. It is reproduced faithfully from the Stage-1 dispatch code and is the executable definition of G2V/V2G behaviour in every experiment of this thesis.

**Algorithm 8.1: V2G/G2V mode transition and energy accounting.**

```text
Require: requested setpoints p_req[b] (MW, charge > 0, discharge < 0)
         state SoC[b], station data p_max[b], cap[b]
         one-way efficiency eta = 0.92, interval dt, bounds soc_lo = 0.2, soc_hi = 0.9
Ensure:  committed powers p[b], updated SoC[b]
1: for each station b do
2:     p <- clamp(p_req[b], -p_max[b], +p_max[b])     # power clamp
3:     if p > 0 and SoC[b] >= soc_hi then p <- 0      # charging complete, hold
4:     if p < 0 and SoC[b] <= soc_lo then p <- 0      # discharge floor, hold
5:     if p > 0 then SoC[b] <- SoC[b] + p * dt * eta / cap[b]
6:     if p < 0 then SoC[b] <- SoC[b] + p * dt / (eta * cap[b])
7:     SoC[b] <- clamp(SoC[b], soc_lo, soc_hi)
8:     p[b] <- p
9: end for
10: return p, SoC
```

Three properties of the algorithm deserve emphasis. First, the power clamp precedes the SoC logic, so no request outside the converter rating can enter the state update. Second, the zero-forcing at the bounds implements the contractual floor and ceiling exactly, and combined with the final clamp it yields the bound invariance established by Proposition 3.2. Third, the accounting uses the direction-dependent efficiency of Equation (8.5), so energy bookkeeping remains consistent with the physical converter at eta = 0.92.

Economic optimisation decides when a station should charge and when it should discharge. The thesis prices energy with the hourly day-ahead series of the Open Power System Data (OPSD) package, which provides 17,540 hourly price values over the window 2018-09-30 to 2020-09-30 with a minimum of −90.01, a mean of 35.81, and a maximum of 200.04 EUR/MWh [42]. Negative prices are real market outcomes in hours of surplus renewable generation, not data artefacts, and they invert the usual economics: a station that charges during such hours is paid to consume. A simple two-price calculation illustrates the scale. Charging 1 MWh of deliverable energy requires roughly 1.18 MWh purchased at the round-trip efficiency of 0.8464, so buying at deeply negative prices and discharging near the price maximum retains a wide margin even after the 15.4% round-trip loss.

A practical dispatch policy charges when the price is low and SoC headroom exists, discharges when the price is high and the SoC floor permits, and otherwise holds. Degradation enters as a per-discharge cost derived from the throughput coefficient of Equation (8.1), which discourages cycling during shallow price spreads; the same trade-off is formalised as degradation-limiting optimisation in [84]. Because the real-time controller in this thesis acts on voltage and loss objectives, price-driven scheduling runs on the slower tertiary timescale and supplies the SoC trajectory within which the real-time V2G commands of Section 8.3 must remain.

## 8.3 Coordinated V2G/G2V Frequency Regulation Strategies

Frequency control is organised as a cascade of three tiers with successively slower timescales. Primary control arrests frequency deviations within seconds through local droop response; secondary control, implemented as automatic generation control (AGC), restores nominal frequency over tens of seconds to minutes; and tertiary control re-schedules the participating units on a five-to-fifteen-minute cadence to restore reserves. EV fleets can participate in all three tiers, and the aggregator is the organisational device that turns thousands of vehicles into a single schedulable resource [11], [14].

Primary response is proportional to the frequency deviation. Equation (8.6) gives the droop law used for the EV contribution, where P_i^0 is the scheduled station power, f is the measured frequency, f_0 is its nominal value, and R_f is the droop coefficient in MW/Hz. A falling frequency increases net injection (less charging or more discharging) and a rising frequency does the reverse. The response is local and needs no communication beyond a frequency measurement, which is why the corresponding row of Table 8.3 carries the tightest latency requirement.

```eq
%%8.6
P_i = P_i^0 - \frac{f - f_0}{R_f}
```

Secondary control removes the steady-state offset that pure droop leaves behind. Equation (8.7) integrates the deviation into the AGC signal, and Equation (8.8) expresses the aggregated power balance of the interconnection, in which M is the aggregate inertia constant, Delta P_g is the primary-controlled generation response, Delta P_EV is the net EV contribution, and Delta P_L is the load disturbance.

```eq
%%8.7
P^{AGC}(t) = -K_I \int_0^t \left( f(\tau) - f_0 \right) d\tau
```

```eq
%%8.8
M \frac{d\Delta f}{dt} = \Delta P_g + \Delta P_{EV} - \Delta P_L
```

**Proposition 8.2 (Steady-state frequency restoration).** Consider the balance of Equation (8.8) closed by primary droop of the form Delta P_g = −Delta f / R and by the integral secondary law of Equation (8.7) with K_I > 0. If a quasi-steady state exists in which d(Delta f)/dt = 0 and the integrator output is bounded, then the quasi-steady-state deviation satisfies Delta f = 0.

*Proof.* At a quasi-steady state Equation (8.8) reduces to 0 = −Delta f_ss / R + P_AGC^ss + Delta P_EV^ss − Delta P_L, where all right-hand terms are constant at that operating point. Constancy of the remaining terms requires P_AGC^ss to be constant, so dP_AGC^ss/dt = 0. By Equation (8.7) with K_I > 0 this implies Delta f_ss = 0. Substituting back fixes the integrator output at P_AGC^ss = Delta P_L − Delta P_EV^ss, that is, the integral action absorbs the residual imbalance that primary droop alone would leave as a frequency offset. This completes the proof.

The proposition explains the division of labour between tiers. Droop response alone settles at a nonzero offset proportional to the disturbance; the EV fleet's fast power electronics make it a strong primary participant, but energy limits its sustained contribution, since a station discharging at its SoC floor must vacate the service. Integral secondary control then restores nominal frequency, and the EV aggregator tracks the AGC signal within the SoC window, recharging later at lower prices. Tertiary scheduling closes the loop by re-optimising commitments so that the energy consumed by regulation is replenished economically.

Aggregator coordination is naturally a mixed-integer programme, because participation is a commitment decision. Let p_{i,t} be the power of station i in interval t and u_{i,t} a binary variable that commits the station. Equation (8.9) minimises energy cost plus a switching penalty lambda_sw that discourages chattering of the commitment; Equation (8.10) couples the continuous power to the binary commitment through the station rating; and Equation (8.11) enforces the SoC window with the direction-dependent efficiency factor eta_p, equal to eta for charging and 1/eta for discharging.

```eq
%%8.9
\min \sum_{t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \left( \pi_t p_{i,t} + \lambda_{sw} |u_{i,t} - u_{i,t-1}| \right)
```

```eq
%%8.10
-u_{i,t} P_i^{max} \le p_{i,t} \le u_{i,t} P_i^{max}, \quad u_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}
```

```eq
%%8.11
SoC_i^{min} \le SoC_{i,t} + \frac{\eta_{p_{i,t}}\, p_{i,t}\, \Delta t}{E_i} \le SoC_i^{max}
```

The programme is solved on the tertiary cadence of five to fifteen minutes for the three stations of the benchmark, which keeps the integer search small (three binaries per interval) while still capturing commitment boundaries: a station at its SoC floor cannot be committed for discharge, and one at its ceiling cannot be committed for charge. The formulation follows the fleet-management optimisation patterns surveyed in [11] and the optimal-charging analyses of [10], adapted to the aggregated-station abstraction of Section 8.1. The commitment outputs then serve as the envelope for the real-time droop and AGC participation of the same stations.

Because load, renewable output, and availability are uncertain when commitments are made, the scheduling problem is cast in two stages [14]. The first stage chooses the commitments u before the uncertainty resolves; the second stage chooses the recourse powers p(omega) for each realisation omega of the scenario set, and Equation (8.12) minimises the first-stage cost plus the expected second-stage cost. The scenario set is generated by the probabilistic forecaster of Section 8.4, so forecast uncertainty propagates directly into the quality of the commitment rather than being discovered after the fact. Two-stage formulations of this kind are the standard market-facing abstraction for EV aggregators [10], [11].

```eq
%%8.12
\min_{u}\; C^{first}(u) + \mathrm{E}_{\omega}\left[ Q(u, \omega) \right]
```

Coordination at these timescales is a communication problem as much as a control problem. Table 8.3 summarises the requirements per service tier, following the latency analyses of load-based frequency control in [82], [83]. Primary droop tolerates no round trip through an aggregator and therefore runs on local measurements with a 100 ms bound; secondary AGC updates every one to five seconds; tertiary and market layers operate on minute-scale cadences with correspondingly relaxed reliability targets. These tiered latencies map directly onto the real-time task periods of the HIL deployment in Chapter 10.

**Table 8.3: Communication requirements for EV-based frequency control.**

| Service | Max latency | Update rate | Reliability | Architecture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary frequency droop | 100 ms | 10 to 100 Hz (local) | ≥ 99.99% | Decentralised, on local measurement |
| Secondary AGC tracking | 1 to 5 s | 0.2 to 1 Hz | ≥ 99.9% | Aggregator fleet controller |
| Tertiary re-scheduling | 1 to 5 min | Every 1 to 5 min | ≥ 99% | Central optimiser per aggregator |
| Market bidding and settlement | 5 to 15 min | Every 5 to 15 min | ≥ 99% | Market systems, offline |

## 8.4 Load Forecasting with Uncertainty Quantification

Every scheduling decision of Sections 8.2 and 8.3 consumes a forecast, and point forecasts discard exactly the information that commitment decisions need: how likely is demand to exceed the planned envelope? Probabilistic load forecasting addresses this by predicting quantiles or full predictive densities rather than single values, and its tutorial literature consolidates the methodological landscape [80]. In the CAPSM pipeline, probabilistic forecasts serve two consumers: the two-stage V2G scheduler of Section 8.3, whose scenarios must cover plausible demand and renewable trajectories, and the QIRL candidate evaluation of Chapter 4, which scores control candidates under representative operating conditions.

Quantile regression estimates the conditional quantile function directly by minimising the pinball loss, stated in Equation (8.13), where y is the realised load, y_hat is the predicted tau-quantile, and tau in (0, 1) is the quantile level [81]. The loss penalises under-prediction with weight tau and over-prediction with weight 1 − tau, so minimising it across a grid of tau values (0.05 to 0.95 in this work) yields a consistent estimate of the predictive distribution. The quantile bands are produced for load, wind, and solar series on the hourly OPSD data foundation, using the train, validation, and test splits fixed in Chapter 3.

```eq
%%8.13
L_{\tau}(y, \hat{y}) = \tau (y - \hat{y}) \;\mathrm{if}\; y \ge \hat{y}, \quad (\tau - 1)(y - \hat{y}) \;\mathrm{otherwise}
```

No single model dominates across seasons and horizons, so the forecaster is assembled as an ensemble with dynamic weighting. Members include a quantile neural network over lagged load and calendar features, a gradient-boosted tree regressor on price and weather covariates, and seasonal-persistence baselines that anchor the ensemble during regime changes. Equation (8.14) sets the weight of member m proportional to the exponential of its recent validation loss, with beta controlling the sharpness of the weighting; the softmax form keeps the weights normalised and prevents a single member from monopolising the mixture after one favourable week. The dynamic weighting also provides an early warning of distribution shift: when the persistence member gains weight, the data-generating process has moved, and downstream scenario sets are widened accordingly.

```eq
%%8.14
w_m(t) = \frac{\exp(-\beta \bar{L}_m(t))}{\sum_{j=1}^{M} \exp(-\beta \bar{L}_j(t))}
```

Scenario generation converts the predictive quantiles into discrete operating scenarios for controller evaluation. For each scheduling step the forecaster provides quantile bands, and the generator samples load, wind, and solar trajectories within and across these bands while preserving the empirical ramp statistics of the OPSD data, including the largest observed three-hour national ramps of +28,390 and −23,588 MW. The QIRL candidate set of 32 candidates per decision step is scored across the scenario sample, and the amplitude update of Chapter 4 aggregates the scenario-wise rewards; the reserved planning horizon of six steps is designed for multi-step scenario evaluation in future runs. This closes the loop between forecasting and control: uncertainty quantification is not a reporting accessory but an input to candidate selection and commitment quality.

## 8.5 DER Coordination Strategies

EV fleets are one constituent of the broader class of distributed energy resources (DERs) that includes solar photovoltaics (PV) with battery storage, flexible loads, and small-scale wind. Coordinating resources of this diversity requires hierarchical aggregation: a tertiary layer optimises over aggregators, secondary layers coordinate resources within each aggregator, and primary layers deliver local response, with distributed-optimisation methods coordinating across the hierarchy without centralising all state [46]. Peer-to-peer and bilateral-contract structures extend the same hierarchy to trading between aggregators [47]. The CAPSM control stack mirrors this structure: FACTS devices and EV stations act as specialised agents, the Metacognitive Arbiter coordinates their outputs, and the scheduling layers above supply SoC and commitment context.

Table 8.5 summarises the grid services that the principal DER types can provide and the metric by which each service is measured. The complementarity is the point: PV with storage is an energy-and-voltage resource, EV fleets are power-limited energy storage with strong responsiveness, and flexible loads provide cheap but coarse modulation. A portfolio is more valuable than any single resource because the services have different response, energy, and precision requirements, and no single DER class satisfies all of them at once.

**Table 8.5: Grid service provision capabilities of different DER types.**

| Service | Solar PV + storage | EV fleets | Flexible loads | Key metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary frequency response | Limited (fast but energy-shallow) | Strong (droop via chargers) | Limited | Response time (ms to s) |
| Secondary frequency restoration | Moderate (storage-backed) | Strong (AGC tracking within SoC window) | Moderate | Delivery accuracy (%) |
| Voltage support | Strong (inverter reactive capability) | Moderate (station reactive headroom) | Limited | Delta-V per MW injected (p.u.) |
| Energy arbitrage and peak shaving | Strong (storage) | Strong (V2G window) | Moderate | Cost saving (EUR/MWh) |
| Reserve provision | Moderate (state-dependent) | Strong (SoC-guaranteed windows) | Moderate | Firm MW available |

Power-electronic DERs displace synchronous machines and, with them, rotational inertia, so frequency declines faster after a disturbance in high-penetration systems. Synthetic inertia counters this by making inverters respond to the rate of change of frequency: Equation (8.15) gives the virtual-synchronous-machine law, where K_syn scales the emulated inertia constant [85]. The approach is effective but interacts with the remaining synchronous machines in ways that require small-signal care; analyses of low-inertia systems show that poorly tuned synthetic inertia can introduce oscillatory modes rather than remove them [86]. In the CAPSM benchmark the EV stations provide the fast power response, and the synthetic-inertia law is the natural extension path for their converters.

```eq
%%8.15
P_{syn} = K_{syn} \frac{df}{dt}
```

Resilience completes the service picture. Adaptive islanding splits a stressed network into stable sub-networks before cascading outages can propagate, using real-time loading and stability margins to choose the split points; DER-rich islands then survive on local generation and storage, with EV stations and battery energy storage providing frequency and voltage support until resynchronisation. As a design target for the framework, adaptive islanding is treated as a supervisory capability above the CAPSM real-time layer: the controller must recognise the onset of conditions in which islanding is preferable to collapse, and the HIL campaign of Section 8.6 verifies that EV coordination behaves correctly under the severe frequency excursions that precede such decisions.

## 8.6 HIL Validation of V2G Coordination

Offline analysis establishes feasibility; hardware-in-the-loop establishes that the coordination survives real-time constraints. The HIL campaign implements the EV stations as real charger models on the OPAL-RT simulator, built on the MathWorks File Exchange reference charger (entry #182226) and extended with a custom bidirectional converter model that adds PLL grid synchronisation, SoC-based power limiting, and PWM switching consistent with Simscape Electrical device models [102], [57]. Three such station models are connected at buses 3, 8, and 15 of the IEEE 39-bus network running at a 50 µs electromagnetic-transient step on the 4-core OPAL-RT target, with the same station parameters as the offline model (50 MW, 100 MWh, eta = 0.92, SoC window [0.2, 0.9]).

Realistic demand is supplied by the OPSD profiles. The January 2019 load and renewable shapes drive the network, and the charger duty follows the price-aware charging pattern of Section 8.2, so the fleet responds to dispatch requests on top of a real demand trajectory rather than a synthetic constant. The real-time model inherits the 20 ms PMU-based load profiles of the underlying eMegaSim implementation and maps the hourly OPSD shapes onto them by interpolation over the simulated window [57]. This construction keeps the offline benchmark and the hardware campaign demand-compatible, which is what makes the later offline-to-HIL comparison meaningful.

The V2G coordination test follows the ev_v2g scenario of the RT-LAB configuration. The run lasts 300 s of simulated time; coordinated V2G/G2V engagement begins at t = 60 s; and a frequency event is injected so that the fleet must transition between charging and discharging within the secondary-control timescale. The event is realised as a generation drop on a renewable bus (60% in the renewable-variability scenario), which excites the power balance of Equation (8.8) and exercises the droop and AGC paths of Equations (8.6) and (8.7) in real time. Measurements are logged through the I/O core at 1 ms resolution, including per-station power, SoC, and local bus frequency.

The campaign results, reported as HIL-campaign outcomes, support the coordination design. The fleet responded to the injected frequency event within secondary-control timescales, transitioning between V2G discharge and G2V charge without violating the SoC window: the mode-transition logic of Algorithm 8.1, executed in real time, held every station inside [0.2, 0.9] for the duration of the run. Station powers tracked their dispatch setpoints with the dynamics expected from the charger model, and the timing budget of the four-core allocation was met with the grid step fixed at 50 µs. These hardware outcomes are consistent with the Stage-1 finding that EV dispatch respects power and SoC bounds across consecutive steps, now verified under real-time execution.

Two limitations bound the claim. The station model is an aggregated, converter-level representation, so per-vehicle heterogeneity enters only statistically through the availability and degradation models; and the campaign verifies coordination behaviour and feasibility rather than market settlement, which remains an offline function. Within these bounds, the campaign demonstrates the chapter's central claim: coordinated V2G/G2V operation of the three stations survives the transition from offline optimisation to real-time hardware execution with its safety properties intact. The quantitative comparison of offline and hardware results, including the timing tables, is consolidated in Chapter 11.

## 8.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter developed the EV and DER layer of the CAPSM framework. The aggregated station model (three stations at buses 3, 8, and 15, each 50 MW / 100 MWh, eta = 0.92, SoC window [0.2, 0.9] with initial value 0.5) was justified against individual-vehicle modelling and equipped with stochastic availability based on a non-homogeneous Poisson arrival process, four behavioural charging clusters, and a throughput-based degradation model. Bidirectional power flow was given exact efficiency accounting: Proposition 8.1 derived the round-trip loss expression |P| dt (1/eta − eta), and the implemented mode-transition logic of Algorithm 8.1 inherits the proven SoC-bound invariance of Proposition 3.2 from Chapter 3.

Frequency regulation was organised in the primary, secondary, and tertiary cascade, with Proposition 8.2 proving that integral secondary control restores nominal frequency at any quasi-steady state, and with the aggregator's mixed-integer formulation and two-stage stochastic scheduling placing commitment decisions on an optimisation footing. Probabilistic forecasting supplies the uncertainty model through pinball-loss quantile regression and dynamically weighted ensembles, and the scenario generator feeds both the scheduler and QIRL candidate evaluation. The DER coordination view placed the EV fleet within the wider resource portfolio, with service capabilities per technology, synthetic inertia for low-inertia support, and adaptive islanding as the resilience supervisory layer.

The HIL campaign on the 4-core OPAL-RT closed the loop. Charger models with PLL synchronisation and SoC control executed the coordination in real time, responded to injected frequency events within secondary-control timescales, and maintained SoC feasibility throughout the runs. The chapter thereby connects the economic and physical modelling of EV flexibility to its verified real-time behaviour, and the quantitative offline-versus-hardware comparison is deferred to Chapter 11.
