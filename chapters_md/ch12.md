# Chapter 12: Discussion and Implications

The results of Chapter 11 establish what the CAPSM framework achieves; this chapter examines what those achievements mean and where their boundaries lie. The discussion proceeds from the limitations of the control system itself, through the regulatory and policy environment that will govern any deployment, to the practical challenges of implementation and the ethical questions raised by placing learning-based controllers in critical infrastructure. It then positions the framework against the state of the art and closes with the argument that the hardware-in-the-loop validation is the element that converts an interesting research system into a candidate for industrial adoption. Throughout, the distinction established in Chapter 11 is maintained between Stage-1 measurements, HIL-campaign results, and design targets, because the strength of each conclusion in this chapter depends on which class of evidence supports it.

## 12.1 Analysis of Control System Limitations

The most important limitation exposed by the Stage-1 rebuild is the constraint imposed by FACTS placement, and it deserves to be stated with candour. The blended CAPSM controller reduced violations by 1.20% while System 2 alone achieved 1.30%, so the arbiter's blending with the reflex path costs three bus-hours of performance relative to the pure deliberative controller, a small price for the reflex capability but a price nonetheless. More significantly, both figures are bounded by the electrical reach of the devices: the violation cluster at buses 22 to 29 sits far from the FACTS devices at buses 14 and 39, and no controller, however intelligent, can correct a voltage at a bus it cannot influence. The honest conclusion is that the measured improvement reflects the placement, not the ceiling of the control method, and that placement re-optimisation of the kind formulated in Chapter 5 is a prerequisite for larger gains. This limitation is a property of the study design rather than of the algorithm, but the thesis reports it as measured because it shapes how the headline numbers should be interpreted.

The second limitation concerns the fidelity of the inverter-based resource models in the offline stage. The Stage-1 environment represents renewable injection, storage, and fleet behaviour in a quasi-static sense, stepping hourly power flows with proportional redispatch and a curtailment rule, and this representation captures energy balance and steady-state voltage but not the fast dynamics of converter control. Electromagnetic phenomena such as converter interaction, control instability, and harmonic behaviour lie outside the quasi-static lens entirely. The HIL campaign partially compensates by exercising the controller against a 50 µs EMT model, yet the converter models themselves remain simplified relative to detailed vendor equipment models. The practical implication is that conclusions about energy and voltage management transfer well from offline to hardware, as Section 11.10 quantified, while conclusions about fast transient behaviour rest on the HIL campaign alone and inherit the fidelity limits of its models.

The third limitation is the hardware envelope of the validation platform. The four-core allocation, with System 1 on a 5 ms period, the arbiter on 10 ms, System 2 on 50 ms, and the EMT layer at 50 µs, was verified with margin, but it is an envelope for one 39-bus system with four FACTS devices and three aggregated fleet stations. A network several times larger, or a fleet with thousands of individual participants rather than three aggregations, would pressure both the computational partition and the communication fabric, and the 86.7% parallel efficiency at 16 nodes (HIL campaign) indicates headroom but not unlimited headroom. The single-scenario character of the Stage-1 evaluation compounds this: the offline benchmark covers January 2019 only, so seasonal variation, storm-driven extremes, and the 2015 to 2020 spread of the data foundation remain unexercised in the controller's evaluation record even though they are present in the data. A controller evaluated on one winter month has demonstrated competence, not proven it across conditions.

Finally, the deliberative layer does not yet plan with a model of the system's dynamics. The QIRL evaluates candidate actions against the current power flow state, the planning horizon parameter of six steps is reserved but unused, and no learned dynamics model exists to roll candidates forward in time. This is the difference between coordinated single-step optimisation and genuine multi-step planning, and it bounds what the deliberative layer can anticipate: ramps that are already visible in the present state are handled well, while ramps that require anticipation of a future state must wait until they arrive. The limitation is acknowledged in Chapter 13 as the leading algorithmic extension, and the reserved planning horizon is the designed-in seam where that extension will attach. None of these four limitations undermines the reported results, but all four define the boundary within which those results should be quoted.

## 12.2 Regulatory and Policy Implications

A learning-based controller that closes the loop on transmission equipment enters a densely regulated space, and the framework's properties map onto that regulatory landscape in specific ways. Grid codes and reliability standards express their requirements as measurable obligations: the frequency-response and frequency-bias obligations of BAL-003-1.1 [93] and the protective-setting requirements of PRC-024-3 [104] define what a control system must withstand and deliver rather than how it must be built. CAPSM's evaluation record speaks directly to this style of obligation, because the HIL campaign measured exactly the quantities such standards specify: frequency-regulation response characteristics in Table 11.9, behaviour under telemetry attack in Table 11.10, and timing budgets in Table 11.6. The interconnection standard IEEE 1547 [90] plays the analogous role at the distribution interface for the DER and fleet side of the framework, and the SoC-feasibility and mode-transition results of the campaign are the kind of evidence an interconnection review would request.

The certification pathway for AI-based controllers is not yet standardised, and this gap is itself a policy finding of the thesis. Conventional controller certification rests on linearised analysis, worst-case tuning studies, and type testing, none of which addresses a learned component whose behaviour depends on training data. The V-model traceability of Chapter 10 offers a credible template: every requirement descends into a design element, every design element is verified by a test, and the 52-test suite plus the HIL campaign constitute the verification record. A regulator evaluating CAPSM would find an unusually complete evidence chain, from the reproducibility apparatus of Appendix A through the statistical comparison of Section 11.1 to the hardware timings of Section 11.5, and the framework's failure behaviour, the Safe Mode demonstration of Section 11.9, addresses the question a regulator asks before any performance question: what does the system do when it is wrong?

The practical form of such a pathway can be sketched from the evidence structure the framework already possesses. A certifier would likely require three classes of demonstration: functional correctness against the standards' measurable obligations, which the campaign tables supply for frequency response and protective behaviour; failure behaviour under faulty or manipulated inputs, which the cyber-attack scenarios and Safe Mode demonstration address; and reproducibility of the evidence itself, which the pinned-data apparatus of Appendix A and the documented campaign protocol supply. What the pathway lacks is an agreed standard for the learned components themselves, and the thesis argues that the template already exists in how the industry certifies adaptive protection: bounded adaptation envelopes, recorded settings, and type tests at the envelope edges. Translating CAPSM's language to that template, the action box is the adaptation envelope, the blending weights and thresholds are the recorded settings, and the HIL campaign is the type test at the envelope edges, which suggests that certification of AI controllers is a translation problem rather than an invention problem.

Responsibility and liability allocations follow from that same record. When a learned controller recommends an action that an operator accepts, or executes under limited autonomy, the chain of accountability must be traceable to a verifiable design decision, and the arbiter's blending weights, threshold, and Safe Mode logic provide precisely that traceability in software artefacts. The advisory deployment phase proposed in Section 12.3 has a further policy virtue: while the controller advises and the operator acts, the responsibility structure remains the conventional one, and the transition to closed-loop operation can proceed only as fast as the evidence, and the regulator, allow. The potential for NERC-style compliance is real but conditional: the measured evidence covers the behaviour classes tested, and compliance claims beyond those classes would require the extended campaign outlined in Chapter 13. The policy environment, in short, rewards exactly the validation discipline this thesis practises, which is a strong argument that the discipline is not optional.

## 12.3 Implementation Challenges and Solutions

The first implementation challenge is integration with legacy infrastructure. Transmission control rooms run energy management systems, SCADA chains, and protection philosophies that evolved over decades, and a new controller must attach to them through standard interfaces rather than replace them. The framework's communication design anticipates this by conforming to the synchrophasor and utility communication conventions used in the HIL campaign, so the integration boundary is the data plane rather than the control logic. The practical pattern that emerges from the campaign architecture is a gateway arrangement in which CAPSM consumes telemetry streams and produces setpoints that the existing automation layer remains free to accept, modify, or reject, which preserves the legacy layer as the last line of defence rather than bypassing it.

The second challenge is the deployment sequence itself, and the thesis advocates a strictly phased one. In the advisory phase the controller runs in shadow mode, its recommendations displayed to operators while conventional control remains authoritative, so that the controller builds an auditable performance record inside the live environment without holding any authority. In the limited closed-loop phase it takes authority over a bounded subsystem, for instance a single FACTS device group or one fleet station, with the operator override one keystroke away. Only in the integrated phase does it close the full loop across FACTS devices and fleet resources. This sequence mirrors the roadmap formalised in Section 13.5, and its purpose is as much institutional as technical: each phase produces the evidence that justifies the next, and each phase trains the operators who must trust the system.

Operator trust deserves separate treatment because it is the binding constraint in practice. The Stage-1 and HIL evidence helps here in a specific way: the arbiter's behaviour is inspectable, since the blending weight responds smoothly to a published stress metric, the QIRL's choice is an interpretable probability distribution over evaluated candidates, and the Safe Mode state is unambiguous. A controller whose internal state can be explained in one sentence to a duty operator is easier to trust than one that cannot be, and the framework was designed with that explainability in mind, as Section 12.4 elaborates. The remaining implementation challenges are computational and communicative. The offline pipeline runs comfortably on a workstation, but the deployment path compiles the reflex network to the real-time target, and the ONNX-based export route identified in Chapter 13 is the practical bridge; the communication budget is set by the PMU reporting rate and the gateway latency, and the sub-10 ms detection result (HIL campaign) demonstrates that the budget closes for the 39-bus case, with larger systems requiring the partitioning analysis of Chapter 10 repeated at scale.

## 12.4 Ethical Considerations

Placing a learning-based controller in critical infrastructure raises ethical questions that are distinct from its engineering performance, and the first is the black-box risk. Deep components resist direct inspection, and an operator or regulator may reasonably ask why a particular setpoint was commanded. The framework mitigates this at the architecture level rather than by post-hoc explanation: the reflex path is trained by behaviour cloning from a rule-based demonstrator whose logic is published, the deliberative path's choice is a Born-rule draw from an evaluated candidate set whose reward values are recorded, and the arbiter's blend is a closed-form function of a published stress metric. The consequence is that CAPSM's decisions decompose into interpretable parts, and the residual opacity of the CNN-LSTM itself is bounded by its imitation objective. This is an honest mitigation rather than a complete answer, and the thesis does not claim otherwise.

The second ethical concern is accountability, and the design answers it with the override principle: a human operator can always assume control, and the Safe Mode state machine provides the mechanism by which the controller withdraws itself. The campaign demonstration of Safe Mode under attack (HIL campaign) is therefore not only a performance result but an ethical one, because it shows the system deferring to a safer default when its own inputs become untrustworthy. Data privacy is a third concern with a comparatively benign profile in this thesis: the Stage-1 work uses aggregated national and control-area data from a public source [42], contains no consumer-identified information, and the fleet model treats vehicles as aggregated stations, so the privacy exposure of the research design is minimal even though a production fleet aggregator would carry real privacy obligations.

Algorithmic bias is the fourth and most subtle concern. A controller trained or evaluated on January 2019 has learned the statistical signature of one winter month, and its behaviour under conditions absent from that signature, summer peaks, extreme storms, or a changed generation mix, is extrapolation rather than interpolation. The ethical implication is that deployment claims must be scoped to evaluated conditions, and the phased rollout of Section 12.3 exists partly to widen that scope under supervision before authority is granted. Bias here is not demographic but distributional, yet the remedy is the same discipline: evaluate on data that represents the served conditions, publish the scope of the evidence, and treat unevaluated territory as unknown. The thesis's labelling convention, separating measurements, campaign results, and design targets, is itself an ethical instrument, because it prevents the quiet inflation of evidence that is the characteristic failure mode of learning-based system claims.

## 12.5 Comparison with State-of-the-Art Approaches

Positioning CAPSM against the state of the art requires comparing architectural philosophies rather than leaderboard numbers, because the framework's contribution is a composition of control functions that the literature treats separately, and the reinforcement-learning-for-power-systems reviews [27, 28] document how rarely those functions are validated beyond simulation. Model predictive control brings explicit constraints and receding-horizon optimality but pays for them with model dependence and online computation; deep reinforcement learning brings model-free adaptability but in its canonical discrete-action form [23] struggles with continuous device setpoints, and its continuous-action descendants [24, 25] trade sample efficiency against stability of training. Safe reinforcement learning adds constraint handling by construction [29, 30], physics-informed neural networks embed physical structure in the learned model itself [31], multi-agent reinforcement learning distributes decisions across resources [69], and transactive designs coordinate through markets rather than algorithms [47]. Table 12.1 summarises the comparison, and the paragraphs after it draw out the three relationships that matter most.

**Table 12.1: Comparative analysis of CAPSM with state-of-the-art approaches.**

| Approach | Strengths | Limitations | Relation to CAPSM |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPC [67, 68] | explicit constraints, receding-horizon optimality | model dependence, heavy online computation | deliberative role of System 2; CAPSM replaces online optimisation with candidate evaluation inside verified budgets |
| DQN [23] | model-free learning from raw state | discrete actions, value overestimation, no guarantees | contrast case for QIRL, which needs no training data and yields interpretable candidate probabilities |
| TD3 [25] | continuous control with reduced value bias | sample-hungry, delicate training, no constraint handling | candidate for future reflex-path fine-tuning; CAPSM instead uses behaviour cloning plus arbitration |
| Safe RL [29, 30] | constraint satisfaction by construction | conservative behaviour, training complexity | CAPSM achieves safety structurally via action-box closure and Safe Mode rather than via constrained training |
| PINN [31] | physics-consistent learned models | training difficulty, scaling to large grids | embedded in the detection fusion chain; candidate dynamics model for future multi-step QIRL |
| MARL [69] | decentralised scalability across resources | non-stationarity and convergence conditions | natural extension path for multi-area deployment; CAPSM itself is a centralised dual-process design |
| Transactive and P2P [47] | market-based coordination of flexible resources | requires market design, latency, regulatory acceptance | complementary economic layer above CAPSM dispatch for fleet flexibility pricing |

The first relationship worth stating is that CAPSM is not a rival to these methods so much as a container for several of them. The deliberative layer solves, at every step, the kind of problem MPC would formulate, but it does so with a fixed evaluation budget of 32 candidates and a closed-form amplitude update instead of an online numerical optimisation, which is what allows the 41 ms hardware execution (HIL campaign) inside a 50 ms period; the reflex layer plays the role that continuous-action actor-critic methods play, but it is trained by imitation so that its first deployed behaviour is verifiably the demonstrator's behaviour. Safety is achieved structurally, through the convexity of the action box under blending and through Safe Mode, rather than through the constrained-training machinery of safe RL, and that structural route is what made the hardware timing evidence obtainable at all.

The second relationship is that the comparison clarifies what CAPSM does not do. It does not decentralise decision-making across agents as MARL does, and its centralised arbiter is a scalability boundary that the multi-area direction of Chapter 13 addresses through distributed optimisation [44]. It does not create market mechanisms as transactive designs do, and it would sit beneath any such market as the physical-dispatch layer. And it does not yet exploit learned physical models as PINN-based approaches do, although the detection chain already uses physics-informed components and the model-based QIRL extension would bring the framework closer to that family. An honest comparison therefore presents these approaches as an orthogonal design space in which CAPSM occupies a specific, defensible cell: centralised, dual-process, structurally safe, and hardware-validated.

The third relationship is evidential. Most of the approaches in Table 12.1 are validated, in the published literature, on offline simulation only, and the survey position taken in Chapter 2 argued that this is the field's systemic weakness. CAPSM's differentiator against every row of the table is therefore not architectural but evidential: the timing budgets of Section 11.5, the sub-10 ms detection of Section 11.6, and the Safe Mode demonstration of Section 11.9 were measured on hardware, and no architectural elegance substitutes for that record when the question is whether a method can be installed. The comparison table should thus be read as claiming parity or better on architecture and a distinctly stronger claim on validation.

## 12.6 Implications of HIL Validation for Industry Adoption

The HIL campaign changes the adoption conversation because it changes the burden of proof. A utility considering a new controller must satisfy its own engineering review, its regulator, and its insurers, and all three ask the same first question: has this system been run against a real-time model of the plant it will control, on hardware, under the events that matter? Offline simulation, however extensive, answers a different and weaker question, because it cannot exhibit interface delays, solver quantisation, scheduling jitter, or the consequences of a control period that is no longer soft. The HIL methodology [51], [52] answers the strong question, and the results of Sections 11.5 to 11.10 constitute the answer for CAPSM: every timing budget met with margin, every V2G target passed, every tested attack detected and recovered from, and the offline-to-hardware gaps measured and attributed rather than merely observed.

Traceability is the second adoption mechanism, and it flows from the V-model discipline of Chapter 10. Each requirement in the specification maps to a design element, each element to a test in the 52-test suite or to a campaign experiment, and each result in Chapter 11 back to a requirement; the certification evidence a review board needs is therefore an index away rather than a reconstruction project. This traceability also has a commercial dimension, because it makes the evidence auditable by third parties: the reproducibility apparatus of Appendix A allows an independent engineer to regenerate the offline results from pinned data and code, and the campaign protocol allows the hardware results to be repeated on any equivalent platform. Adoption processes move at the speed of trust, and auditable evidence is how trust is manufactured.

The third mechanism is de-risking in the literal financial sense. The phased roadmap of Section 13.5 asks the utility to grant authority gradually, and each phase gate is an HIL-class evidence package: the advisory phase requires the offline record, the limited closed-loop phase requires the timing and safety demonstrations of Sections 11.5 and 11.9, and the integrated phase requires the full campaign record including the consistency analysis of Section 11.10. Because the campaign already demonstrates Safe Mode on hardware, the most feared failure mode, a controller that misbehaves and cannot be stopped, has a measured answer. The design targets that the campaign did not independently verify, such as the economic figures quoted in Section 11.11, are precisely the ones the roadmap defers to operational experience, which is the correct epistemic order: hardware evidence first, financial extrapolation second.

A final qualification strengthens rather than weakens the adoption argument, because it states what HIL evidence cannot do. A controller-in-the-loop campaign validates the controller against a model of the plant, not against the plant itself, so parameter errors in the model remain a shared-mode risk, power amplification and protection dynamics of a full power-hardware experiment are not exercised, and the scenario library, however well chosen, is finite. The correct industrial reading is therefore that HIL evidence establishes eligibility for the next stage of scrutiny, pilot installation under advisory mode, rather than adequacy for unconditional autonomy. Framed this way, the campaign's role in adoption is precise: it filters out systems that cannot survive real-time constraints before they reach the grid, it prices the residual risk that remains after filtering, and it leaves a documented boundary between what has been demonstrated and what is being assumed. CAPSM's campaign was designed to leave that boundary as narrow and as clearly marked as the evidence allows.

The conclusion of this chapter is that the HIL validation is not an accessory to the research but the element that makes the research adoptable. It converts response-time claims into measured budgets, converts safety claims into a demonstrated fallback, and converts the offline study into a certification-ready evidence chain, and it does so against the standard question every reviewer, regulator, and operator will ask. The final chapter draws the contributions, limitations, and roadmap together on the basis of that evidence.
