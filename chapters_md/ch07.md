# Chapter 7: Fault Detection and Localisation

Fault detection and localisation is the third perception layer of CAPSM and the one with the tightest latency constraints. The controller can only respond to disturbances it detects, and on a real-time simulator the detection path competes for the same milliseconds as the control path. This chapter reviews the detection and localisation approaches that inform the design, specifies the multi-modal fusion architecture that combines phasor, SCADA, and learned features, formalises the model-based and learned anomaly detectors and their statistical guarantees, treats fault-type classification, analyses the response-time budget, and reports the HIL validation of the detection stack on the OPAL-RT platform. False data injection (FDI) receives particular attention throughout, because a detector that can be deceived by corrupted measurements is a liability rather than a safeguard.

## 7.1 AI-Based Fault Detection and Localisation Approaches

Classical fault localisation rests on two measurement principles. Impedance-based methods estimate the apparent impedance between the measurement point and the fault from phasor quantities and convert it to a distance along the line; they are simple and run on SCADA-rate data, but their accuracy is biased by series compensation, load imbalance, and remote infeeds [73]. Travelling-wave methods timestamp the surges generated by the fault at two or more terminals and triangulate the fault position from the time of arrival; they achieve high accuracy but demand high-rate sampling and tight time synchronisation, which historically limited them to transmission corridors with dedicated instrumentation [73]. Both families remain valuable, and the fusion architecture of Section 7.2 treats them as experts whose confidence varies with the event and the instrumentation.

Machine-learning classification forms the third family. Learned classifiers map waveform-derived features directly to fault types and locations, bypassing the explicit physical models and their systematic biases, and they have demonstrated strong accuracy on transient-stability and fault data alike [87]. The appeal for CAPSM is speed: a trained classifier evaluates in microseconds-to-milliseconds, whereas iterative physical estimation loops cannot. The corresponding risk is silent failure on scenario types absent from the training corpus, which motivates the multi-modal design: learned detectors are combined with model-based tests that carry statistical guarantees even where the learned detectors are unverified.

The adversary completes the picture. False data injection attacks corrupt measurements so that the corrupted data remains consistent with a plausible system state, and the seminal analyses showed that such attacks can deceive state estimation without raising any alarm [71], [72]. Cyber-physical testbeds were developed precisely to study these interactions under realistic timing and protocol constraints [76], and the OPAL-RT campaign of Section 7.6 follows that methodology. Any detection architecture proposed for a modern grid must therefore state not only what it detects but also what it provably cannot detect, a discipline this chapter adopts in Section 7.3.

Figure 7.1 shows the detection pipeline that organises these approaches within CAPSM. Raw measurements from the simulator and the sensor layer enter signal processing, where waveform features and phasors are extracted; learned encoders and classical estimators operate in parallel; their outputs meet in the fusion stage; and the fused verdict drives fault analysis, which assigns type and location. The pipeline is deliberately staged so that each stage can be validated and, where necessary, replaced without disturbing the others.

## 7.2 Multi-Modal Data Fusion Architecture

The fusion architecture operates at three levels. At the signal level, raw measurement streams are aligned, conditioned, and windowed so that downstream stages see comparable time bases. At the feature level, modality-specific encoders phi_i extract compact representations from each aligned stream, and the fused feature vector is the attention-weighted combination given in Equation (7.1), where x_i is the measurement stream of modality i, phi_i its encoder, and alpha_i the fusion weight. At the decision level, the individual detectors and classifiers deliver verdicts that the fusion layer combines into the final detection and localisation decision, with weights that the same attention mechanism governs.

```eq
%%7.1
z = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \alpha_i \phi_i(x_i)
```

The fusion weights are not hand-tuned but learned by the same soft-attention construction used in the System 1 encoder, stated in Equation (7.2). A small scoring network produces per-modality scores e_i from the extracted features, and a softmax normalises them into weights alpha_i that sum to one. The practical effect is that the fusion layer learns when to trust which modality: phasor features dominate during electromechanical transients, waveform harmonics dominate during arcing faults, and SCADA-consistency features dominate during slow developing anomalies.

```eq
%%7.2
\alpha_i = \frac{\exp(e_i)}{\sum_{k=1}^{M} \exp(e_k)}, \quad e_i = v^T \tanh(W_a \phi_i(x_i))
```

The modalities themselves differ sharply in rate and protocol, which is why alignment comes first. Phasor measurement units deliver synchronised measurements at 30 to 60 Hz under IEEE Std C37.118 [89], SCADA telemetry updates on the order of 2 to 4 s, and the time alignment across these streams rests on the Precision Time Protocol of IEEE Std 1588 [92]. In the HIL deployment the simulator provides the reference clock and the PTP discipline keeps the measurement layer within the synchronisation budget analysed in Chapter 10. The rate heterogeneity is handled by windowing: each modality contributes its freshest window, and the attention mechanism absorbs the resulting differences in information content rather than assuming equal reliability.

Figure 7.2 shows the fusion architecture with its three levels and the modality feeds. The design deliberately mirrors the fusion stage already introduced for the CAPSM architecture in Chapter 3, so the fault-detection stack and the general perception stack share one fusion formalism. This reuse reduces the certification surface: one fusion mechanism, one set of attention semantics, two applications.

## 7.3 Anomaly Detection Methods

The learned anomaly detector is the autoencoder of Section 6.4: the reconstruction error r(x) = ||x - D(E(x))||^2 of Equation (6.7) serves as the detection score, thresholded at the 99.7th percentile of the validation distribution. The model-based detector, in contrast, rests on weighted least squares (WLS) state estimation, the classical workhorse of supervisory control [74]. Given m measurements z and the measurement model h, the WLS estimate is defined in Equation (7.3), and at the optimum the normal equations of Equation (7.4) hold, where J is the measurement Jacobian evaluated at the estimate and W collects the weights w_i.

```eq
%%7.3
\hat{x} = \arg\min_{x} \sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i \left( z_i - h_i(x) \right)^2
```

```eq
%%7.4
J^T W \left( z - h(\hat{x}) \right) = 0
```

The detection statistic is built from the residual vector r = z - h(x_hat), stated with its normalised quadratic form in Equation (7.5). Large residuals indicate measurements inconsistent with the estimated state, which is the signature of either bad data or an attack. The statistical behaviour of this statistic under pure measurement noise is what gives the test its false-alarm guarantee, formalised in Theorem 7.1.

```eq
%%7.5
r = z - h(\hat{x}), \quad \chi^2 = r^T R^{-1} r
```

**Theorem 7.1 (Chi-square residual test).** Let the measurements obey z = h(x) + e with Gaussian noise e ~ N(0, R), and let the residual be evaluated at the true state, so that r = e under the null hypothesis of no attack and no bad data. Then the statistic chi-squared = r^T R^{-1} r follows a central chi-square distribution with m degrees of freedom; with the residual evaluated at the WLS estimate the degrees of freedom reduce to m - n for n estimated states [74]. Consequently, choosing the threshold tau_alpha = chi-squared quantile at 1 - alpha with m degrees of freedom, as in Equation (7.6), bounds the false-alarm probability at alpha.

```eq
%%7.6
\tau_{\alpha} = \chi^2_{1-\alpha,\, m}, \quad \mathrm{alarm\ if}\ \chi^2 > \tau_{\alpha}
```

*Proof.* Under the null hypothesis r = e ~ N(0, R). Whiten the noise by defining y = R^{-1/2} e, which is distributed N(0, I_m). The statistic rewrites as r^T R^{-1} r = y^T y, a sum of squares of m independent standard normal variables, which is by definition chi-square distributed with m degrees of freedom. The tail bound follows from the quantile definition: P(chi-squared > tau_alpha) = alpha by construction of tau_alpha, and the quantiles of the null distribution are tabulated, completing the argument [74], [117]. ∎

The theorem is the good news; the bad news is stated next, and it shapes the whole architecture. An attacker who knows the measurement model can corrupt the data in a direction that the estimator cannot distinguish from a legitimate state change.

**Proposition 7.1a (FDI stealth condition).** In the linearised measurement model z = Hx + e, an additive attack a that lies in the column space of H, that is, a = Hc for some state perturbation c, is undetectable by any residual-based test.

*Proof.* The corrupted measurements are z_a = z + Hc, and the WLS estimate, being linear in the data for the linear model, shifts to x_hat + c. The residual becomes z_a - H(x_hat + c) = z + Hc - H x_hat - Hc = z - H x_hat, which equals the pre-attack residual, so every statistic computed from the residual, including Theorem 7.1, is unchanged. ∎

This stealth condition, established in the FDI literature [71], [72], has a direct architectural implication: state-based residual tests alone are provably blind to an entire class of attacks, namely those that inject consistency-preserving corruption. The CAPSM answer is redundancy of principle rather than redundancy of sensors. The autoencoder score of Equation (6.7) is computed from the raw observation distribution, not from the estimated state, so a stealth attack that keeps the residual unchanged still perturbs the reconstruction error unless the corruption mimics the learned normal manifold in every coordinate. The multi-modal fusion of Section 7.2 adds a third, independent view through waveform-level features that never pass through the state estimator. Table 7.1 summarises the four detection principles and their complementary strengths and weaknesses.

**Table 7.1: Detection principles compared for fault and attack detection.**

| Method | Detection principle | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impedance based [73] | Apparent impedance to the fault from phasors | Simple; runs on SCADA-rate data | Biased by series compensation, load imbalance, and remote infeed |
| Travelling wave [73] | Time of arrival of fault-generated surges | High accuracy with high-rate sampling | Requires high-rate sampling and tight synchronisation |
| WLS residual test [74] | Chi-square statistic on the estimation residual | Statistical false-alarm bound (Theorem 7.1) | Blind to stealth attacks in the column space of H |
| Autoencoder score | Reconstruction error against the learned normal profile | Captures non-linear, non-topological anomalies | Threshold tuning; no physical guarantee |
| Multi-modal fusion (this thesis) | Attention-weighted signal, feature, and decision fusion | Detects residual-stealth and waveform-diverse events | Calibration and integration complexity |

The sensor-attack model used in the offline campaign implements exactly the measurement-layer abstraction of this section: the injection function corrupts observed voltage magnitudes at target buses by an additive bias plus small Gaussian noise and flags the observation, while the physical power-flow solution remains untouched, mimicking a sensor or communication-layer attack rather than a physical disturbance. This fidelity to the threat model of [71], [72] is what allows the offline FDI results to be compared meaningfully with the HIL campaign of Section 7.6.

## 7.4 Classification Algorithms for Fault Type Identification

Once an event is detected, the protection and restoration logic needs to know what kind of event it is. The fault taxonomy distinguishes symmetric faults, meaning balanced three-phase faults, from unsymmetric faults comprising single line to ground (SLG), line to line (LL), and double line to ground (LLG), together with high-impedance faults whose weak arcing signature evades simple overcurrent logic. The discriminating information lives in the sequence components and the harmonic content of the voltages and currents, which is why the feature extractor computes the symmetrical components of the measured phasors, stated for one phase set in Equation (7.7), where a is the 120-degree rotation operator. Balanced events concentrate their energy in the positive sequence; SLG faults elevate the zero sequence; LL and LLG faults elevate the negative sequence with and without zero-sequence content respectively; and high-impedance faults show low-amplitude intermittent second and fifth harmonics from the arc.

```eq
%%7.7
X_0 = \frac{1}{3}(X_a + X_b + X_c), \quad X_1 = \frac{1}{3}(X_a + a X_b + a^2 X_c), \quad a = e^{j 2\pi/3}
```

The classifier stack combines a gradient-boosted tree ensemble over the tabular sequence and harmonic features with a neural classifier over the fused waveform features of Section 7.2, and the fusion layer arbitrates between them. The rationale is practical rather than doctrinal: gradient boosting dominates small, well-structured tabular feature spaces and offers feature attributions that auditors can inspect, while the neural branch captures interactions among raw waveform features that hand-crafted statistics miss. Both branches consume the same windowed features, so the marginal cost of the ensemble is modest.

Table 7.3 reports the per-class detection accuracies of the classification stack. These figures are design targets from the framework specification and the HIL campaign, labelled as such: the prevalence-weighted aggregate of the per-class design targets corresponds to the 95.8 percent headline detection and localisation accuracy adopted in the framework specification. The ordering across classes follows the physics: balanced and strongly unbalanced faults carry large, unambiguous sequence signatures, whereas high-impedance faults remain the hardest class by a clear margin, which is why the specification assigns them a dedicated low-amplitude harmonic feature group.

**Table 7.3: Per-class fault detection accuracy of the classification stack (design targets).**

| Fault type | Discriminating features | Detection accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Three-phase symmetric | Balanced voltage drop; no negative-sequence content | 97.2% |
| Single line to ground | Elevated zero sequence; characteristic arc harmonics | 96.1% |
| Line to line | Elevated negative sequence; near-zero zero sequence | 95.0% |
| Double line to ground | Elevated zero and negative sequence | 94.6% |
| High impedance | Low-amplitude intermittent 2nd and 5th harmonics | 88.5% |

False alarms receive as much design attention as misses, because a detector that cries wolf will be ignored or disabled. The false-positive reduction mechanism operates on three levels: the multi-modal fusion requires corroboration across modalities before raising a protection-grade alarm; the sequence-component features separate load-driven depressions from fault-driven ones; and the alarm logic applies a short hysteresis so that a single noisy sample cannot trip the stack. The framework specification sets the combined effect at a 74 percent reduction in false positives relative to the conventional single-criterion detection, reported here as a design target for the HIL campaign to verify rather than as an achieved Stage-1 measurement.

## 7.5 Response Time Optimisation

Detection value decays with latency, and the latency budget is fixed by the HIL architecture: the fault-detection path must deliver its verdict within the 10 ms budget allocated to the arbitration and communication core of the four-core OPAL-RT allocation (Chapter 10), which in turn must fit inside the protective response window of the overall system. The response-time design therefore attacks three levers: the detection rule itself, the feature pipeline, and the deployment mapping.

The detection rule is a sliding-window test, and its latency has a hard, provable ceiling that we state as Proposition 7.2.

**Proposition 7.2 (Sliding-window detection latency).** Let the detector evaluate a sliding window of length W samples with stride s not exceeding W, and let it alarm at the first evaluation whose window contains at least one anomalous sample. Then an anomaly that first appears at sample index t0 is detected with latency at most s + 1, and therefore at most W + 1, samples.

*Proof.* The window evaluated at the first stride boundary at or after t0 contains sample t0, and that boundary occurs at most s samples after t0 because the stride does not exceed W. The evaluation and alarm add one further sample of processing, giving the bound s + 1 with s less than or equal to W. ∎

The bound converts a heuristic concern into a design constant: choosing the window length and stride fixes the worst-case detection latency independently of the event, which is exactly the property a real-time certification requires. In the deployed configuration the stride is one sample for the fast modalities, so the bound collapses toward its minimum, while the slow SCADA modality contributes through the fusion layer without gating the fast alarm path.

The feature pipeline is the second lever. The encoders compute incremental window statistics rather than re-scanning raw buffers, sequence-component extraction operates on the freshest phasor window only, and the fused representation is compressed to the modest dimension the classifiers consume. Feature compression here is not an afterthought but a latency instrument: every feature removed from the critical path buys margin inside the 10 ms budget. The deployment mapping, finally, assigns the detection stack its execution slots on the real-time cores, and the schedulability analysis of Chapter 10 verifies that the slots hold.

Against the conventional protection cycle, the framework specification sets two response-time design targets: a 37 percent reduction in response time to critical events for the System 1 path, and a 61.4 percent improvement over the best traditional rule-based response. Both are stated as design targets. Their plausibility rests on the architecture rather than on any single trick: the learned detectors evaluate in constant time, the sliding-window bound of Proposition 7.2 caps the waiting component, and the fusion layer is sized so that corroboration does not serialize the fast path. The HIL campaign is the arena in which these targets are measured, and Section 7.6 reports its detection-side outcome.

## 7.6 HIL Validation of Fault Detection

The offline machinery of Sections 7.2 through 7.5 was exercised on the OPAL-RT platform in the controller-HIL campaign, following the cyber-physical testbed methodology of [76]. On the simulated side, faults are injected through the network model, with three-phase short circuits applied via the admittance matrix and line trips applied through branch status switching, exactly mirroring the event functions of the offline environment. On the cyber side, FDI scenarios corrupt the observation layer with additive biases on selected bus voltage magnitudes, so the controller receives corrupted measurements while the simulated grid remains physical. The injected-event framework is identical in structure to the offline one, which is what makes the offline-versus-HIL comparison of Chapter 11 meaningful.

The campaign runs five scenario families, previewing the scenario matrix of Table 10.5: normal operation, physical faults, renewable-driven stress, cyber events (FDI on the measurement layer), and EV-charging disturbances. Each family stresses a different part of the detection stack: normal operation calibrates the false-alarm behaviour, physical faults exercise the sequence-component and waveform detectors, renewable stress probes the detector under high-variability but non-fault conditions, cyber events test the residual and autoencoder layers against measurement corruption, and EV disturbances verify that large but legitimate load transients do not trip the protection-grade alarms.

The headline detection outcome of the campaign is that sub-10 ms fault detection was achieved on the real-time hardware, within the budget allocated to the detection and arbitration path (HIL-campaign result). The timing budget of the four-core allocation was met with the detection stack resident, which was a prerequisite for the controller-level results reported in Chapter 11. We deliberately confine the claim to detection latency: localisation accuracy and per-class confusion behaviour under HIL are reported with the full statistical protocol of Chapter 11, including the offline-to-HIL consistency metrics (mean absolute error and relative gap) that the validation methodology defines.

The offline-versus-HIL comparison follows a fixed methodology, previewed here and elaborated in Chapter 11. For each scenario family, the same event definitions are executed offline in the Stage-1 environment and on the simulator, and the paired outcomes are compared on detection latency, detection accuracy, and false-positive counts. Agreement between the two environments across these metrics is the consistency evidence that justifies using the much cheaper offline environment for detector tuning, while the HIL campaign provides the certification-grade verification on real-time hardware. This division of labour is the same three-stage validation philosophy that governs the entire thesis, applied here to the detection stack.

## 7.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter developed the fault-detection and localisation layer of CAPSM around three mutually correcting detection principles. The model-based WLS residual test carries a statistical false-alarm guarantee, formalised in Theorem 7.1 through the chi-square distribution of the normalised residual and the quantile threshold of Equation (7.6). The stealth analysis of Proposition 7.1a established precisely what that guarantee does not cover: attacks in the column space of the measurement matrix leave every residual statistic unchanged, which is the theoretical justification for adding the autoencoder reconstruction score and the multi-modal fusion layer, since these operate on the observation distribution and the waveform features rather than on the estimated state. The comparison of Table 7.1 organised the four principles and their complementarity.

On the classification side, the sequence-component features of Equation (7.7) feed a gradient-boosting and neural ensemble whose per-class design targets aggregate to the 95.8 percent headline accuracy, with high-impedance faults correctly identified as the hardest class and a 74 percent false-positive reduction set as a design target for the fusion and hysteresis mechanisms. On the timing side, Proposition 7.2 converted the sliding-window detector into a certifiable latency bound of at most W + 1 samples, and the 10 ms HIL budget bounded the deployment envelope; the response-time figures of 37 percent and 61.4 percent are design targets awaiting the HIL accounting. The campaign itself achieved sub-10 ms detection on the real-time hardware (HIL-campaign result), and the offline-to-HIL comparison methodology hands a consistent evidence base to the validation chapters that follow.
