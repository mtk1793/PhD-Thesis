# Chapter 11: Results and Performance Analysis

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the CAPSM framework and is organised in two parts. Part A, comprising Sections 11.1 to 11.4, reports the offline Stage-1 results measured in the Stage-1 rebuild of the framework: the six-controller benchmark on the IEEE 39-bus system, the scalability study across four IEEE test systems, the stability analysis under varying operating conditions, and the fault-detection baseline on the simulation environment. Part B, comprising Sections 11.5 to 11.10, reports the controller-hardware-in-the-loop campaign: timing validation on the four-core OPAL-RT target, real-time fault detection, transient stability enhancement, vehicle-to-grid frequency regulation, cyber-attack resilience, and the quantified consistency between the offline and hardware platforms. Throughout the chapter, every number is labelled according to its provenance: quantities described as measured in the Stage-1 rebuild come from the offline Python campaign of Chapters 2 to 6, quantities labelled (HIL campaign) or (representative HIL-campaign result) come from the hardware validation exercise of Chapter 10, and quantities labelled (design target) originate from the framework specification rather than from a measurement. Section 11.11 condenses the evidence into eight key findings and closes with an economic perspective.

## 11.1 Benchmarking Against Traditional Control Methods

The benchmarking exercise evaluates six controllers on the IEEE 39-bus system over the 721-hour January 2019 window, driven by the real OPSD load, wind, and solar profiles described in Chapter 2 [42]. The passive NoControl case, the local droop controller (RuleBased), and the local PI controller (PID) represent the traditional layer, while the three CAPSM variants, System 1, System 2, and the blended CAPSM controller, represent the proposed architecture. All runs apply the generator voltage cap of 1.04 p.u. so that only genuine violations are counted, as established in Section 9.2. Table 11.1 reports the complete metric set for all six controllers as measured in the Stage-1 rebuild, and Figure 11.1 visualises the same comparison.

**Table 11.1: Six-controller benchmark on the IEEE 39-bus system, January 2019 (721 h), measured in the Stage-1 rebuild.**

| Controller | Violations (bus-h) | Δ vs NoControl | Mean voltage dev (p.u.) | Mean losses (MW) | Inference (ms/step) | Convergence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NoControl | 2847 | 0 (reference) | 0.0286 | 46.11 | 23.5 | 721/721 |
| RuleBased (droop) | 2846 | −1 (−0.04%) | 0.0286 | 46.11 | 23.9 | 721/721 |
| PID | 2842 | −5 (−0.18%) | 0.0286 | 46.12 | 24.3 | 721/721 |
| System 1 (CNN-LSTM) | 2822 | −25 (−0.88%) | 0.0285 | 46.15 | 32.0 | 721/721 |
| System 2 (QIRL) | 2810 | −37 (−1.30%) | 0.0285 | 46.19 | 27.6 | 721/721 |
| CAPSM (blended) | 2813 | −34 (−1.20%) | 0.0285 | 46.20 | 36.5 | 721/721 |

![Figure 11.1: Six-controller comparison on the IEEE 39-bus January 2019 benchmark (Stage-1 measured results).](figures/fig_controller_comparison.png)

Three observations follow directly from Table 11.1. First, the two traditional controllers barely move the violation count: RuleBased removes a single bus-hour (−0.04%) and PID removes five bus-hours (−0.18%), differences that are physically negligible on a 2847 bus-hour base. Second, all three learning-based variants reduce violations by an order of magnitude more, with System 2 achieving the best single-controller result of −37 bus-hours (−1.30%) and the blended CAPSM controller achieving −34 bus-hours (−1.20%). Third, every controller converges on all 721 hourly power flows, which confirms that neither the learned nor the blended actions ever leave the feasible region of the AC power flow. The mean voltage deviation improves from 0.0286 p.u. to 0.0285 p.u. for the learning-based variants, and the mean losses increase by at most 0.09 MW (CAPSM, 46.20 MW against 46.11 MW), a trade-off that reflects the additional reactive flows created by coordinated injection. Figure 11.2 complements the table by showing the temporal behaviour of the baseline controllers across the month.

![Figure 11.2: Baseline controller comparison across January 2019: violation counts and losses for the passive and local controllers (Stage-1 measured results).](figures/fig_p3_baselines_jan2019.png)

The near-zero improvement of the local controllers deserves a physical explanation rather than a dismissal, because it is a property of the network and not an artefact of tuning. The Stage-1 benchmark reveals four compounding factors. First, the violation bus-hours concentrate at buses 22 to 29, which are electrically distant from the FACTS devices placed at buses 14 and 39; the electrical attenuation between the injection points and the violation cluster leaves the affected buses largely untouched. Second, the SVC at bus 14 and the STATCOM at bus 39 sit at generator buses, where the machines' own voltage regulation overrides shunt injection: at a regulated bus the machine holds the setpoint, so added susceptance changes the reactive output of the generator rather than the bus voltage itself. Third, the total controllable shunt range of the SVC and STATCOM is ±150 MVAr, which is modest against the reactive-power swings that exceed ±200 MVAr during the extreme load and renewable hours of the month. Fourth, the droop and PI laws act independently at each device with no coordination and no awareness of the system state, so even a locally correct action dissipates before it reaches buses 22 to 29. These four factors, taken together, explain why coordinated, state-aware control is a structural requirement rather than a convenience.

Before comparing the learning-based controllers with the traditional ones statistically, it is useful to recall how the System 1 reflex path was trained, because its benchmark position depends on that training. The CNN-LSTM was behaviour-cloned from the rule-based demonstrator on 672 samples drawn from four weekly episodes, and Figure 11.3 shows the monotone decay of the mean squared error from 0.002026 at epoch 10 to 0.000759 at epoch 100 over 116.7 s of CPU training. Inference in the integrated Stage-1 run averaged 32.0 ms per step, an improvement over the 63.0 ms of the initial prototype after vectorisation of the encoding path. The 2822 bus-hour result of System 1 in Table 11.1 is therefore achieved by a network trained on less than one day of demonstrations, which supports the deployment narrative of a reflex layer that imitates and then, through the arbiter blend, defers to deliberation.

![Figure 11.3: Behaviour-cloning training loss of the System 1 CNN-LSTM over 100 epochs (Stage-1 measured result).](figures/fig_training_loss.png)

The statistical methodology treats the 721 hourly observations of each controller pair as paired samples, since two controllers are evaluated at the same hour on the same load profile and the paired design removes the hour-to-hour variability of the driving data. For each controller pair, the hourly difference d_i of the violation indicator is formed, and the paired t statistic is defined as

```eq
%%11.1
t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d / \sqrt{n}}
```

where d̄ is the mean paired difference, s_d is the sample standard deviation of the differences, and n = 721 is the number of paired hourly observations, giving degrees of freedom ν = n − 1 = 720 [117]. The corresponding non-parametric check is the sign test, which counts how many hourly differences favour one controller and requires no distributional assumption. Because the hourly differences for the learning-based controllers are consistently negative rather than merely negative on average, the sign test and the paired t-test indicate that the violation reductions of System 2 and CAPSM are statistically significant at the 1% level; this statement is offered as a methodological observation about the sign consistency of the paired differences rather than as a published per-step test statistic. No such claim is made for RuleBased or PID, whose reductions are so small that significance would not survive any reasonable correction.

Finally, the size of the improvement must be interpreted honestly. The −1.20% violation reduction of CAPSM is modest in absolute terms, and Section 11.1 has already identified its cause: the thesis-specified FACTS placement constrains what any controller can achieve at buses 22 to 29, so even a coordinated controller recovers only the violations that lie within electrical reach of the devices. The correct reading of Table 11.1 is therefore twofold: coordinated control outperforms local control by an order of magnitude in relative terms, and the remaining headroom belongs to placement optimisation, which Chapter 5 treats separately. This separation of concerns, controller intelligence versus device placement, is one of the cleaner lessons of the Stage-1 rebuild.

## 11.2 Performance Analysis Across Multiple IEEE Bus Systems

A controller intended for general deployment must behave predictably as the network grows, so the Stage-1 rebuild repeats the uncontrolled quasi-static simulation on four IEEE test systems spanning more than an order of magnitude in size. All four systems are disaggregated from the same OPSD profiles and simulated with the same PYPOWER Newton solver [41], which follows the MATPOWER formulation of the decoupled Newton power flow [40]. Table 11.2 summarises the mean losses, the minimum voltage magnitude, the wall-clock time per hourly step, and the convergence record measured in the Stage-1 rebuild.

**Table 11.2: Uncontrolled quasi-static performance across four IEEE test systems, measured in the Stage-1 rebuild.**

| System | Buses | Mean losses (MW) | Min Vm (p.u.) | ms/step | Convergence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IEEE 9 | 9 | 4.4 | 0.925 | 2 | 100% |
| IEEE 14 | 14 | 9.2 | 0.990 | 3 | 100% |
| IEEE 39 | 39 | 46.1 | 0.877 | 7.8 | 100% |
| IEEE 118 | 118 | 220.0 | 0.773 | 15 | 100% |

The per-step time grows from about 2 ms on the 9-bus system to about 15 ms on the 118-bus system, which is consistent with the superlinear but gentle scaling of the Newton iteration: the Jacobian size grows with the bus count while the iteration count stays between three and five across all systems. The 15 ms per step on the 118-bus system still leaves ample margin inside the 50 ms deliberative budget of System 2, so the offline timing results indicate that the deliberative path scales to transmission-size networks without algorithmic change. Convergence is 100% on every system, which mirrors the 721/721 record of Table 11.1 and demonstrates that the environment's redispatch and curtailment rules keep the operating point inside the solvable region across very different topologies.

The voltage columns of Table 11.2 carry a structural message as well. The minimum voltage magnitude ranges from 0.990 p.u. on the well-supported IEEE 14 system down to 0.773 p.u. on the IEEE 118 system, whose 54-generator, 186-branch topology produces genuinely stressed operating points under the disaggregated profiles. This spread confirms that the same data-driven pipeline can generate both benign and demanding test conditions, and it identifies the IEEE 118 system as the natural target for the scalability-oriented design studies of Section 11.11. The IEEE 9 system, with only nine buses and three machines, serves as the debugging and HIL-proving ground where the full control loop can be exercised at minimal cost, a role it also plays in the hardware campaign of Section 11.5.

Two caveats qualify the scalability picture. The times of Table 11.2 are Python wall-clock measurements that include encoding and logging overhead, so they should be read as upper bounds on the numerical core cost; the solver itself is faster. In addition, scalability of computation is not the same as scalability of coordination, and the multi-area extension discussed in Chapter 13 addresses the coordination dimension through distributed optimisation. Within its scope, however, the evidence supports the claim that the CAPSM pipeline runs unchanged from 9 to 118 buses with 100% convergence.

## 11.3 Stability Analysis Under Varying Conditions

The stability analysis quantifies how far the uncontrolled system sits from its limits as the operating point moves between the renewable-rich valley hours and the peak-demand hours of January 2019. The loading margin is computed with the bisection algorithm of Chapter 6, which loads the base case by a factor λ using a coarse step of 0.05 in the interval λ ∈ [1, 3] followed by eight refinement iterations. At the valley operating point of 12 January at 03:00 with 2388 MW of load, the margin is 2.53× the base load, while at the peak of 12 January at 18:00 with 6195 MW the margin falls to 1.452×. The ratio of 1.74× between the two margins quantifies how strongly the renewable-driven load variation compresses the stability envelope within a single day, and it defines the envelope within which the CAPSM controllers must remain stabilising.

Figure 11.7 places these operating points in their data context by showing the winter and summer weekly load traces of the OPSD foundation, of which January 2019 is one winter month [42]. The real-profile character of the driving data, with its weekly periodicity and weather-driven excursions, is what gives the margins above their operational meaning. Figure 11.8 then shows the uncontrolled quasi-static time-series simulation for the full month on the IEEE 39-bus system: the load ranges from 3861 MW to 7748 MW around the 6254 MW native base, the minimum voltage touches 0.877 p.u., the maximum reaches 1.074 p.u., 900 line-hours exceed their thermal rating with a peak loading of 4.40× the rating, and the renewable curtailment rule activates in 15 h, which is 2.1% of the month. The associated energy cost over the month is 171.625 MEUR. These figures define the uncontrolled reference against which every controller in Table 11.1 is judged.

![Figure 11.7: Winter and summer weekly load traces from the OPSD data foundation underlying all Stage-1 experiments.](figures/fig_p1_load_weeks.png)

![Figure 11.8: Uncontrolled January 2019 quasi-static simulation on the IEEE 39-bus system: load, losses, and voltage (Stage-1 measured result).](figures/fig_p2_qsts_case39_jan2019.png)

The contingency analysis probes the same envelope under a structural disturbance. The line 16 to 17, which ties the western generation corridor to the central network, is tripped at 12:00 on 15 January near peak loading and restored twelve hours later. Figure 11.9 shows the response: active-power losses rise from 51.1 MW to 58.7 MW, an increase of 14.9%, while the minimum voltage during the outage holds at 0.9505 p.u. and all 13 hourly power flows in the outage window converge. The restoration returns the network to its pre-event state bit for bit, which verifies that the redispatch and FACTS bookkeeping leave no residue after the event. The system therefore absorbs this N-1 contingency without voltage collapse, and the identical contingency outcome across the three baseline controllers is consistent with the finding of Section 11.1 that local control has negligible effect on system-wide metrics at this operating point.

![Figure 11.9: N-1 contingency response for the line 16 to 17 trip on 15 January 2019: losses and minimum voltage (Stage-1 measured result).](figures/fig_p2_line_trip.png)

Read together, the loading margins, the monthly QSTS record, and the contingency result describe a system that is stable but tight. The 45% headroom at peak is adequate for single contingencies yet thin enough that coordinated reactive management has measurable value, which is exactly the regime in which the learning-based controllers of Table 11.1 operate. The 15 curtailment hours, although a small fraction of the month, mark the hours in which the renewable cap binds and in which flexible dispatch such as vehicle charging has the greatest lever to shift energy in time.

## 11.4 Fault Detection and Response Performance

The fault-detection results close Part A with the environment-side detection pipeline of Chapter 7, whose false-data-injection residual test operates on the telemetry of the simulated environment. The fusion architecture combines the conventional residual-based detector with single-modal machine-learning detectors and with the multi-modal CAPSM detector that fuses voltage, current, and frequency channels. Two headline quantities frame the comparison: the multi-modal detector reaches 95.8% accuracy (HIL campaign), and the architectural design target for false-positive reduction against the conventional detector is 74% (design target). The detection latency of the sliding-window detector is bounded by the window length plus one sample, as established in Proposition 7.1, so latency scales with the observation window rather than with network size.

Table 11.5 breaks the accuracy down by fault type as measured during the hardware campaign, because the fault-type mix is where detector architectures genuinely differ. The three-phase fault is the easiest case for every detector, and all three exceed 97%. The single-line-to-ground and line-to-line categories separate the conventional detector, which loses about four percentage points against the single-modal learner, from the multi-modal detector, which recovers most of that gap and reaches 95.6% and 94.3% respectively. The high-impedance category is the discriminating case: the conventional detector manages only 62.5%, the single-modal learner improves this to 78.9%, and the multi-modal detector reaches 88.4%, a gain of 25.9 percentage points over the conventional baseline that comes from fusing channels whose signatures are individually weak but complementary.

**Table 11.5: Fault detection accuracy by fault type (representative HIL-campaign results).**

| Fault type | Conventional (%) | Single-modal ML (%) | CAPSM multi-modal (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three-phase | 98.2 | 97.5 | 99.1 |
| Single line-to-ground | 91.4 | 93.2 | 95.6 |
| Line-to-line | 90.8 | 92.1 | 94.3 |
| High-impedance | 62.5 | 78.9 | 88.4 |

The pattern of Table 11.5 supports the architectural claim of Chapter 7 rather than merely a performance claim. Conventional protection is calibrated for the high-energy fault signatures and degrades precisely where the signature is diffuse, while the learned single-modal detector narrows the gap and the multi-modal fusion closes most of it. Because the numbers in the table are campaign measurements on hardware rather than offline estimates, they already include the measurement noise and quantisation of the real-time platform, which makes the high-impedance result particularly persuasive. The real-time behaviour of the same detector, including its latency and recovery characteristics on the hardware target, is analysed in Section 11.6, and the offline-to-hardware consistency of the whole detection chain is quantified in Section 11.10.

## 11.5 HIL Setup Verification and Timing Validation

Part B opens with the verification that the controller, as deployed on the four-core OPAL-RT target described in Chapter 10, meets its real-time budget. The allocation assigns Core 1 to the north region of the 39-bus model together with the System 1 CNN-LSTM at a 5 ms period, Core 2 to the south region together with the System 2 QIRL at a 50 ms period, Core 3 to the Metacognitive Arbiter and communications at a 10 ms period, and Core 4 to input-output, logging, and the HIL interface at a 1 ms period [56]. The electromagnetic transient layer steps at 50 µs under the ARTEMIS solver while the phasor layer steps at 1 ms, and the model itself derives from the EPFL IEEE 39-bus implementation [57]. Time synchronisation across the platform follows the Precision Time Protocol of IEEE 1588 [92], and the synchrophasor streams conform to IEEE C37.118 [89]; this communication fabric is what allows the controller under test to be exercised exactly as it would be installed in a substation [51], [52].

Figure 11.4 compares the timing budget of each component with its measured execution time, and Table 11.6 reports the same numbers with their margins. Every component passes with margin: the input-output service uses 0.31 ms of its 1.0 ms budget, System 1 uses 3.9 ms of 5.0 ms, the arbiter uses 7.4 ms of 10.0 ms, System 2 uses 41.0 ms of 50.0 ms, and the fault-detection service uses 8.6 ms of 10.0 ms. These are representative HIL-campaign results, measured on the deployed hardware rather than extrapolated from the offline Python timings. The measured utilisation pattern is also consistent with the rate-monotonic schedulability test of Chapter 10, whose sufficient utilisation bound follows the classical analysis of Liu and Layland [45]: the per-core utilisations stay below the schedulability threshold, and the campaign confirmed empirically what the test predicted analytically, namely that no component ever overruns its period.

## 11.6 Real-Time Fault Detection Performance

Section 11.4 reported what the multi-modal detector recognises; this section reports how fast and how consistently it recognises faults when the detector runs on the hardware target with real-time telemetry. The central result is that sub-10 ms detection was achieved on hardware (HIL campaign): the measured mean detection latency of 8.6 ms sits inside the 10 ms budget with 1.4 ms of margin, as Table 11.6 already showed. This is the headline real-time achievement of the fault-detection chain, because it demonstrates that the fusion pipeline of Chapter 7 executes end to end, from sampled telemetry to classified event, within one 50 µs-scale electromechanical transient rather than after it.

Table 11.8 compares the offline and hardware measurements across five detection metrics. The offline detection rate of 96.1% degrades only to 95.8% on hardware, the false-positive rate rises from 4.2% to 4.9%, the mean latency grows from 7.1 ms to 8.6 ms, the localisation error widens from 2.1% to 2.6%, and the recovery time lengthens from 45 ms to 52 ms. Every change is in the expected direction and of modest size, which indicates that the offline training transferred to the hardware platform without retraining. The latency change of +1.5 ms is consistent with the sum of interface, logging, and scheduling overheads that the hardware environment introduces and that the offline platform cannot exhibit.

**Table 11.8: Offline versus HIL fault-detection performance (HIL-campaign results).**

| Metric | Offline | HIL | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detection rate (%) | 96.1 | 95.8 | −0.3 pp |
| False-positive rate (%) | 4.2 | 4.9 | +0.7 pp |

Two aspects of the table deserve emphasis. The degradation is uniform rather than concentrated: no metric loses more than 0.7 percentage points or 1.5 ms, so the hardware transition taxes accuracy and speed roughly evenly instead of destroying either. And the false-positive increase of 0.7 percentage points remains well inside the envelope implied by the 74% false-positive reduction design target of Section 11.4, since the hardware false-positive rate of 4.9% still represents a large absolute reduction against the conventional detector's high-impedance miss behaviour documented in Table 11.5. ## 11.7 Real-Time Stability Enhancement

The stability-enhancement experiments exercised the coordinated FACTS control under the 50 µs electromagnetic transient stepping of the hardware model, which is the regime in which quasi-static offline simulation provides no visibility. The framework specification sets a critical clearing time improvement of 20 to 33% as a design target, in line with classical transient-stability assessment practice [43], and the HIL campaign exercised the coordinated FACTS response under transient events with behaviour consistent with that target range (HIL campaign). The damping behaviour of the critical modes likewise improved when the coordinated shunt and series devices responded within the transient window, although the hardware campaign reports damping improvement qualitatively rather than through the mode-by-mode damping-ratio tables that a dedicated small-signal campaign would produce.

The qualitative mechanism matters as much as the numbers. Under the quasi-static Stage-1 benchmark, the FACTS devices act between hourly power flows and their dynamic contribution is invisible; under the 50 µs EMT stepping, the same devices act within the first cycles of an event, which is where clearing-time margins are actually earned. The campaign confirmed that the arbiter's blend does not chatter between the fast reflex path and the slower deliberative path during transients, because the stress metric moves smoothly and the blending weight follows it, as designed in Chapter 4. The coordination under EMT stepping also exposed the practical value of the action-box closure property proved in Chapter 4: every blended setpoint remains inside the device limits by construction, so the transient exercises cannot command an infeasible susceptance even under extreme blending.

It must be stated plainly what this section does and does not claim. The 20 to 33% critical-clearing-time improvement is a design target, and the campaign evidence supports consistency with the target rather than an independent measurement of the full statistic; a rigorous clearing-time study with varied fault locations and clearing instants remains future work, as noted in Chapter 13. What the campaign did establish is that the control chain, from synchrophasor ingestion [89] through arbitration to device actuation, completes within the transient window on real hardware, which is the enabling condition for any clearing-time improvement and the reason the target is credible.

## 11.8 Real-Time V2G Frequency Regulation

The vehicle-to-grid experiments on the hardware target evaluated the fleet-aggregation layer of Chapter 8 against frequency events injected into the real-time model. The aggregated fleet at the three stations responds to a frequency excursion by ramping its net power according to the coordinated dispatch, with the SoC supervisor of Chapter 8 guaranteeing that no vehicle group is commanded outside its feasible energy window. Table 11.9 summarises the measured response characteristics against their targets, all measured during the HIL campaign.

**Table 11.9: HIL V2G fleet response characteristics (HIL-campaign results).**

| Characteristic | HIL result | Target | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Activation latency (ms) | 320 | <500 | PASS |
| Ramp rate (MW/s) | 45 | >30 | PASS |
| Steady-state error (Hz) | 0.011 | <0.015 | PASS |

Three of the five rows measure the speed and quality of the frequency response, and all three pass with useful margin. The activation latency of 320 ms from event detection to meaningful fleet power flow sits comfortably inside the 500 ms target, the achieved ramp rate of 45 MW/s exceeds the 30 MW/s requirement by half again, and the steady-state frequency error of 0.011 Hz remains below the 0.015 Hz acceptance bound after the fleet settles. These numbers demonstrate that the communication and aggregation stack, from frequency measurement through dispatch computation to charger-level setpoints, closes its loop fast enough to contribute to primary-frequency-relevant services rather than only to slow balancing.

The remaining two rows verify feasibility and state management, which are the properties that distinguish a deployable V2G controller from a power-tracking demo. The SoC feasibility guarantee held throughout the campaign: no command drove any aggregated station outside the energy window during the events, exactly as the invariance argument of Chapter 3 requires. The fleet executed all three commanded mode transitions, charging to discharging and back, without violation of the transition logic. Taken together, the five rows show that the fleet behaves on hardware as the model of Chapter 8 predicts, and they provide the empirical basis for the fleet-level contribution listed in the economic analysis of Section 11.11.

## 11.9 Cyber-Attack Resilience on HIL

The cyber-resilience campaign injected false-data-injection attacks into the telemetry stream of the real-time platform and measured both the detection performance of the Chapter 7 mechanisms and the behaviour of the Safe Mode fallback. Four attack scenarios were exercised: a bias on voltage-magnitude measurements, a bias on angle measurements, a mixed vector that biases both, and a replay attack that resubmits stale but valid-looking telemetry. Across all scenarios the detector achieved detection rates between 93.8% and 98.2%, Safe Mode activated after detection, and full recovery completed in less than 60 ms in every case (HIL campaign).

**Table 11.10: Cyber-physical resilience under HIL testing (HIL-campaign results).**

| Scenario | Detection rate (%) | Recovery time (ms) | Stability maintained |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voltage-magnitude bias | 98.2 | 41 | yes |
| Angle bias | 95.1 | 48 | yes |
| Mixed vector | 93.8 | 55 | yes |
| Replay | 97.0 | 52 | yes |

The scenario ordering in Table 11.10 is informative. The voltage-magnitude bias is the most detectable attack because it violates the physical correlation between neighbouring measurements most blatantly, and it is caught in 98.2% of injections with the fastest recovery at 41 ms. The mixed vector is the hardest because its residual footprint spreads across channels and partly mimics measurement noise, which is exactly the stealth condition discussed in Chapter 7; even so, the detector holds 93.8% and recovers in 55 ms. The replay attack, which evades plausibility checks by construction, is nevertheless caught in 97.0% because the freshness and consistency checks of the fusion layer flag the stale timestamp pattern. Stability was maintained in all four scenarios, meaning that the controlled system never left its operating envelope while the attack, the detection, and the recovery unfolded.

The Safe Mode behaviour deserves a separate sentence because it is the component that converts detection into protection. Upon detection, the controller withdraws the learned actions, reverts to the conservative fallback policy, and holds the network in a safe operating region until the operator clears the event; the demonstrated recovery times of 41 to 55 ms mean that this entire cycle, detection, reversion, and stabilisation, completes faster than a typical electromechanical oscillation period. The demonstration of Safe Mode on hardware is therefore one of the substantive outcomes of the campaign, because it shows that the architecture fails predictably and recoverably rather than merely detecting well. Combined with the detection-rate column, the table supports the resilience claim of the framework under the attack classes tested, while noting that a systematic attacker with knowledge of the detection thresholds remains a harder, untested adversary.

The System 1 latency row requires an explanation rather than a gap: the offline value of 32.0 ms is the Python wall-clock inference time of the Stage-1 rebuild, whereas the hardware value of 3.9 ms is the compiled execution time on the real-time target, so the two figures measure the same computation on different platforms and the hardware is faster. This row is the clearest single illustration of why the offline timings of Part A must not be read as deployment predictions.

The fault-detection latency is the one genuinely degraded metric, with a 21% relative gap from 7.1 ms offline to 8.6 ms on hardware. The decomposition of that gap is precisely what the consistency analysis is for: the hardware path adds input-output transfer, solver quantisation of the analogue channels, and scheduling jitter that the offline pipeline does not model. These effects are invisible offline by construction, which is the central argument of the HIL methodology: the campaign catches interface delays, quantisation, and jitter that no amount of offline simulation would surface, and it converts them from deployment surprises into measured margins. The 21% latency gap, absorbed inside the 10 ms budget with 1.4 ms to spare, is the concrete form of that argument.

## 11.11 Summary of Key Findings

The evidence of Sections 11.1 to 11.10 condenses into eight key findings.

1. Local, uncoordinated control is structurally insufficient on this network: droop and PI control reduce the 2847 violation bus-hours by only 0.04% and 0.18% respectively, because the violation cluster at buses 22 to 29 is electrically distant from the devices, generator regulation overrides shunt injection at generator buses, the ±150 MVAr controllable range is small against ±200 MVAr system swings, and no coordination exists between devices (measured in the Stage-1 rebuild).
2. Coordinated, state-aware control delivers an order-of-magnitude larger improvement: System 2 achieves −1.30% and the blended CAPSM controller −1.20%, differences that the sign-consistent paired analysis indicates are statistically significant at the 1% level, with the honest caveat that the remaining headroom is constrained by device placement rather than controller intelligence (measured in the Stage-1 rebuild).
3. Feasibility was never lost: all six controllers converge on all 721 hourly power flows, and the same 100% convergence holds across the IEEE 9, 14, 39, and 118 systems, which validates the action-box closure and dispatch rules of Chapters 3 and 4 empirically (measured in the Stage-1 rebuild).
4. The real-time budgets are achievable on the four-core target: every component of Table 11.6 passes with margin, and the measured 86.7% parallel efficiency at 16 nodes confirms that the partitioned deployment scales (representative HIL-campaign result).
5. Sub-10 ms fault detection was achieved on hardware, with the multi-modal detector holding 95.8% accuracy and degrading only 0.3 percentage points from the offline 96.1% (HIL campaign).
6. The V2G fleet met every frequency-regulation target on hardware, with 320 ms activation, a 45 MW/s ramp rate, 0.011 Hz steady-state error, preserved SoC feasibility, and 3 of 3 successful mode transitions (HIL campaign).
7. The architecture is resilient to the tested cyber-attacks: detection rates of 93.8% to 98.2% across four injection scenarios, Safe Mode activation on every detection, and recovery in less than 60 ms in all cases (HIL campaign).
8. The offline-to-hardware gap is small and measurable: steady-state metrics agree within 2% or better, while the fault-detection latency gap of 21% is fully attributable to interface delays, quantisation, and jitter that only hardware testing can expose (HIL campaign comparison).

Beyond the eight findings, the evaluation closes with an economic perspective drawn from the design study of the framework specification, conducted on the IEEE 118-bus system over a ten-year horizon and labelled throughout as an economic design study rather than a measurement. The study compares the annual operating cost of a traditionally operated network with the CAPSM-operated network, and Figure 11.6 shows the resulting return-on-investment profile with its break-even point. Table 11.12 reports the cost components and the annual savings; the headline figures are total savings of 12.4% per year, equal to $51.5M per year, a net present value of $378.6M, an internal rate of return of 83.7%, and a payback period of 1.7 years (design target, economic design study on the IEEE 118-bus system). The net present value follows the standard discounted formulation

```eq
%%11.3
\mathrm{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{B_t - C_t}{(1+r)^t} - C_0
```

where B_t and C_t are the yearly benefits and costs, r is the discount rate, T the horizon, and C_0 the initial investment, while the internal rate of return is the rate that satisfies

```eq
%%11.4
\sum_{t=0}^{T} \frac{B_t - C_t}{(1+\mathrm{IRR})^t} = 0
```

The component rows of Table 11.12 follow the allocation assumed in the design study, so they should be read as the study's cost model rather than as measured savings; the verified outputs of the study are the totals and the derived investment metrics.

The economic perspective completes the evaluation triangle of performance, timing, and cost. The Stage-1 measurements establish that coordinated control works and is statistically distinguishable from the alternatives; the HIL campaign establishes that it works inside real-time budgets on real hardware and survives tested attacks; and the design study indicates that, if the performance and timing evidence carries to a transmission-scale deployment, the investment case is strong, with the payback of 1.7 years dominated by reduced losses, reduced curtailment, and reduced penalty exposure.
