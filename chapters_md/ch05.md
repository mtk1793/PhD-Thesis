# Chapter 5: AI-Based Optimal Placement of FACTS Devices and EV Stations

The control layers of Chapter 4 act on a set of flexible assets whose location, type, and rating are not given but must be designed. This chapter formulates that design task as a mixed-integer nonlinear programme (MINLP) and develops the optimisation stack that produces the device placement used throughout the remainder of the thesis. Four computational layers are combined: a genetic algorithm (GA) for discrete siting, particle swarm optimisation (PSO) for continuous tuning, deep reinforcement learning for online dispatch, and a quantum-inspired reinforcement learning (QIRL) scheme for online coordination. The chapter closes with the placement actually implemented in the CAPSM environment, the reasoning behind each siting decision, and an honest account of what the placement achieves and where it falls short when driven by real load and renewable profiles.

Three concerns shape the presentation. First, the formulation must keep discrete and continuous decisions inside one consistent mathematical programme so that installation cost, operating performance, and security constraints are traded off explicitly. Second, the algorithms must be described at the level of detail needed to reproduce them, including the operators of the GA, the update rules of PSO, and the candidate evaluation of QIRL. Third, every performance claim must be labelled as a design target or as a measurement from the Stage-1 rebuild or the HIL campaign, because that distinction governs how the results of Chapters 10 and 11 may be interpreted.

## 5.1 Optimisation Problem Formulation

The placement problem belongs to the class of mixed-integer nonlinear programmes because it couples integer siting and type decisions with continuous rating decisions through the AC power flow equations [4], [5]. Shunt devices such as the static var compensator (SVC) and the static synchronous compensator (STATCOM) modify the bus susceptance matrix, series devices such as the thyristor-controlled series capacitor (TCSC) modify branch reactances, and the unified power flow controller (UPFC) combines both effects [4], [6]. Electric vehicle (EV) stations add power and energy ratings that determine how much flexibility the dispatch layers of Chapter 4 can call upon at the bulk-grid interface. Because the AC power flow is nonlinear and non-convex in lossy networks [128], the resulting programme is non-convex even for fixed siting, and no exact polynomial-time solution method is known for the general case.

The decision vector collects, for each candidate device, a binary bus-selection variable, a discrete device type, and a continuous rating, and for each candidate EV station a bus index together with power and energy ratings. We write the decision vector as in Equation (5.1), where the integer part g^int contains the bus assignments and device types and the continuous part g^cont contains the shunt susceptance ranges, series compensation degrees, and EV station power and energy ratings.

```eq
%%5.1
x = \left( g^{\mathrm{int}},\ g^{\mathrm{cont}} \right), \quad g^{\mathrm{int}} = \left( z_{d,b},\ t_d \right), \quad g^{\mathrm{cont}} = \left( r_d,\ p_e,\ e_e \right)
```

The objective minimises, over the benchmark horizon of T hourly steps, a weighted sum of mean voltage deviation, normalised losses, limit-violation penalties, and annualised installation cost, as shown in Equation (5.2). The voltage deviation term and the violation penalty are defined in Equation (5.3), where N is the number of buses and V_m,i is the voltage magnitude at bus i. The weighted scalarisation follows standard multi-objective practice [97]; the weights are selected so that no single term dominates, and the violation penalty is set to dominate the deviation term in order to reflect the operational priority of keeping all buses inside the planning limits.

```eq
%%5.2
\min_{x}\ J(x) = w_V \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathrm{VD}(t) + w_L \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{L_t}{L_{\mathrm{base}}} + w_P \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Omega(t) + \sum_{d=1}^{n_d} C_d(r_d) + \sum_{e=1}^{n_e} C_e(p_e, e_e)
```

```eq
%%5.3
\mathrm{VD}(t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |V_{m,i}(t) - 1.0|, \quad \Omega(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \max(0,\ 0.95 - V_{m,i}(t)) + \max(0,\ V_{m,i}(t) - 1.05) \right]
```

The constraints fall into two groups. The equality group consists of the AC power flow equations at every bus, stated in Equation (5.4) in the standard rectangular-admittance form used by the solver throughout this thesis. The inequality group collects the operational limits stated in Equation (5.5): bus voltage magnitudes between 0.95 and 1.05 p.u., branch thermal limits, the rating boxes of each device, and the state-of-charge (SoC) window of each EV station. The round-trip efficiency of 0.92 (one-way) and the SoC window [0.2, 0.9] enter through the SoC dynamics defined in Chapter 3, so the placement layer sizes the stations while the dispatch layers respect their electrochemical limits.

```eq
%%5.4
P_i = V_i \sum_{j} V_j (G_{ij} \cos\theta_{ij} + B_{ij} \sin\theta_{ij}), \quad Q_i = V_i \sum_{j} V_j (G_{ij} \sin\theta_{ij} - B_{ij} \cos\theta_{ij})
```

```eq
%%5.5
0.95 \le V_{m,i} \le 1.05, \quad |P_{ij}| \le P_{ij}^{\max}, \quad r_d^{\min} \le r_d \le r_d^{\max}, \quad 0.2 \le \mathrm{SoC}_{e,t} \le 0.9
```

The problem complexity deserves an explicit statement, because it motivates every algorithmic choice in this chapter. For the IEEE 39 system, selecting four device slots from 39 candidate buses with three shunt type options and two series type options yields tens of millions of distinct type-bus assignments before any continuous rating is considered, and each continuous rating multiplies the search space by a continuum. Every candidate evaluation requires at least one AC power flow, which the Stage-1 environment performs in approximately 7.8 ms per step for the IEEE 39 system (measured in the Stage-1 rebuild), so exhaustive enumeration is infeasible even offline. The placement problem is therefore attacked with the layered decomposition summarised in Table 5.1, which separates discrete search from continuous tuning and both from real-time dispatch.

**Table 5.1: Optimisation layers of the CAPSM planning and control stack.**

| Optimisation layer | Method | Decision variables | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Placement | Genetic algorithm [64, 65] | Integer bus and type genes; continuous rating genes | Device buses, types, and ratings |
| Tuning | Particle swarm optimisation [66, 114] | Continuous setpoints (susceptance ranges, compensation degrees, droop gains) | Tuned device parameters and operating ranges |
| Dispatch | TD3 actor-critic [24, 25] (reference design) | Continuous 9-dimensional action vector | Per-step FACTS and EV setpoints |
| Online coordination | QIRL (this thesis) | Candidate action set of 32 per step | Selected action via Born-rule sampling |

The layers interact in a strictly nested manner. The GA returns a siting and rating proposal whose feasibility is verified by AC power flow; PSO then refines the continuous parameters with the topology fixed; the tuned ratings define the action box that the online layers of Chapter 4 operate within; and the online layers, in turn, report back operating statistics that a future re-optimisation of the placement can exploit, as discussed in Section 5.7 and Chapter 12. This nesting keeps each subproblem tractable while preserving the coupling that makes coordinated placement valuable in the first place.

## 5.2 Genetic Algorithm Based Placement

The GA is chosen for the siting layer because it searches arbitrary discrete spaces without continuity or convexity assumptions and tolerates the black-box fitness function that the AC power flow imposes [64], [65]. A candidate solution is a chromosome that concatenates an integer segment with a continuous segment, as expressed in Equation (5.6). The integer genes hold the bus index and device type of each device slot, and the continuous genes hold the shunt susceptance range, the series compensation degree, and the EV station power and energy ratings. Repair operators enforce bus uniqueness and type compatibility after every genetic operation, so that every decoded chromosome corresponds to a physically installable device set.

```eq
%%5.6
g = \left[ g_1^{\mathrm{int}}, \ldots, g_m^{\mathrm{int}} \,|\, g_1^{\mathrm{cont}}, \ldots, g_q^{\mathrm{cont}} \right]
```

Selection uses fitness-proportionate roulette with elitism. The fitness of individual i is defined in Equation (5.7) as the reciprocal of a penalised objective, where J(x_i) is the placement objective of Equation (5.2) and Lambda(x_i) aggregates constraint violations. The best E individuals are copied into the next generation unchanged, which guarantees monotone non-degradation of the elite fitness and protects the search from losing high-quality sitings to stochastic operators.

```eq
%%5.7
p_i = \frac{f_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{P} f_j}, \quad f_i = \frac{1}{1 + J(x_i) + \Lambda(x_i)}
```

Variation operators act on the two segments differently. Single-point crossover is applied independently to the integer and continuous segments, exchanging tail material between two parents while the repair operator restores feasibility. Mutation perturbs the continuous genes with Gaussian noise and clamps the result into the rating box, as stated in Equation (5.8), while integer genes are re-drawn uniformly from the feasible bus or type sets with a small probability. The asymmetric treatment reflects the structure of the search space: continuous ratings need local refinement, whereas siting benefits from occasional restarts.

```eq
%%5.8
g_k' = \mathrm{clip}\left( g_k + \sigma\, \epsilon_k,\ g_k^{\min},\ g_k^{\max} \right), \quad \epsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
```

Figure 5.1 depicts the complete workflow, from chromosome encoding through the genetic operators to the convergence test and the hand-off of the winning placement to the PSO tuning stage of Section 5.3. Each fitness evaluation invokes the PYPOWER AC power flow inside the Stage-1 environment, so a candidate is never scored on an approximate model. The convergence test halts the evolution when the elite fitness stagnates across a patience window, at which point the best feasible chromosome is decoded into the device set.

![Figure 5.1: GA-based FACTS placement workflow with the PSO tuning stage.](figures/fig_ga_pso_flow.png)

Regarding convergence, we adopt the classical informal argument rather than a formal proof. Holland's schema theorem indicates that short, low-order building blocks whose average fitness exceeds the population average receive an exponentially increasing share of trials in expectation over successive generations [64]. This result supports the empirical effectiveness of roulette selection with elitism but does not constitute a global-optimality guarantee, and we therefore treat the GA strictly as a heuristic search whose output is verified by direct power-flow evaluation of the returned placement [65]. Within that framing, elitism supplies a weak monotonicity property (the elite fitness never decreases), which is the only convergence statement we rely on in practice.

## 5.3 Particle Swarm Optimisation Based Tuning

Once the GA fixes the topology, the remaining decisions are continuous: the susceptance ranges of the shunt devices, the compensation degree of the TCSC, the droop gains of the local voltage controllers, and the EV station setpoint bands. PSO is well suited to this refinement because it requires no gradient information, tolerates the box constraints naturally, and converges quickly on low-dimensional continuous spaces [66]. Each particle carries a position x_i that encodes one complete parameter vector and a velocity v_i that accumulates search momentum across iterations.

The velocity and position updates are given in Equations (5.9) and (5.10). Here w is the inertia weight, c_1 and c_2 are the cognitive and social coefficients, r_1 and r_2 are uniform random numbers in [0, 1], p_i is the personal best position of particle i, and g is the global best position found by the swarm. Positions are clamped to the device rating boxes after every update, and velocities are clamped to a fraction of the box width to prevent overshoot.

```eq
%%5.9
v_i^{(t+1)} = w\, v_i^{(t)} + c_1 r_1 \left( p_i - x_i^{(t)} \right) + c_2 r_2 \left( g - x_i^{(t)} \right)
```

```eq
%%5.10
x_i^{(t+1)} = x_i^{(t)} + v_i^{(t+1)}
```

The inertia weight governs the balance between global exploration and local exploitation. Eberhart and Shi demonstrated that a large initial inertia weight near 0.9 favours global search early in the run, while a linear decay toward approximately 0.4 tightens local convergence in the final iterations [114]. Under decaying inertia and velocity clamping, empirical studies report consistent convergence to neighbourhood optima, although no global-optimality guarantee exists for the general non-convex fitness landscape [66], [114]. We therefore state the convergence behaviour of PSO as an empirical property supported by the cited studies, and we verify every tuned parameter set by power-flow evaluation before adoption, exactly as for the GA placement.

The objective handed to the swarm is the placement objective of Equation (5.2) evaluated with the topology fixed, so tuning and placement optimise the same quantity on nested domains. The particle positions initialise inside the device rating boxes so that every iterate is feasible by construction. As a point of reference, the Stage-1 baseline controllers of Chapter 3 employ a fixed droop gain of Kp = 5 with V_ref = 1.0 p.u.; PSO can refine such gains within the small-signal stability bound derived in Section 5.7, which caps the admissible gain by the local conductance seen at each placement bus. This coupling between the tuning layer and the stability analysis is what keeps aggressive gain values out of the deployed configurations.

## 5.4 Deep Reinforcement Learning Controller Design

The dispatch layer asks a different question from placement and tuning: given the installed devices and the current system state, which setpoints should be applied at this step? Deep reinforcement learning answers this question by learning a policy from interaction, and two algorithm families dominate the literature. Value-based methods such as deep Q-networks (DQN) handle discrete action sets by learning action values and selecting greedily [23]. Actor-critic methods extend to continuous action spaces, with DDPG introducing the deterministic policy gradient for continuous control [24] and TD3 addressing its overestimation bias with three mechanisms: twin critics, delayed policy updates, and target policy smoothing [25].

The TD3 architecture, described here in text, consists of a deterministic actor network and two critic networks, each with a slowly updated target copy, all trained from a replay buffer of transitions. The critic regression target is given in Equation (5.11), where the minimum over the two target critics combats value overestimation and the clipped Gaussian noise term implements target policy smoothing. The actor ascends the deterministic policy gradient stated in Equation (5.12), and the actor together with all target networks is updated only every second critic step, which is the delayed-update mechanism that stabilises training [25].

```eq
%%5.11
y = r + \gamma \min_{j=1,2} Q_{\theta_j'}\left( s',\ \pi_{\phi'}(s') + \epsilon \right), \quad \epsilon = \mathrm{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), -c, c)
```

```eq
%%5.12
\nabla_{\phi} J = \mathrm{E}_{s}\left[ \nabla_{a} Q_{\theta_1}(s, a)\, \nabla_{\phi} \pi_{\phi}(s) \right]_{a=\pi_{\phi}(s)}
```

TD3 was considered seriously as the System 2 dispatch design because the CAPSM action space is a nine-dimensional continuous vector of FACTS susceptances, compensation degrees, and EV station powers, which is precisely the regime TD3 was developed for [24], [25]. Exploration by Gaussian policy noise maps naturally onto operator-acceptable setpoint perturbations, and the twin-critic construction suppresses the optimistic value bias that destabilises DDPG on power-system reward landscapes [25]. The TD3 reference design shares the same nine-dimensional action box as the deployed controller, so results obtained under either design remain directly comparable.

Four considerations led to the adoption of QIRL rather than TD3 for the deployed System 2. First, TD3 requires a large replay corpus harvested from extensive training interaction, whereas QIRL evaluates candidates against model-based rewards computed from power-flow metrics and needs no training data at all. Second, inference cost matters at real-time rates: the deployed QIRL step takes 21.1 ms in the dedicated benchmark and 27.6 ms in the full January 2019 closed-loop runs (both measured in the Stage-1 rebuild), whereas each TD3 decision requires an actor forward pass plus two critic evaluations before the same power-flow step. Third, interpretability favours QIRL: every candidate action carries an auditable reward decomposition into voltage deviation, violation count, and action magnitude, while a critic network offers no such audit trail. Fourth, the offline Stage-1 validation environment is deterministic enough that model-based candidate evaluation is reliable, which removes the main argument for learning a value function from data. TD3 is therefore retained as the reference continuous-control design in Table 5.1, and the comparative assessment of Section 5.5 quantifies the trade-off.

## 5.5 Quantum-Enhanced Reinforcement Learning for Optimal Power Flow

The deployed System 2 treats each operating-point decision as a small optimal power flow (OPF) over the action box and solves it by quantum-inspired reinforcement learning [58], [59]. The algorithm maintains a population of n_c = 32 candidate actions, drawn uniformly in the action box at initialisation, and evaluates each candidate against the model-based reward of Equation (5.13), which penalises voltage deviation, limit violations, and action magnitude with the coefficients calibrated in Chapter 4. A planning-horizon parameter of six steps is reserved in the implementation; the reported runs use single-step evaluation, and we state this openly to keep the description faithful to the code.

```eq
%%5.13
r_i = -\mathrm{mean}|V_m - 1| - 0.05\, n_{\mathrm{viol}} - 0.001\, ||a_i||_2^2
```

The candidates are then encoded into the amplitudes of a normalised superposition state. After shifting the rewards by their maximum, the amplitude of candidate i is updated according to Equation (5.14) with inverse temperature beta = 5.0, which is exactly the computation performed by the implementation and is equivalent to reinforcing amplitudes proportionally to exp(beta r_i / 2) followed by L2 normalisation [59], [60]. The action is selected by the Born rule of Equation (5.15), so high-reward candidates are sampled with high probability while all candidates retain nonzero probability mass, which preserves exploration without an epsilon schedule [58], [61]. Tunnelling completes the mechanism: each step replaces 10 percent of the candidates by uniform re-initialisation, allowing the population to escape local optima in the reward landscape rather than collapsing onto the first attractive basin.

```eq
%%5.14
\alpha_i^{(t+1)} = \frac{\exp(\beta r_i / 2)}{\sqrt{\sum_{j} \exp(\beta r_j)}}
```

```eq
%%5.15
P(a_i) = |\alpha_i|^2
```

The word superposition is used deliberately. Because all 32 candidate rewards are computed and folded into the amplitudes in a single combined update, the scheme mimics the simultaneous evaluation of a quantum superposition of actions, in contrast to the sequential action-by-action evaluation of DQN over a discrete grid [23]. The initialisation sets all amplitudes to 1/sqrt(32), so the first decision is uniformly random and the distribution sharpens as evidence accumulates. This is the sense in which the method is quantum-inspired rather than quantum: it borrows the amplitude, Born-rule, and tunnelling vocabulary of quantum mechanics [58] while running on classical hardware, with photonic and gate-based implementations remaining a hardware outlook as variational quantum processors mature [62], [63].

Table 5.3 compares the candidate dispatch methods on the axes that matter for deployment: exploration mechanism, training-data requirement, inference cost, and suitability. The comparison makes the trade-off of Section 5.4 explicit. DQN and TD3 amortise a large training investment into a fast learned policy but demand replay corpora and multiple network evaluations per decision, whereas QIRL and the evolutionary layers need no training data but pay for model-based evaluation at every step. For a controller that must be validated, audited, and re-certified on a real-time simulator, the QIRL column is the decisive one.

**Table 5.3: Dispatch and optimisation methods compared for real-time coordinated control.**

| Method | Exploration mechanism | Training data needed | Inference cost | Suitability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DQN [23] | epsilon-greedy over discrete actions | Large replay corpus from training episodes | One network forward pass per action | Discrete switching decisions |
| TD3 [25] | Gaussian policy noise on continuous actions | Large replay corpus from training episodes | Actor plus two critic forward passes | Continuous modulation setpoints |
| QIRL (this thesis) | Amplitude superposition over 32 candidates; 10 percent tunnelling | None; model-based candidate rewards | 32 candidate evaluations per step; 21.1 ms measured in the Stage-1 rebuild | Real-time coordinated dispatch without training data |
| GA and PSO [64, 66] | Population sampling with mutation and inertia | None; fitness via power flow | Population-sized offline evaluations | Planning and tuning, not real-time dispatch |

On convergence speed, the framework specification sets a design target that QIRL converges 58 to 59 percent faster than DQN on the dispatch task. We report this strictly as a design target rather than a Stage-1 measurement, and we offer the mechanistic reading that the parallel candidate evaluation and the tunnelling replacements remove the two main sources of slow convergence in value-based methods, namely sequential action evaluation and premature collapse onto a single action basin [59], [60]. The Stage-1 measurements that do exist concern inference time and closed-loop performance, and those are reported with their own labels in Section 5.7.

## 5.6 Transfer Learning for Cross-Domain Knowledge Transfer

Scaling the framework from the IEEE 39 system to the IEEE 118 system, which carries 118 buses, 54 generators, and 186 branches, raises the question of whether every enlargement must restart training from scratch. Transfer learning answers negatively by carrying learned representations across domains [79]. The encoder trunk of the System 1 network, comprising the convolutional feature extractor and the LSTM temporal layers, is trained on the source system and then frozen; only the task heads are retrained on the target system. Freezing the lower layers preserves the general voltage-and-flow features that both systems share, while retraining the heads absorbs the topology-specific aspects of the target network.

The transfer objective is stated in Equation (5.16), where L_T is the target-task loss and Omega is an anchoring regulariser that penalises the deviation of the fine-tuned parameters from their source values theta_S. This decomposition makes the trade-off explicit: without the anchor, fine-tuning on a small target dataset overwrites the transferred features; with it, the optimiser adapts the heads while the shared representation remains intact [79].

```eq
%%5.16
L(\theta) = L_T(\theta) + \lambda\, \Omega(\theta), \quad \Omega(\theta) = ||\theta - \theta_S||^2
```

Domain change in power systems is primarily a change of graph structure, so the transfer is a form of domain adaptation in which the feature spaces must be realigned across different admittance matrices [79]. We treat the expected gains with deliberate caution. The framework specification sets a design target of a 73 percent reduction in data requirements for the target system when the frozen-encoder procedure is used, and a related design target of 60 percent computation reduction through distributed OPF at scale; both are labelled as design targets and neither is claimed as a Stage-1 measurement. The physical scaling facts are, however, measured: the uncontrolled IEEE 118 power flow takes approximately 15 ms per step in the Stage-1 environment, so candidate evaluation on the larger system remains tractable offline even before any transfer benefit is realised.

The practical workflow is straightforward. Pre-train the encoder on IEEE 39 trajectories harvested from the January 2019 benchmark; freeze the convolutional and LSTM layers; instantiate heads for the IEEE 118 observation dimensions; and fine-tune with the anchored objective of Equation (5.16) on a reduced target dataset. Because the action box structure is identical across systems (per-device susceptances, compensation degrees, and station powers), the heads inherit the action semantics of the source system, which is the property that makes the transfer well-posed in the first place.

## 5.7 HIL-Validated Placement Results

This section reports the placement actually implemented in the CAPSM environment and validated through the HIL campaign, and it explains the reasoning behind each siting decision. On the IEEE 39 system the installed device set comprises an SVC at bus 14 with a susceptance range of plus or minus 0.5 p.u. (approximately plus or minus 50 MVAr on the 100 MVA base), a STATCOM at bus 39 with a range of plus or minus 1.0 p.u. (approximately plus or minus 100 MVAr), a TCSC on the line between buses 16 and 17 with X_eff = (1 - k) X and k in [0, 0.7] with an operational setpoint band of [0.3, 0.7], and a UPFC at bus 26 combining a shunt susceptance of plus or minus 0.5 p.u. with series compensation on the branch 26 to 28, described in the thesis action space as an injected series power in [−0.5, 0.5] p.u. Three aggregated EV stations at buses 3, 8, and 15 complete the flexible set, each rated at 50 MW and 100 MWh, corresponding to an aggregated fleet of roughly 1,250 vehicles at the reference per-vehicle ratings discussed in Chapter 8, with round-trip efficiency 0.92 and SoC window [0.2, 0.9]. Figure 5.2 maps the placement onto the test system.

![Figure 5.2: FACTS device and EV station placement on the IEEE 39-bus system.](figures/fig_placement_map.png)

The siting rationale follows the electrical geography of the IEEE 39 system. The SVC at bus 14 and the STATCOM at bus 39 are placed at generator-dominated buses: bus 14 sits in the northern corridor where one of the two wind injection buses interacts with nearby machine regulation, and bus 39 anchors the external equivalent with the slack machine, so shunt support is installed where the reactive headroom of the system concentrates. The TCSC is placed on the western corridor line 16 to 17, which connects the generation at bus 35 to the central network and emerges from the Stage-1 contingency analysis as the N-1 critical line: tripping it at the near-peak operating point of 15 January 2019, 12:00 UTC raises losses from 51.1 to 58.7 MW, an increase of 14.9 percent, while the minimum voltage holds at 0.9505 p.u. and all 13 contingency-window steps converge (measured in the Stage-1 rebuild). Series compensation on that line directly relieves the corridor that the contingency stresses. The UPFC at bus 26 addresses the south-east region, where the violation buses 22 to 29 cluster in the Stage-1 benchmarks, and its combination of shunt and series authority gives two degrees of freedom for steering flows into that cluster. The EV stations sit at buses 3, 8, and 15, which are load and solar-injection buses, so V2G dispatch can act locally where the renewable variability appears.

The placement was determined offline in Python with PYPOWER and then compiled unchanged into the OPAL-RT Simulink model, giving the offline-to-HIL consistency check that the HIL campaign confirms: the same device set, buses, and rating boxes drive both environments, and the campaign established that the timing budget is met on the four-core allocation (achieved in the HIL campaign). The detailed protocol and timing measurements are deferred to Chapters 10 and 11, but the placement-level conclusion belongs here: no modelling discrepancy emerged that required resiting a device between the offline and real-time environments.

Because the baselines act on these devices through local droop control, the admissible gain range deserves a small-signal justification. We represent a placement bus by a first-order equivalent in which the aggregate conductance provides the damping of the local voltage response and the droop loop closes through the conductive coupling of the equivalent, and we state the resulting condition as Proposition 5.1.

**Proposition 5.1 (Local droop small-signal stability).** Consider a placement bus represented by the first-order equivalent in Equation (5.17), where v is the bus voltage magnitude in per unit, g_Sigma is the sum of the local conductances (load conductances plus the driving-point conductance of the aggregated network), q_0 collects voltage-independent injections, and the droop-controlled shunt obeys the law b = Kp (v_ref - v) with injected reactive power q_sh = b v squared. Aggregating the regulator response of the local machines into the equivalent, the deviation dynamics about an operating point v0 near v_ref take the form of Equation (5.18), and the equilibrium is locally exponentially stable if and only if Kp is smaller than g_Sigma.

```eq
%%5.17
\tau\, \frac{dv}{dt} = -g_{\Sigma}\, v + K_p (v_{\mathrm{ref}} - v)\, v_0^2 + q_0
```

```eq
%%5.18
\tau\, \frac{d\delta v}{dt} = -v_0^2 \left( g_{\Sigma} - K_p \right) \delta v
```

*Proof sketch.* Linearising Equation (5.17) about the operating point and substituting the droop law yields the single first-order deviation dynamics of Equation (5.18), whose only eigenvalue is lambda = -(v0 squared / tau)(g_Sigma - Kp). By the Lyapunov criterion for linear systems the equilibrium is exponentially stable exactly when this eigenvalue is negative, which is equivalent to Kp < g_Sigma; the Lyapunov function V = delta v squared over two decreases monotonically under the same condition, completing the argument. ∎

The baselines of Chapter 3 use Kp = 5 per-unit susceptance per per-unit voltage error with V_ref = 1.0 p.u. Under the normalisation of the reduced equivalent the condition of Proposition 5.1 is satisfied with margin for this gain, which is consistent with the stable local behaviour observed throughout the Stage-1 benchmarks; the proposition also supplies the bound that constrains any PSO-based gain refinement in Section 5.3, since pushing the droop gain beyond the local conductance would invert the damping of the local loop. We present the result as a reduced-order sufficient condition, in the spirit of the reduced droop-stability analyses of low-inertia systems, rather than as a full network-wide stability theorem [36], [123].

The honest limitation of this placement must now be stated plainly, because it is one of the substantive findings of the Stage-1 rebuild. At generator buses (type 2 and 3 in the solver), the machine voltage regulation overrides shunt injection: the SVC at bus 14 and the STATCOM at bus 39 change reactive power flows but cannot move the bus voltages that the generators hold. Meanwhile the violations concentrate at buses 22 to 29, which are electrically distant from all four devices, and the effect of any single device attenuates across the network before reaching that cluster. The consequence is measured, not conjectured: against the NoControl benchmark of 2,847 violation bus-hours, the local baselines reduce violations by at most 0.18 percent, and even the full coordinated stack achieves only the order of one percent, with System 2 reaching 2,810 bus-hours (minus 37, or minus 1.30 percent), CAPSM 2,813 (minus 34, or minus 1.20 percent), and System 1 2,822 (minus 25, or minus 0.88 percent), all measured in the Stage-1 rebuild; mean losses move only marginally (46.19 and 46.20 MW under System 2 and CAPSM against 46.11 MW uncontrolled). The framework specification targets a 32.7 percent voltage-deviation reduction and a 24.3 percent loss reduction under a coordinated placement study, and these are explicitly design targets that the present placement alone cannot deliver. This gap between the design target and the measured performance of the fixed placement is precisely what motivates the placement re-optimisation taken up in Chapter 12, informed by the coordination behaviour that Chapters 9 and 11 document.

## 5.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter formulated FACTS and EV station placement as a MINLP that couples integer siting, discrete type selection, and continuous ratings through the AC power flow, with an objective trading voltage deviation, normalised losses, violation penalties, and installation cost under voltage, thermal, rating, and SoC constraints. The combinatorial product of device slots and continuous ratings rules out enumeration on the IEEE 39 system, so the problem was decomposed into the four layers of Table 5.1: GA placement with roulette selection, elitism, single-point crossover, and Gaussian mutation; PSO tuning governed by the inertia-weight mechanism; TD3 as the reference continuous-dispatch design with twin critics, delayed updates, and target smoothing; and the deployed QIRL coordination layer that evaluates 32 candidates per step through amplitude superposition, Born-rule selection, and 10 percent tunnelling without any training data. The comparison of Table 5.3 made the deployment trade-off explicit, and the transfer-learning layer with the anchored loss of Equation (5.16) prepares the framework for the IEEE 118 system, with a 73 percent data-requirement reduction set as a design target.

The implemented placement places an SVC at bus 14, a STATCOM at bus 39, a TCSC on the N-1 critical western corridor line 16 to 17, and a UPFC at bus 26 facing the violation cluster at buses 22 to 29, with aggregated EV stations at buses 3, 8, and 15. Proposition 5.1 bounded the admissible local droop gain by the aggregate local conductance, covering the Kp = 5 baseline configuration. The Stage-1 measurements show that this placement, with any local control, removes at most the order of one percent of the 2,847 violation bus-hours, because generator regulation overrides shunt injection at the placement buses and the violation cluster is electrically distant from every device. That finding is carried forward as the motivation for the placement re-optimisation of Chapter 12 and as the honest baseline against which the HIL campaign of Chapters 10 and 11 must be read.
